Macrozamia parcifolia is a small cycad with no visible trunk from south-eastern Queensland, in eastern Australia, confined to the Biggenden district, between Childers and Tiaro, where it grows in scattered stands in tall open eucalypt forest on clay soils of basaltic origin. This species of the genus Macrozamia, described in 1994, has the narrowest and thinnest leaflets of its group, which give its leaves an untidy, wispy look. It belongs to section Parazamia and is listed as Endangered by the IUCN.

Key facts about Macrozamia parcifolia

  • Accepted name: Macrozamia parcifolia P.I.Forst. & D.L.Jones (1994), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and the Australian Plant Census
  • Former names and synonyms: no synonym; before its description, the informal name Macrozamia sp. (Biggenden P.I.Forster 12301B)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales; section Parazamia, Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi complex
  • Common names: no English or Aboriginal name specific to the species is recorded
  • Natural range: south-eastern Queensland, Australia: Biggenden district, between Childers and Tiaro; 120 to 220 m according to WildNet
  • IUCN status: Endangered (EN B1ab(iii,v)+2ab(iii,v)), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated 9 September 2020 and published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union; Vulnerable under the federal EPBC Act and under Queensland law
  • Documented hardiness: no cultivation data; rare, light frosts in its region, with −1.7 °C (28.9 °F) the lowest temperature at Gayndah Airport since 2003 (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, stony ridges and slopes, hard red-brown clay loams of basaltic origin, under tall open forest
  • Habit and adult size: underground stem 10 to 20 cm across, unbranched; 1 to 4 leaves 65 to 95 cm long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Royal Botanic Garden Sydney (plants of wild origin received in 2022); declared to BGCI by Lotusland in California and by the conservatory of the University of Minnesota; no British garden
  • Toxicity: not documented for the species; seeds and leaves toxic throughout the genus (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Macrozamia parcifolia

Macrozamia parcifolia is recognised by its few spirally twisted leaves, set with very many thread-like, limp leaflets that droop towards their tips and give the plant what its authors call an “untidy and wispy appearance”. The original description (Jones and Forster, 1994) gives the following characters.

  • Stem: underground, more or less ovoid, 10 to 20 cm across, unbranched.
  • Leaves: very few, in a very sparse crown, 65 to 95 cm long, dark green, erect; young leaves light green and hairless; petiole 15 to 30 cm long; axis spirally twisted two to six times, dark green with greenish-white marks between the bases of the leaflets.
  • Leaflets: 100 to 220 per leaf, very narrowly linear, 15 to 40 cm long and only 1 to 3 mm wide, dark green and dull above, bright green beneath, strongly channelled, thin-textured, themselves twisted one to three times, set at about 30 degrees to the axis, limp and drooping in their upper half or third; callous base greenish-white and inconspicuous.
  • Male cones: more or less cylindrical, 7 to 14 cm long and 2.5 to 4 cm across, usually curved with age; the upper scales carry a stiff spine up to 1.5 cm long.
  • Female cones: ovoid to ovoid-cylindrical, 8 to 14 cm long and 4 to 6 cm across, erect, on a smooth stalk 13 to 18 cm long; the longest spines, near the top, about 2.8 cm.
  • Seeds: ovoid, 1.7 to 2.5 cm long and 1.5 to 2 cm wide, with orange to red flesh.

The online Flora of Australia counts 1 to 4 leaves per crown, but describes the leaves as “mid-green, highly glossy”, which contradicts the original description (leaflets dark green and dull above); the original description is followed here.

Hybrids

No hybrid involving Macrozamia parcifolia has been documented, in the wild or in cultivation.

Possible confusion

Its closest relative is Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi, a species of the coastal lowlands of the same region. According to Jones and Forster (1994), Macrozamia parcifolia “can be immediately distinguished” by its narrower, thinner-textured, darker green leaflets, set at a steeper angle on the axis, and by their callous base, greenish-white and inconspicuous, whereas it is cream to white and prominent in Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi. The two species do not share a habitat: Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi lives in the coastal “wallum”, heath and woodland on deep siliceous sands, Macrozamia parcifolia on the clay soils of inland ridges.

Macrozamia crassifolia, of the same group, is told apart by its much thicker leaflets; it grows further west, near Mundubbera and Eidsvold.

Taxonomy

Macrozamia parcifolia P.I.Forst. & D.L.Jones is accepted by The World List of Cycads and the Australian Plant Census. Paul I. Forster and David L. Jones described it in 1994 in the journal Austrobaileya (vol. 4, no. 2, pp. 279–281, fig. 6). The type, a male plant, was collected near Biggenden on 5 January 1992 by P.I. Forster and P. Machin (no. 9318B); the holotype is held in Brisbane (BRI), with an isotype in Canberra (CBG).

The species belongs to section Parazamia: the very title of the paper that describes it announces “seven new species of Macrozamia section Parazamia”, and its authors place it in the Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi complex, with very narrow, deeply channelled leaflets, alongside Macrozamia crassifolia and Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi. Section Parazamia brings together small species with an underground stem, usually twisted leaves, no spiny leaflets and no mucilage canals in the leaflets. The species has no synonym.

Etymology and history

The epithet comes from the Latin parcus, “frugal, thrifty, scanty”, and folium, “leaf”, “an allusion to the slender, wispy leaves” (Jones and Forster, 1994; Haynes, 2022). The genus name Macrozamia combines the Greek makros, “large”, with the genus name Zamia.

Before its description, the plant was already recorded in Queensland under an informal name, Macrozamia sp. (Biggenden P.I.Forster 12301B), and coded as rare (R) by Forster (1994).

Common names

No common name specific to Macrozamia parcifolia is recorded in English, and no Aboriginal name is attested for it. Gardeners and botanists use the scientific name.

In the wild

When it was described, Macrozamia parcifolia was known from three localities near Biggenden, in the Wide Bay district; WildNet now places its range between Childers, Biggenden and Tiaro. It grows on stony ridges and slopes, on hard, well-drained, red-brown clay loams of basaltic origin, in tall open forest dominated by Eucalyptus citriodora and Eucalyptus fibrosa, with a shrubby understorey of Jacksonia scoparia and Psychotria daphnoides (Jones and Forster, 1994; WildNet). WildNet gives an altitude of 120 to 220 m, the original description 180 to 220 m.

The cones form from October to January and the seeds ripen from February to April; cones may not be produced every year when conditions are unfavourable. As in other Macrozamia with an underground stem, adult plants resprout after a fire has burnt their leaves, and a fire is often followed by synchronised coning; seedlings and seeds left on the surface, on the other hand, are usually killed (Flora of Australia, after the Queensland Herbarium, 2007).

No pollinator specific to the species has been documented. The Flora of Australia and WildNet profiles of Macrozamia parcifolia contain a sentence on the pollination of “Macrozamia conferta” by a Tranes weevil: this is evidently a copying error, and the information cannot be applied to Macrozamia parcifolia.

How to tell an adult plant

In a cycad without a trunk, it is hard to say whether a plant is old enough to cone. In 2015, A.C. Borsboom, J. Wang and P.I. Forster studied tagged plants of Macrozamia parcifolia on four plots of eucalypt-dominated open forest in south-eastern Queensland. The width of the petiole base of the longest mature leaf, combined with several years of coning records, proved the best way to pick out mature plants, and the method still worked with a single year of coning data. The population structure was bimodal; among immature plants, a broadly “reverse-J” distribution showed that mortality was highest among the youngest. The authors propose this simple method for other threatened trunkless cycads.

Conservation status and regulation

Macrozamia parcifolia is listed as Endangered (EN) on the IUCN Red List under criteria B1ab(iii,v)+2ab(iii,v), which point to a very restricted range and a continuing decline in the quality of its habitat and in the number of mature plants. The assessment, by J.D. Bösenberg, is dated 9 September 2020 and was published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, GBIF and Crossref, checked on 26 October 2026).

In Australia, it is listed as Vulnerable under the federal Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act 1999 (EPBC), effective 16 July 2000, and under the Queensland Nature Conservation (Plants) Regulation 2020 (Flora of Australia; WildNet, checked on 26 October 2026): the national listing is therefore less severe than the IUCN one. In 1994, one population lay mainly within a national park and the other two in state forests.

Like other Macrozamia, it has been listed in CITES Appendix II since 4 February 1977, with annotation #4: seeds and pollen are exempt, but plants are not. In the European Union it falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026 (Species+, checked on 26 October 2026). Check the regulations in force before any purchase or import, and buy only plants whose cultivated origin is proven.

Growing Macrozamia parcifolia

No cultivation experience with Macrozamia parcifolia has been published. Its habitat gives a few pointers: a hard but well-drained clay loam on ridges, under open forest, in a subtropical region with summer rain and rare frosts. These are assumptions drawn from the wild, not tested advice.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Light: in the wild, tall open forest; a bright position with some shade is a deduction from the habitat.
  • Soil: in the wild, stony, well-drained clay loam over basalt; a free-draining but fairly heavy mix is the logical choice.
  • Water: no published data; at Gayndah Airport, the nearest inland station, about 620 mm of rain falls a year, mostly in summer.
  • Space: a small plant whose few leaves reach 95 cm.

Container cultivation

No container culture has been described. A deep pot suits the underground stem; in a pot the plant loses the protection that the soil gives the stem in winter (see Cold hardiness).

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney holds an accession of wild origin received in 2022 (S2022-0081), according to its Garden Explorer; it is the only Australian garden that declares the species to BGCI.
  • United States: Ganna Walska Lotusland (Santa Barbara, California) and the Conservatory and Botanical Collection of the University of Minnesota (St Paul) declare it to BGCI. A BGCI declaration says that a garden has held the plant, not that a living specimen is there today. Montgomery Botanical Center lists no accession under this name in its online catalogue.
  • United Kingdom: no British garden declares it to BGCI (checked on 26 October 2026).

No grower in the English-speaking world has published a cultivation result.

Propagation

Macrozamia parcifolia is propagated by seed. A male and a female plant are needed, and outside Australia the female cone must be hand-pollinated, since the pollinator of the species is unknown. The seeds of this threatened species must come from a legal, documented source. Wear gloves to clean the seeds, whose flesh is toxic.

Pests and diseases

No pest or disease specific to Macrozamia parcifolia is documented. In the wild, fire mainly kills seedlings. For Macrozamia grown in Europe, growers' forums mainly mention scale insects and rot in cold, wet soil (see the genus page).

Cold hardiness

The cold hardiness of Macrozamia parcifolia has not been tested in cultivation: no result has been published.

Mediterranean and European experience

No result has been published in Europe, in the ground or in a pot. In March 2014, on the French Fous de palmiers forum, a grower reported the opinion of an Australian specialist who placed Macrozamia parcifolia among the “least hardy” species, those of inland Queensland, the third of four groups. No temperature was given.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

The Bureau of Meteorology lists no temperature station at Biggenden. At Gayndah Airport (111 m), some 50 to 75 km west of the known localities, the lowest temperature recorded since 2003 is −1.7 °C (28.9 °F), in July and again in August 2018, and frost occurs on about one day a year; at Maryborough, on the coast, the absolute minimum since 1957 is −1.4 °C (29.5 °F) (Bureau of Meteorology). The plants therefore know only rare, light frosts. In Britain and in the colder parts of the United States, it should be treated as a plant for a frost-free glasshouse or conservatory.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data specific to Macrozamia parcifolia have been published, but the risk is well documented for the genus. Macrozamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a poison of the liver; cattle that eat the leaves can develop a locomotor disorder of the hind legs (Healy, 1967), which McKenzie (2006) describes as irreversible, ending in paralysis of the hindquarters, with no treatment.

Treat the plant as toxic: wear gloves to handle the seeds and keep them out of reach of children and animals. In case of ingestion, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use specific to Macrozamia parcifolia is recorded. Other Macrozamia provided Aboriginal peoples with seeds that could be eaten after long processing (see the genus page); never eat cycad seeds.

Frequently asked questions

Which section of the genus does Macrozamia parcifolia belong to?

Section Parazamia. The 1994 paper that described it deals with seven new species of this section, and its authors place it in the Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi complex. Placing it in section Macrozamia would be a mistake.

How does it differ from Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi?

By its narrower leaflets (1 to 3 mm), thinner and darker, set at a steeper angle, with an inconspicuous base. It also grows on inland basalt clays rather than on coastal sands.

What does parcifolia mean?

“With scanty leaves”. The name, formed from the Latin parcus and folium, refers to its slender, wispy leaves.

How hardy is it?

Nobody knows. No trial has been published; its region has only rare, light frosts (−1.7 °C, or 28.9 °F, at Gayndah Airport since 2003), and an Australian specialist ranks it among the less hardy species.

Is it endangered?

Yes. The IUCN lists it as Endangered (2022) and Australian law as Vulnerable. Its range is limited to the Biggenden district.

Is it poisonous?

Probably, like all cycads. No study deals with this species, but the seeds and leaves of Macrozamia are toxic to people and livestock.
