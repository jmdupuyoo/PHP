Microcycas calocoma is a tree-like cycad endemic to western Cuba, where it grows only in the hills and mountains of Pinar del Río province, on steep limestone hills (mogotes) as well as on slopes and ravines of schist and clay. Cubans call it “palma corcho”. It is the only species of the genus Microcycas: a palm-like tree whose trunk often reaches several metres, crowned with glossy leaves that look cut off square at the tip. It was described in 1852 as Zamia calocoma, a name still found in old catalogues. Listed as Critically Endangered by IUCN and in CITES Appendix I, it is one of the most threatened cycads in the world.

Key facts about Microcycas calocoma

  • Accepted name: Microcycas calocoma (Miq.) A.DC. (1868), published in the Prodromus systematis naturalis regni vegetabilis 16(2): 538; accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15), the WCVP (Kew) and IPNI
  • Former names and synonyms: Zamia calocoma Miq. (1852), the basionym; “Dendrozamia calocoma”, an invalid name cited by Sauvalle in 1873
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales; the only species of Microcycas
  • Common names: “palma corcho”, “palma de corcho” or “corcho” in Cuba (Spanish); Species+ gives “Palm Corcho” as the English name
  • Natural range: endemic to western Cuba, Pinar del Río province (The World List of Cycads; Flora de la República de Cuba)
  • IUCN status: Critically Endangered (CR, criterion C2a(i)), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I since 1 July 1975, without annotation (seeds included); Annex A in the European Union; National Natural Monument in Cuba since 1989
  • Documented hardiness: no measured minimum temperature published; a tropical plant (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, from shaded understorey to open sites, on limestone with almost no soil, stony ground, clay and schist; no cultivation recipe specific to the species has been published
  • Habit and adult size: upright trunk, sometimes branched, 25 cm to 10 m tall and 1 to 6 dm across according to Caldwell and Baker (1907), 10 to 20 m (33 to 66 ft) and more according to the Flora of Cuba; 10 to 50 leaves 60 cm to 1 m long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Fairchild and Montgomery in Florida hold the main collections outside Cuba; declared by Kew, Bristol and the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney (see In cultivation)
  • Toxicity: roots once used as rat poison in Cuba; toxins known in all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Microcycas calocoma

As an adult it is the only Cuban cycad with a true trunk above ground, topped by a full crown of leaves that look truncated: the leaflets in the middle and at the tip of the leaf are almost the same length, so that the leaf seems cut off square (The World List of Cycads). Its cones, over 20 cm long, are also much larger than those of the Cuban Zamia. The description below follows the Flora de la República de Cuba (González Géigel, 2003) and the observations made in Cuba by Caldwell and Baker (1907) and Caldwell (1907):

  • Trunk: cylindrical and upright, rarely branched according to the Flora of Cuba (sometimes with two or three branches), “often branched” according to Caldwell and Baker; smooth and greyish at the base, the upper part armoured with the remains of leaves and cataphylls. The pith is pinkish, rich in starch and very brittle (Caldwell and Baker, 1907). The published sizes differ: 25 cm to 10 m tall and 1 to 6 dm across according to Caldwell and Baker, Caldwell having measured a trunk of 9.5 m in Cuba and an old plant 2 m in girth; (0.25–)10 to 20 m, “or more”, and 10 to 90 cm across according to the Flora of Cuba. The figure of 11 m for the oldest plants, often quoted, comes from the Spanish Wikipedia and from a Czech grower's website.
  • Young plants: they start with a buried stem, like a Zamia; Caldwell (1907) describes a plant 9.5 cm across buried to within 3.75 cm of its top. As the trunk lengthens the plant looks very like a Cycas revoluta.
  • Leaves: 10 to 50 (6 to 40 according to Caldwell and Baker), more or less upright, produced together in a crown, 60 cm to 1 m long, covered at first with a pale brown to whitish mealy down that soon falls. The petiole, about 10 cm long, has no spines; the leaf axis stays on the plant for four to five years after the leaflets have fallen.
  • Leaflets: 50 to 80 on each side, opposite to nearly opposite, jointed to the axis by a narrowed base, leathery, entire, linear-lanceolate and pointed, 8 to 12 cm long (up to 20 cm), reflexed, glossy above, with parallel veins that fork and no midrib. They live one to three years.
  • Cataphylls: triangular scales 4 to 6 cm long and 1.5 to 2 cm wide, often with a twisted tip, covered with long grey hairs.
  • Male cones: one or two, cylindrical to cylindric-conical, (20–)25 to 30 cm long (up to 54 cm) and (5–)8 to 12 cm across, on a stalk 2 to 8 cm long, with about 500 fertile scales in some twenty rows. After shedding pollen they hang from the top of the plant (Caldwell, 1907).
  • Female cones: usually one, cylindric-conical, 50 to 70 cm long and 13 to 16 cm across, on a short woolly stalk; each scale carries two ovules. Caldwell (1907) weighed a cone 94 cm long and 9.5 kg (21 lb), the largest cycad cone recorded at the time.
  • Seeds: ellipsoid, (2.5–)3.2 to 3.5 cm long and 1.2 to 1.8 cm wide; the fleshy coat (sarcotesta), 4 mm thick, is pale pink then yellowish brown when ripe according to the Flora of Cuba; Caldwell describes salmon-pink ovules, and The World List of Cycads gives a red sarcotesta for the genus.
  • Chromosomes: 2n = 26 (Flora of Cuba, after Sax and Beal 1934 and Moretti 1990).

Hybrids

No hybrid is known. Microcycas calocoma is the only species of its genus, and no published source reports a cross with another cycad, in the wild or in cultivation.

Possible confusion

An adult cannot be confused with any other Cuban plant. The key of the Flora de la República de Cuba separates the two cycad genera of the island on three characters: Microcycas has a trunk above ground, many leaves and cones over 20 cm long; the Zamia have an underground stem, few leaves and cones about 15 cm long. Two of these Zamia also grow in Pinar del Río province, Zamia pygmaea and Zamia erosa.

Confusion has nevertheless happened, in herbaria. In 1905 Otis W. Caldwell brought back from Cuba cones and leaves of a small cycad said to be Microcycas calocoma: it was a Zamia, which Caldwell and Baker (1907) identified as Zamia pumila, in the broad sense that name then had. They also note that specimens distributed by the New York Botanical Garden as Microcycas calocoma, collected at Madruga and in the Matanzas area, probably belonged to the same Zamia. A young plant with its stem still underground can still pass for a Zamia today.

Older, before the trunk lengthens, the plant recalls a Cycas revoluta, the resemblance that gave the genus its name (see Etymology and history). Its jointed leaflets, which come away cleanly from the axis, and the lack of a midrib set it apart from the Cycas, whose leaflets each have a clear central vein.

Taxonomy

Microcycas calocoma (Miq.) A.DC. is an accepted name in The World List of Cycads, the World Checklist of Vascular Plants (WCVP, Kew, the database behind POWO) and IPNI. The species is placed in the family Zamiaceae.

Friedrich Anton Wilhelm Miquel described it as Zamia calocoma in volume 7 of the Belgian journal Flore des serres et des jardins de l'Europe (pp. 141–142), dated 1852 by IPNI and The World List of Cycads, 1851 by the Flora of Cuba. In the protologue Miquel already placed it in a “new section” of Zamia, section Microcycas. In 1868 Alphonse de Candolle raised this section to the rank of genus in the Prodromus and made the combination Microcycas calocoma (Miq.) A.DC., of which Zamia calocoma is the basionym. According to Caldwell and Baker (1907), who quote his text, de Candolle based the genus on Charles Wright's Cuban collections and placed it between Dioon and Zamia. The form “Microcycas A.DC.”, still used by the Flora of Cuba, is also met with.

The reference specimen for the name (neotype), designated by Stevenson and Sabato in 1986, is a collection by Charles Wright made in Cuba between 1861 and 1864 (no. 3193), kept in the de Candolle herbarium in Geneva (Flora of Cuba). The same flora records an invalid name, “Dendrozamia calocoma”, attributed to de Candolle by Sauvalle in 1873.

Some authors have placed Microcycas in Zamia; the Flora of Cuba considers that Norstog's work (1990) confirms its independence. In D.W. Stevenson's classification (1992) the genus forms on its own the subtribe Microcycadinae, in the tribe Zamieae, next to Zamia. Molecular studies agree: the complete chloroplast genome places Microcycas as the sister group of Zamia, in line with earlier work on nuclear genes (Salas-Leiva et al., 2013, cited by Liu et al., 2019), and in the same clade as Ceratozamia and the African Stangeria eriopus (Chang et al., 2020).

Etymology and history

Microcycas means “a little Cycas” (The World List of Cycads), and the protologue explains the choice: Miquel writes that the plant “perfectly recalls the habit of a small Cycas revoluta”. The epithet calocoma combines the Greek calo-, “beautiful”, and come, “head of hair”, for the handsome crown of leaves (Haynes, 2022, quoted by The World List of Cycads).

The protologue is titled “Sur une espèce nouvelle de Zamia des Indes occidentales, introduite dans l'établissement Van Houtte à Gand”. Miquel, then director of the botanic garden in Amsterdam, describes a young plant grown there: a trunk of 10 inches (about 25 cm), single or forked, eleven leaves, 60 to 70 leaflets on each side. He calls it “a very distinct species, one of the most beautiful in the genus”, close to Zamia tenuis but different in the shape and number of its leaflets, and notes that its fruiting “is not yet known”. He gives its origin as “the Isle of Pines, in the Antilles”, whereas all the populations known since are in the west of the main island.

From Caldwell and Baker's review of the early literature (1907), Regel described in 1857 a plant brought from Cuba, one to two feet tall, and already thought it might form a new genus; Grisebach cited Wright's collection in 1866; Bentham and Hooker gave a mistaken species name, “cubensis”, in 1880. The plant remained poorly known: after the mix-up of 1905, Caldwell returned to Cuba in January and February 1907 and at last found several groups of true Microcycas in the western sierras, with their cones. The two articles he published that year in the Botanical Gazette were the first to describe the tree-like habit, the cones and the habitat.

Later work includes Chrysler (1926) on anatomy, Foster and Rodríguez San Pedro (1942), who already judged the species very rare and its reproduction limited, Mercado (1977) on the coralloid roots and Norstog (1990) on the motile sperm cells. In 1989 the Cuban government declared it a National Natural Monument, for its strict endemism, its rarity, its scientific interest and its beauty (Ferrer et al., 2013; Martínez Serrano et al., 2023).

Common names

The attested vernacular name is Cuban: “palma corcho”. Caldwell (1907) recorded it among the mountain people of Pinar del Río, and the title of an article by Cendrero (1940) gives three forms: “palma corcho, palma de corcho o corcho”. The origin of the name is debated. For Caldwell it comes from the appearance of the seeds packed in the ripe cone, which recalls cork; the Spanish Wikipedia, citing a dictionary, links the Cuban word corcho to a hollow trunk holding a beehive. Species+ gives “Palm Corcho” as the English name. Whatever the name, the plant is not a palm.

In the wild

Microcycas calocoma lives only in western Cuba, in Pinar del Río province. Caldwell (1907) found it in the western sierras, in a few scattered stations where the plants grew singly or in small groups. According to the Flora of Cuba, its range once extended to the city of Pinar del Río, where a few isolated plants survive in courtyards.

The habitats are varied. Caldwell saw it on limestone with almost no soil, on stony ground and in clay, at all exposures, in the open as well as in deeply shaded woodland. The Flora of Cuba places it in the vegetation of the mogotes, the steep limestone hills of the region, on flat ground or on slopes, preferably in moist, shaded places. The synthesis of earlier work by the rangers of Viñales National Park, in the municipality of Viñales, distinguishes two main habitats (Martínez Serrano et al., 2023): schist soils on slopes and in ravine bottoms, under pine and oak forest, secondary semi-deciduous forest or pasture; and shallow rendzinas on karstic limestone bristling with sharp rock (the “diente de perro”), under more or less dense semi-deciduous forest.

Numbers are poorly known and published figures are hard to compare. According to the Spanish Wikipedia, which summarises the work of the National Botanic Garden of Cuba, Peña-García et al. (1998) counted 23 localities and 800 to 1,000 plants, in colonies of a few individuals to 150, up to 665 m (about 2,180 ft) in the Sierra del Rosario. Recent work shows that new stations are still being found:

  • Viñales National Park: eight years of monitoring in five localities provided the basis of a management plan for the species (Ferrer et al., 2013, abstract). According to Martínez Serrano et al. (2023), this monitoring revealed previously unknown localities.
  • One population of the park, counted in 2021–2022: rangers found 88 individuals, mostly on the summit and rocky slopes: 11 seedlings, 42 juveniles and 35 adults by height; 13 females and 4 males identified, the sex of the other 71 remaining unknown for lack of cones; three plants bore cones, without any seed. Nine damaged plants (loss of the terminal bud, wounded trunk) had branched (Martínez Serrano et al., 2023).

Pollination and reproduction

Like most cycads, Microcycas calocoma is pollinated by insects rather than wind: it was thought to be wind-pollinated, but the field work of Vovides et al. (1997) points to insect pollination (Flora of Cuba). In 2005 Ramiro Chaves and Julio A. Genaro described a new small beetle, Pharaxonotha esperanzae (family Erotylidae), which feeds on the pollen and breeds in the male cones, and presented it as its probable pollinator. Martínez Serrano et al. (2023) report that its presence and natural pollination have been observed in Viñales National Park.

According to the Flora of Cuba, male cones are seen from May to December and female cones from October to April. Natural reproduction has long been weak: Foster and Rodríguez San Pedro noted it in 1942, and the IUCN action plan (Donaldson, 2003) states that seed production in the wild is minimal. The English Wikipedia claims, without a source, a very unbalanced sex ratio “in many populations”; the only published count of known sex, in the population counted in 2021–2022, concerns just 17 plants.

Conservation status and regulation

Microcycas calocoma is Critically Endangered. The World List of Cycads cites the IUCN Red List assessment published in 2022 by J.D. Bösenberg: category CR, criterion C2a(i), that is, fewer than 250 mature individuals, a continuing decline and no subpopulation of more than 50 mature plants; GBIF relays the same category (checked on 31 October 2026). In 2003 the IUCN action plan already classed it CR (criteria B1ab(iii,iv,v)+2ab(iii,iv,v)), with 300 to 500 plants, citing reproductive failure, habitat destruction and over-collection. In Cuba, Peña et al. (1998) classed it as critically endangered for its small range, its strong fragmentation and the inferred decline of its habitat and of the number of reproductive females (Martínez Serrano et al., 2023).

The documented threats are:

  • Habitat destruction and degradation (IUCN, 2003). In the population studied in 2021–2022, illegal felling of neighbouring trees (including Calycophyllum candidissimum and Zanthoxylum martinicense) injures the Microcycas, and fires are lit by poachers and wild-honey collectors (Martínez Serrano et al., 2023).
  • Reproductive failure: few or no seeds in many stations (IUCN, 2003), with the presumed loss of the pollinator from degraded sites.
  • Collection: over-collection is among the threats listed by IUCN in 2003, and Kay et al. (2011) name the over-collection of wild plants as one factor in the decline of natural populations.
  • Hurricanes: the brittle pith makes the trunk fragile; Caldwell (1907) thought the oldest plants had been broken by wind before branching. Hurricane Ian (category 4, September 2022) struck the population counted in 2021–2022 after the survey; its effects remained to be assessed (Martínez Serrano et al., 2023).

The species has been listed in CITES Appendix I since 1 July 1975, like the Encephalartos and Stangeria eriopus, before all cycads were listed in Appendix II in 1977 (IUCN, 2003; Species+). The listing carries no annotation: unlike cycads in Appendix II, its seeds are covered by CITES. International commercial trade is possible only for artificially propagated plants with the required documents. In the European Union the species is in Annex A of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, in the list set by Commission Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026 (Species+, checked on 31 October 2026). The United Kingdom, the United States and Australia apply CITES through their own legislation; check the rules in force with your national CITES authority before any purchase or shipment. Between 1983 and 1999, CITES parties reported exports of only 30 plants and 162 seeds of Microcycas (IUCN, 2003).

In Cuba the species has been a National Natural Monument since 1989. Part of its stations lie in Viñales National Park, where it is monitored as an “umbrella” and “flagship” species; its trunk also supports many epiphytes (Ferrer et al., 2013).

Growing Microcycas calocoma

Microcycas calocoma is a plant for botanic gardens and specialist collections: rare and reputed difficult, it needs a documented legal origin (Appendix I) and, outside the tropics, a heated glasshouse. No cultivation recipe specific to the species has been published; the points below come from its natural habitat and from the gardens that grow it.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Climate: tropical only. The species is grown at the National Botanic Garden of Cuba in Havana (Lazcano Lara, 2007) and in the great cycad collections of the Miami area (see In cultivation).
  • Light: in the wild it grows both in shaded woodland and in the open (Caldwell, 1907), with a preference for shade according to the Flora of Cuba. According to the Spanish Wikipedia, cultivated plants do better in full sun or light shade than in shade.
  • Soil: it grows on limestone as well as on clays and schist soils; no standard mix is documented. Drainage matters more than pH.
  • Watering: no rule specific to the species is documented. In the wild it prefers moist places according to the Flora of Cuba; as with all cycads, avoid standing water around the roots.
  • Growth: slow; a Czech grower states that the first cones appear at about 16 years, exceptionally at 8 (Microcycas.cz, grower's information).

Container cultivation

Outside the tropics, Microcycas calocoma is a plant for a heated glasshouse or warm conservatory, in a large, deep, free-draining pot that can stand outdoors in summer in warm climates. Even at the University of California Botanical Garden in Berkeley, where most cycads are grown outdoors, Microcycas needs a glasshouse (Plant Collections Network, 2015). In Europe it is seen under glass in a few botanic gardens, such as the Hortus Botanicus in Amsterdam, according to the Dutch botanic gardens network, and the botanic garden in Liberec, in the Czech Republic.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

Outside Cuba the species survives in cultivation mainly thanks to two Florida gardens. BGCI PlantSearch (queried on 31 October 2026) lists it in 35 gardens worldwide, 19 of them in the United States; a BGCI entry shows that a garden has declared the species, not that a living plant is there today.

  • United States: the largest collections outside Cuba are at Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden and Montgomery Botanical Center, two neighbouring gardens in the Miami area, both of which list the species in their online catalogues. Their staff have germinated Microcycas seed since the 1980s (Hubbuch, 1987, cited by Donaldson, 2003). Montgomery has developed a protocol for hand pollination and propagation, sends pollen to other gardens and sells seed at public auction with the Florida Nursery, Growers and Landscape Association; over fifteen years the average price per seed has fallen by more than half as supply grew, and the authors expect it to fall to a point where collecting in the wild no longer pays (Kay et al., 2011). At the Missouri Botanical Garden, a plant accessioned in 1987, probably from Montgomery seed, grows under glass (Deloso et al., 2025). The Huntington, Lotusland, the San Diego Zoo, Naples Botanical Garden, the Marie Selby Botanical Gardens, the Atlanta Botanical Garden and several gardens in Hawaii also declare it in BGCI.
  • United Kingdom: Kew and the University of Bristol Botanic Garden declare it in BGCI; a 2010 study of the Edinburgh collection found every cycad genus there except Microcycas (Clugston, 2010). No plant on public display has been verified on site.
  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney lists Microcycas calocoma in its Garden Explorer (updated 1 October 2026) and in BGCI; it is the only Australian garden to declare it.

Propagation

Microcycas calocoma is propagated from seed. Seed has been germinated successfully at Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden and Montgomery Botanical Center (Hubbuch, 1987, cited by IUCN, 2003), and Montgomery has published its method, from pollination to the establishment of young plants (Kay et al., 2011). The plant being dioecious and its pollinator absent from collections, pollination is done by hand between a male and a female plant; pollen can be sent from one garden to another. A Czech grower states that the seeds quickly lose their viability and must be sown soon after cleaning (Microcycas.cz).

The World List of Cycads notes that the genus occasionally produces basal offsets, and Caldwell (1907) saw new branches arise from dormant buds on old trunks, one of them rooted in the parent plant. Propagation from such offsets is not documented.

Pests and diseases

No pest or disease specific to the species in cultivation is documented. In the wild, in the population counted in 2021–2022, about 80 % of the plants were in good condition; the damage recorded was mainly sooty mould and the loss of leaves or of the terminal bud, and the trunks carried few epiphytes, mostly Tillandsia fasciculata and Tillandsia utriculata (Martínez Serrano et al., 2023). Seeds and seedlings are eaten by pigs and rodents, according to the Cuban work summarised by the Spanish Wikipedia.

Cold hardiness

No measured minimum temperature has been published for Microcycas calocoma. It is a tropical plant that should not be exposed to frost; the values circulating online (USDA zone 10b or 11) are not linked to any dated observation.

Mediterranean and European experience

No trial in the ground has been published in Europe, on the Mediterranean coast or elsewhere, and no cold episode is documented for the species. On the French forum Fous de palmiers it appears only in connection with trips to Cuba and foreign collections, without any cultivation report; the Jardin zoologique tropical in the south of France has published no observation of it. In Europe it can only be considered for a heated glasshouse or warm conservatory.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

In the United States the species is grown in the botanic gardens of the Miami area, and two articles in The Cycad Newsletter deal with its cultivation in Florida, in the south of the state (Calonje, 2007) and in central Florida, more exposed to frost (Broome, 2007). No dated growing report giving a place, a temperature and a result has been published. In Britain it is a glasshouse plant.

Toxicity and precautions

Yes, it should be treated as poisonous. Caldwell (1907) reports that, according to local lore, the roots of the palma corcho were used as rat poison. No toxicity study specific to the species is documented, but cycads of the family Zamiaceae contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, and these toxins cause serious poisoning in other cycads, especially through the seeds. Keep seeds and plant debris out of reach of children and animals, wear gloves to clean the seeds and, if any part has been eaten, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

The only documented traditional use is that of the roots as rat poison, reported by Caldwell in 1907. No food use is recorded, although the pith is rich in starch. According to the Spanish Wikipedia, the plant was also collected as a rare ornamental until the mid-20th century, with some exports. Its status as a National Natural Monument reflects its value as part of Cuba's natural heritage.

Frequently asked questions

Is Microcycas calocoma the only species in its genus?

Yes. It is the only species of the genus Microcycas, with no subspecies or varieties, according to The World List of Cycads.

Are Zamia calocoma and Microcycas calocoma the same plant?

Yes. Zamia calocoma is the name published by Miquel in 1852; de Candolle moved the species to the genus Microcycas in 1868. The correct name today is Microcycas calocoma.

Why is it called a “little Cycas” when it is so tall?

Because Miquel described it from a young plant grown in Amsterdam, with a trunk of about 25 cm, which reminded him of “a small Cycas revoluta”. Its tree-like habit was not described until 1907, from plants seen in Cuba.

How tall does it grow?

It depends on the source. Caldwell and Baker (1907) give up to 10 m and measured a trunk of 9.5 m; the Flora of Cuba gives 10 to 20 m and sometimes more. Growth is slow, and young plants keep a buried stem for a long time.

Why does it reproduce so poorly in the wild?

Because several obstacles add up: small, isolated colonies, a probable pollinator (Pharaxonotha esperanzae) presumed lost from degraded sites, and seeds and seedlings eaten by animals. In the population counted in 2021–2022, none of the three coning plants bore seed.

Can I buy a Microcycas calocoma legally?

Yes, but only plants or seeds from cultivation, with their documents. The species is in CITES Appendix I and in Annex A in the European Union, seeds included. Keep invoices and certificates, and beware of any offer without provenance.

Can it be grown outdoors in the UK?

No, nothing supports it. No outdoor trial is known in Europe and no minimum temperature has been published: it is a tropical plant, to be grown in a heated glasshouse or warm conservatory.

Is it poisonous?

Yes, it should be treated as such. Its roots were used as rat poison in Cuba, and all cycads contain toxins, especially in the seeds. If a child or an animal swallows any part, contact a poison centre or a vet.
