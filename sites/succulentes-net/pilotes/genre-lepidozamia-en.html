The genus Lepidozamia brings together two cycads from eastern Australia, which live in the moist forests of Queensland and New South Wales. These large, palm-like plants carry a trunk covered in scales and a crown of pinnate leaves two to three metres long. One of them, Lepidozamia hopei, is reputed to be the tallest living cycad, with trunks of more than 17 m. The other, Lepidozamia peroffskyana, the pineapple zamia or shining burrawang, is more often seen in cultivation. Both species were long placed in the neighbouring genus Macrozamia: the names Macrozamia peroffskyana, Macrozamia denisonii and Macrozamia hopei are still found in old books and nursery catalogues.

Key facts about Lepidozamia

  • Accepted name (POWO): Lepidozamia Regel (1857), published in the Bulletin de la Société impériale des naturalistes de Moscou
  • Number of species: 2, Lepidozamia hopei and Lepidozamia peroffskyana (POWO; The World List of Cycads)
  • Former names: Macrozamia peroffskyana, Macrozamia denisonii, Encephalartos denisonii, Lepidozamia denisonii (for Lepidozamia peroffskyana); Catakidozamia hopei W.Hill, Macrozamia hopei W.Hill (for Lepidozamia hopei)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, tribe Encephalarteae, subtribe Macrozamiinae (with Macrozamia)
  • Common names: Hope's cycad (Lepidozamia hopei); shining burrawang, pineapple zamia, pineapple cycad or scaly zamia (Lepidozamia peroffskyana)
  • Natural range: endemic to eastern Australia, from north-eastern Queensland to north-eastern New South Wales
  • IUCN status: Least Concern (LC) for both species, 2022 assessments cited by The World List of Cycads (see Regulation and conservation)
  • CITES: Appendix II for both species since 4 February 1977, with annotation #4 (seeds, pollen and in vitro cultures exempt); Annex B in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: about −5 °C (23 °F) quoted by a nursery for Lepidozamia peroffskyana. On a French growers' forum, an unprotected plant in the ground was defoliated by −8 to −9 °C (17.6 to 15.8 °F) in Montpellier in winter 2011–2012, then regrew; in Concarneau (Brittany) the foliage was scorched at −5 °C in February 2012, but the caudex stayed sound. In Texas, a seedling regrew after −9 °C (15 °F) in 2021. No measured value was found for Lepidozamia hopei. Amateur observations, indicative only (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: partial shade or dappled light; free-draining but moisture-retentive, humus-rich soil
  • Habit and size: trunk of 4 to 7 m (13 to 23 ft) in Lepidozamia peroffskyana, more than 17 m (56 ft) in the largest Lepidozamia hopei; leaves up to 3 m (10 ft)
  • Toxicity: all parts are poisonous, especially the seeds (see Toxicity and precautions)

Characteristics of the genus Lepidozamia

A Lepidozamia is recognised by its upright trunk, armoured with the bases of old leaves, by its large pinnate leaves without spines and by its leaflets attached to the top of the leaf axis. It is a large, palm-like cycad.

  • Trunk: cylindrical, sturdy, usually unbranched. In Lepidozamia peroffskyana it is most often 0.6 to 1.8 m tall but can reach 5 to 7 m, with a diameter of 35 to 80 cm depending on the source. In Lepidozamia hopei it can exceed 17 m (Encyclopædia Britannica), or even reach 20 m (about 66 ft) with a diameter of 50 cm (Gymnosperm Database), which probably makes it the tallest and most massive of all cycads.
  • Scales on the trunk: after the leaves fall, the base of each petiole stays attached to the trunk. These persistent bases form an armour of tight scales, hence the “scaly” look that gave the genus its name and Lepidozamia peroffskyana its English names “scaly zamia” and “pineapple zamia”. Between two flushes of leaves, the crown also produces cataphylls, small leaves reduced to scales.
  • Leaves: pinnate, up to 3 m long, with about 150 to 250 leaflets. The petiole and the leaf axis carry neither spines nor prickles, unlike many other cycads. Young leaves emerge brown, soft and hairy, then turn green, glossy and leathery.
  • Leaflets: stalkless, inserted along the midline of the upper side of the axis, with parallel veins and no midrib. Their base is neither swollen nor coloured. They are 17 to 30 mm wide in Lepidozamia hopei, against 7 to 14 mm in Lepidozamia peroffskyana: the simplest way to tell the two species apart.
  • Cones: the plants are dioecious, each one male or female. The cones are almost stalkless; male cones are cylindrical, female cones broadly ovoid. Those of Lepidozamia peroffskyana are among the largest of all cycads: up to 90 cm long and 45 kg according to the Encyclopædia Britannica, 50 to 75 cm according to Australian floras.
  • Seeds: large and fleshy, with a bright red outer coat (sarcotesta), about 5 cm by 2.5 cm in Lepidozamia peroffskyana.

Lepidozamia or Macrozamia?

The two genera are closely related and look alike in cultivation. In Macrozamia, a much more diverse genus (41 species, all Australian), the leaflets are attached to the sides of the axis and carry at their base a callus, often coloured white, yellow or red. In Lepidozamia they arise from the top of the axis and have no callus. Many Macrozamia also have a short or underground trunk, whereas mature Lepidozamia form a clearly visible trunk above ground.

Taxonomy and classification

Lepidozamia is a genus accepted by POWO, which places it in the family Zamiaceae. It has only two species, both accepted. With Macrozamia it forms the subtribe Macrozamiinae, within the tribe Encephalarteae, which also includes the African Encephalartos.

The history of the genus is a series of moves to and from Encephalartos and Macrozamia. Regel described it in 1857. The Dutch botanist Miquel then reduced it to a section, first in Encephalartos (1863), then in Macrozamia (1868), and created the combination Macrozamia peroffskyana. In 1959, L.A.S. Johnson's revision of the Australian Zamiaceae recognised Lepidozamia as one of the three genera of the family endemic to Australia, with Macrozamia and Bowenia; this is the treatment followed today. In practice, the Macrozamia names still met with for these two plants are synonyms.

Other names have circulated: Macrozamia denisonii C.Moore & F.Muell., also combined in Encephalartos and in Lepidozamia, is a synonym of Lepidozamia peroffskyana. Catakidozamia hopei W.Hill (1865) and Macrozamia hopei W.Hill (1886) are synonyms of Lepidozamia hopei; Macrozamia hopei C.Moore (1884), sometimes cited, is an invalid name according to The World List of Cycads.

The author of Lepidozamia hopei is not written the same way everywhere. POWO and IPNI, which this site follows, write Lepidozamia hopei (W.Hill) Regel and treat Catakidozamia hopei as the basionym, the name on which Regel's would be based. The World List of Cycads writes simply Lepidozamia hopei Regel: according to L.A.S. Johnson (1959), Regel was unaware of Walter Hill's publication and cited only a garden name, so his name is not based on Hill's. The species name does not change; only the author citation differs.

Etymology and history of the genus

The name Lepidozamia combines the Greek lepis, lepidos, “scale”, with the genus name Zamia, according to the Cycad Pages of the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney. The sources disagree on which scales are meant: the trunk covered in leaf bases for some, the scaly look of the cones for others. The Gymnosperm Database suggests another reading (Latin lepidus, “graceful”). Regel's protologue could not be consulted to settle the matter.

The genus has an unexpected European history: Eduard Regel described it in 1857 from a living plant grown at the botanic garden in St Petersburg, Lepidozamia peroffskyana, which is the type species. The epithet honours Count Perovsky (Peroffsky), a benefactor of that garden.

The second species was described in Australia by Walter Hill in 1865, as Catakidozamia hopei. Regel published it in Lepidozamia in 1876, in the journal Gartenflora, starting from a horticultural trade name. The epithet hopei honours Louis Hope (1817–1894), of Cleveland (Queensland), regarded in Australia as the father of the state's sugar industry and a patron of horticulture and botany.

Common names

In English, Lepidozamia hopei is called “Hope's cycad” and sometimes “zamia palm”. Lepidozamia peroffskyana is the “shining burrawang” in the Queensland government's official database, and is also known as “pineapple zamia”, “pineapple cycad” and “scaly zamia”. Beware: “burrawang” refers above all to Macrozamia communis, a cycad of New South Wales, so the name on its own is ambiguous.

Biogeography and ecology

The two Lepidozamia live on the eastern seaboard of Australia, in moist forest, but very far from each other: one in the tropics, the other in the subtropical zone.

  • Lepidozamia hopei, tropical Queensland: north-eastern Queensland, in lowland and mid-altitude tropical rainforest, from sea level to about 600 m, on well-structured loamy soils. It grows in the understorey, like a small tree beneath the canopy; it can be seen, for example, near Cairns (Mount Whitfield Conservation Park).
  • Lepidozamia peroffskyana, south-east Queensland and New South Wales: a coastal strip of about 700 km, from around Gympie, north of Brisbane, to around Taree, on the north coast of New South Wales. It usually grows under a eucalypt canopy: wet sclerophyll forest at the edge of rainforest on the slopes of the coastal ranges, impoverished littoral forest or scrub on stabilised dunes near the sea.

Pollination is carried out by insects, not by wind. In Lepidozamia peroffskyana, a field study (Hall et al., 2004) showed that weevils of the genus Tranes, specific to this plant, complete their life cycle in the male cones and visit the female cones in large numbers. Female cones from which insects were excluded were hardly pollinated at all, whereas those protected from wind alone were pollinated normally. In Lepidozamia hopei, the weevil Tranes insignipes develops in the male cones and carries pollen; it is regarded as its pollinator, without such a complete demonstration.

Like other cycads, Lepidozamia have coralloid roots, specialised roots that grow up towards the surface and house cyanobacteria able to fix nitrogen from the air (Chang et al., 2019). This trait is shared by the whole order; no study specific to the genus was found.

Regulation and conservation

Neither species is considered globally threatened, but international trade in them is regulated. Both are listed as Least Concern (LC) on the IUCN Red List, according to 2022 assessments cited by The World List of Cycads; the Red List itself could not be consulted directly.

Like all cycads not listed in Appendix I, Lepidozamia have been in CITES Appendix II since 4 February 1977: international trade in plants requires permits. The listing carries annotation #4, in the version adopted at CoP20 and in force since 5 March 2026: seeds, spores and pollen, seedlings and tissue cultures obtained in vitro and transported in sterile containers, and cut flowers of artificially propagated plants are not covered by CITES. Lepidozamia seeds can therefore move without a CITES permit. The United Kingdom, the United States and Australia apply CITES through their own national legislation and permit systems. For readers in the European Union, Regulation (EC) No 338/97 places Lepidozamia in its Annex B, the list in force being set by Commission Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026. Before importing or selling a plant, check the documents required with your national CITES authority; this general information does not replace the official texts.

In Australia, the Queensland government's official database classes both species as “special least concern” (code SL) under the Nature Conservation Act 1992, a category that regulates in particular the harvesting and trade of wild plants. The CITES status was checked on Species+ on 1 October 2026; check these statuses on the IUCN Red List and Species+ before any regulatory use. For the amateur, the rule is simple: buy only nursery-raised seedlings of traceable origin.

Growing Lepidozamia

Lepidozamia are forest cycads: they need dappled light, a soil that drains well but never dries out, and mild winters. Lepidozamia peroffskyana is the easier and more widely grown of the two; Lepidozamia hopei, a tropical plant, is more tender and rarer.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Light: partial shade or filtered light, as in open woodland. A plant raised in shade will scorch if moved abruptly into full sun. According to the Australian National Botanic Gardens, Lepidozamia peroffskyana can grow in full sun, but needs shelter from afternoon sun to avoid leaf burn.
  • Soil: free-draining but able to stay moist, rich in stable organic matter. Drainage is essential: root rot is the main cause of loss in waterlogged soil. The Australian National Botanic Gardens recommends a sandy loam, slightly acid to neutral.
  • Watering: regular during the warm season, more cautious in winter or when the plant is resting. The Australian National Botanic Gardens and the Australian Native Plants Society say that Lepidozamia peroffskyana withstands long dry spells, without fertiliser.
  • By the sea: no reliable data on salt spray. A Florida nursery rates its salt tolerance as moderate. Avoid the front line facing the sea.
  • Growth: slow, in successive flushes of leaves. The new leaves, soft and brown, are fragile until they harden.
  • Propagation: from seed, since neither species produces many offsets. An isolated plant does not give fertile seed, for lack of a plant of the other sex and of its specialist pollinators.

Container cultivation

In cool climates, Lepidozamia peroffskyana is best grown in a large tub overwintered frost-free in a conservatory or temperate glasshouse, and Lepidozamia hopei needs a warm glasshouse. For pots, the Australian Native Plants Society advises 2.5 cm of gravel at the bottom and 2.5 cm of coarse sand on the surface, so that the compost does not dry out too fast. Water regularly in summer, keep the plant in bright shade, and acclimatise it gradually before any move into sun.

Cold hardiness

No garden or institution has published results of outdoor cultivation in Europe. The data come from the trade and from amateur growers, mostly on the French forum Fous de palmiers. A nursery in the Canary Islands quotes a minimum of −5 °C (23 °F) for Lepidozamia peroffskyana and considers that it grows very well in the warmest Mediterranean regions. In the United States, a Californian nursery reports that plants survived −10 °C (14 °F) during the Texas cold wave of winter 2021, losing their leaves before regrowing; the age of the plants and their protection are not stated. For Lepidozamia hopei, no measured value was found.

Reports from Europe

In France, Lepidozamia peroffskyana survives in the ground in Montpellier and in Finistère, but loses its foliage at −5 °C (23 °F) in Concarneau and at −8 to −9 °C (17.6 to 15.8 °F) in Montpellier before regrowing. Near Paris, a small sheltered plant survived but did not grow, for lack of warmth. These reports suggest that the species can be tried on the Mediterranean coast and in the mildest Atlantic gardens, in partial shade and very free-draining soil, accepting defoliation in hard winters; the Brittany reports are the closest guide for south-west England and other mild, damp climates. Lepidozamia hopei belongs in a warm glasshouse: no winter report was found for it on the French forums. These are amateur observations of indicative value, not general rules; the posts were re-read in their threads in October 2026.

  • A grower in Montpellier (Hérault, France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his Lepidozamia peroffskyana in the ground, unprotected for years, was completely defoliated in winter 2011–2012 by lows of −8 to −9 °C (17.6 to 15.8 °F), then produced new leaves in August 2012, without loss. He notes slight yellowing on chalky soil.
  • A grower in Concarneau (Finistère, Brittany, France) reports on the same forum that his Lepidozamia peroffskyana in the ground, without winter protection, had its leaves completely scorched in February 2012 after −5 °C (23 °F) for 2 or 3 hours, including the four leaves of summer 2011; the caudex stayed sound. In March 2019 he reported that the plant, sheltered under a Leptospermum scoparium, had come through winter 2017–2018.
  • A grower in the south-eastern suburbs of Paris (banks of the Marne, France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his young Lepidozamia peroffskyana, planted out in summer 2009, withstood −7 °C (19.4 °F) under protection in winter 2009–2010, without damage.
  • The same grower reports that a small plant in the ground, in a sheltered, dry corner where according to him it never falls below −5 °C, stayed perfect after the cold spell of February 2012 (−8.5 °C, 16.7 °F, in the garden), but produced no leaves in three summers, for lack of warmth. He later dug it up before moving house and considers it less hardy than Macrozamia communis: in his words, “you have to be in zone 9”.
  • A grower in Roussillon reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that a friend's unprotected Lepidozamia peroffskyana was completely defoliated at −4 °C (24.8 °F) in winter 2009–2010; the place is not stated.
  • A grower in Ensuès-la-Redonne (Côte Bleue, Bouches-du-Rhône, France), facing the sea, reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that Lepidozamia peroffskyana “does not cope” with sun and “really needs partial shade”: it was the only one of his cycads to give a serious warning sign (post of April 2014).
  • On the same forum, a participant whose location is not given reports that a Lepidozamia hopei was sunburnt in April 2012 by morning sun alone, while a smaller Lepidozamia peroffskyana placed next to it was unaffected.
  • A grower in the Castelli Romani (Lazio, Italy) reports on the Tropicamente forum that his small Lepidozamia peroffskyana spent winter 2011–2012 outdoors at −5 °C (23 °F), under a porch closed on three sides, with careful watering.

Reports from North America

A grower in Houston (Texas, United States) reports on the PalmTalk forum that his young seedling of Lepidozamia peroffskyana regrew with two new leaves after −9 °C (15 °F) in February 2021. Growing conditions and protection are not stated, and the thread, protected by Cloudflare, is known only from a search snippet.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In short: in Britain Lepidozamia are glasshouse plants, some of them very old; in the United States they are grown outdoors in southern Florida and California; in Australia, both species are present in the main botanic gardens. A species listed by a garden in the BGCI PlantSearch database (consulted in October 2026) shows that it has been declared there at some point, not that a living plant is there today.

  • United Kingdom: the Royal Botanic Garden Edinburgh's Garden Explorer (updated 18 October 2026) lists Lepidozamia hopei and Lepidozamia peroffskyana, both from accessions of 1933, among the oldest cycads in the collection; the Edinburgh glasshouses are closed for restoration. In Belfast, the Tropical Ravine of the Botanic Gardens houses two large Lepidozamia peroffskyana; the female produced a huge cone in 2017, which stayed infertile for lack of a male plant (Friends of Belfast Botanic Gardens). Kew declares both species in BGCI PlantSearch, and Glasgow, RHS Wisley and Birmingham declare Lepidozamia peroffskyana. No Lepidozamia growing in the open ground in Britain was found in a verifiable source.
  • United States: Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida) holds both species in its online catalogue, and Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden's Garden Explorer (updated 16 October 2026) lists Lepidozamia hopei. In California, Lotusland names Lepidozamia peroffskyana among the notable plants of its cycad garden, and the Huntington, the San Diego Botanic Garden and the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley declare it in BGCI.
  • Australia: both species are in the Garden Explorer of the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney (updated 1 October 2026) and in the census of the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria, whose Lepidozamia grow in outdoor beds at Melbourne; the Australian Botanic Garden Mount Annan lists Lepidozamia peroffskyana, and Brisbane, Canberra, Cairns and Adelaide declare both species in BGCI.

Toxicity and precautions

Yes, Lepidozamia are poisonous, like all cycads, and especially their seeds. Cycad toxins have caused fatal poisoning in people and livestock in Australia, and European settlers fell ill after eating unprepared seeds. Aboriginal people ate the seeds of Australian cycads, including Lepidozamia, after long detoxification (crushing, prolonged leaching in running water, cooking), described by Asmussen (2011). The large red seeds can attract children and dogs: pick them up as soon as they fall. If any part has been eaten, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Species of the genus Lepidozamia

Both species are accepted by POWO and by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15), and both have an English page on the site.

  • Lepidozamia hopei (W.Hill) Regel, according to POWO and IPNI (“Regel” alone according to The World List of Cycads, see Taxonomy): north-eastern Queensland, tropical rainforest; the tallest known cycad (more than 17 m, up to 20 m depending on the source); leaflets 17 to 30 mm wide; tender; Least Concern (LC, 2022).
  • Lepidozamia peroffskyana Regel: from south-east Queensland to north-eastern New South Wales; trunk 4 to 7 m; leaflets 7 to 14 mm wide; type species of the genus, the most widely grown and the less tender of the two; Least Concern (LC, 2022).

Frequently asked questions

What is the tallest cycad in the world?

Lepidozamia hopei, according to most sources. The Encyclopædia Britannica credits it with more than 17 m, the Gymnosperm Database with up to 20 m and a diameter of 50 cm. These records concern very old specimens; most plants in the wild are much smaller.

How do you tell Lepidozamia hopei from Lepidozamia peroffskyana?

By the width of the leaflets: 17 to 30 mm in Lepidozamia hopei, 7 to 14 mm in Lepidozamia peroffskyana. Origin helps too: the first comes from tropical Queensland, the second from south-east Queensland and New South Wales.

Are Lepidozamia and Macrozamia the same thing?

No, they are two distinct genera, even though Lepidozamia were placed in Macrozamia in the 19th century. Lepidozamia leaflets arise from the top of the leaf axis and have no coloured callus at their base, unlike those of Macrozamia.

Can a Lepidozamia stay outdoors in winter in the UK?

Not on the evidence available: British gardens grow them under glass, and no plant in the open ground was found in a verifiable source. In France, Lepidozamia peroffskyana has survived in the ground in Brittany and on the Mediterranean coast, losing its leaves at −5 °C (23 °F) in Concarneau and at −8 to −9 °C (17.6 to 15.8 °F) in Montpellier before regrowing; near Paris, it survived under shelter but did not grow. A trial is therefore only for the mildest, most sheltered gardens, in partial shade; Lepidozamia hopei, a tropical plant, must be overwintered under glass.

Are Lepidozamia dangerous to dogs?

Yes. Like all cycads they are poisonous, particularly their large red seeds. Pick up fallen seeds and see a vet at the slightest suspicion that one has been eaten.

Why does my Lepidozamia not produce seed?

Because it takes a male plant, a female plant and a pollinator. Lepidozamia are dioecious and pollinated by specialist Australian weevils, specific to each species. An isolated female may form a cone, as at Belfast in 2017, but its seeds stay infertile without hand pollination.
