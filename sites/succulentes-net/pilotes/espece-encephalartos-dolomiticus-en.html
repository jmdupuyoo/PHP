Encephalartos dolomiticus is a blue-leaved cycad endemic to South Africa, known only from the dolomite ridges of the northern Drakensberg near Penge, in south-eastern Limpopo, where it grows in rock crevices and among large boulders in open grassland. This species of the genus Encephalartos, with short, often twisted blue-green leaves, was described in 1988, the same year as Encephalartos verrucosus, now treated as a synonym. Highly prized and very expensive in the horticultural trade, it is listed as Critically Endangered by the IUCN and may already be extinct in the wild: only four plants were found in 2019.

Key facts about Encephalartos dolomiticus

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos dolomiticus Lavranos & D.L.Goode (1988), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15)
  • Former names and synonyms: Encephalartos verrucosus Vorster, Robbertse & S.van der Westh. (1988)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Wolkberg cycad (English, Species+ and SANBI)
  • Natural range: South Africa, south-eastern Limpopo (northern Drakensberg, between Penge and The Downs, Wolkberg area)
  • IUCN status: Critically Endangered (CR), assessment published in 2022; “possibly extinct in the wild” according to PlantZAfrica, which cites the IUCN assessment (4 plants counted in 2019)
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I (whole genus, since 1977); Annex A in the European Union; Specially Protected in Limpopo; wild collection banned throughout South Africa since 2007
  • Documented hardiness: no dated record; tolerates frost according to PlantZAfrica, without a figure (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, open grassland on thin soils over dolomite ridges, rock crevices and boulders; in cultivation, full sun or light shade and well-drained soil according to PlantZAfrica
  • Habit and adult size: trunk up to 2 m long and 25 to 50 cm in diameter, often suckering at the base; leaves 60 to 80 cm
  • In the UK, US and Australia: the Huntington and the San Diego Botanic Garden in California, the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney; no British record
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; treat it as poisonous like all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos dolomiticus

Look for short, stiff leaves that are blue-green on both faces, with a rachis that is usually twisted, giving the crown a slightly untidy look. The original description by Lavranos and Goode (1988) could not be consulted; the PlantZAfrica account of SANBI (L. Zondi and B. Ndlovu, Walter Sisulu and Pretoria National Botanical Gardens, December 2023) gives these characters:

  • Trunk: up to 2 m long and 25 to 50 cm in diameter, erect but becoming decumbent when long; unbranched above ground but often producing suckers at the base; crown covered in buff to greyish woolly bracts 3 to 6 cm long
  • Leaves: rigid, blue-green above and below, 60 to 80 cm long, straight but often curving gently downwards, the rachis typically twisted
  • Leaflets: leathery and narrow, 12 to 17 cm long and 10 to 14 mm wide, slightly hollowed across the upper face, with a sharp tip; margins entire or with one or two teeth on the lower edge only; terminal leaflets often banana-shaped
  • Petiole: about 12 cm long, straight, with small spines and a red-brown collar at the base covered in silky white hairs
  • Male cones: egg-shaped, blue-green, one to four per stem, 30 to 50 cm long and 5 to 10 cm in diameter, on a stalk 8 to 9.5 cm long; they appear in November and shed pollen in midsummer, in December and January
  • Female cones: rounded, blue-green, sometimes turning yellowish, one to four per stem, 30 to 50 cm long and 18 to 25 cm in diameter, on a stalk 4 to 8 cm long hidden in the wool of the stem apex; they break up in May and June
  • Seeds: yellow, 35 to 45 mm long and 20 to 30 mm in diameter

The San Diego nursery Jungle Music describes a colour ranging from powdery white or greyish white to almost aquamarine blue, and full, upright, compact crowns. It notes that the species is not a fast grower but flushes regularly.

Hybrids

No natural hybrid of Encephalartos dolomiticus has been described. In cultivation the risk of hybridisation is real: on the French Fous de palmiers forum, a collector explained in 2013 that seed of this species cost at least 50 euros and that growers with a female plant but no pollen did not hesitate to cross it with a related species and sell the seed as pure; in 2014 he judged it hard to find plants of certain origin. SANBI also notes that plants may have been collected in the past under the name Encephalartos eugene-maraisii, before the species was described.

Possible confusion

Before 1988, according to SANBI, the plants of this area were included in the Encephalartos eugene-maraisii complex, a group of blue-leaved cycads from north-eastern South Africa. PlantZAfrica names four close relatives that share blue-green leaves without a marked basal collar and leaflets without prominent teeth, and gives the following distinctions:

  • Encephalartos dolomiticus: leaves not straight, often twisted, with a well-defined petiole; leaflets usually entire, sometimes with one or two small teeth, twisting gradually along their length; cones blue-green
  • Encephalartos dyerianus: leaves curving outwards near the base, then gently inwards; dense crown; cones yellowish green
  • Encephalartos eugene-maraisii: slightly curved leaves with a visible petiole; leaflets in the outer half twisted at the base into an S shape at 90 degrees; cones reddish brown
  • Encephalartos middelburgensis: straight leaves with a distinct petiole; terminal leaflets often twisted into an S; cones green to reddish brown
  • Encephalartos nubimontanus: slightly twisted leaves forming a rounded, arching crown; straight petioles without prickles; cones green

The World List of Cycads also cites a paper by Hurter and Glen on the description of Encephalartos hirsutus, which compares the two species. Only cones and a documented origin make identification certain.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos dolomiticus is an accepted name. Jacobus Lavranos and Douglas Goode published it in 1988 in the Bulletin du Jardin botanique national de Belgique (58(1–2): 219–224), in a paper titled “Notes on Southern African Cycadales”. The type was collected in 1975 by A. Prozeski in the Drakensberg of the former Transvaal, north of Penge and south of The Downs, at about 1,200 m (holotype at the NH herbarium in Durban, isotype at MO, the Missouri Botanical Garden).

In the same year, P. Vorster, P. J. Robbertse and S. van der Westhuizen described Encephalartos verrucosus in the South African Journal of Botany (54(5): 487–490). The World List of Cycads treats that name as a synonym of Encephalartos dolomiticus, which is therefore the name to use. As neither original paper could be consulted, the exact reasons for the synonymy and the order of publication of the two names have not been checked.

Etymology and history

The epithet dolomiticus refers to dolomite, the magnesium-rich limestone that forms the soil of its range in Limpopo (Haynes, 2022). The epithet of the synonym, verrucosus, is Latin for “warty”; the character it referred to has not been checked.

The type plant was collected in 1975, more than a decade before the description. International trade in cultivated plants began in 1996, according to SANBI. PlantZAfrica notes that the species has become common in private collections even as it has all but vanished from the wild; it cones infrequently and was never abundant, which makes seeds and seedlings difficult and expensive to obtain.

Common names

The English name Wolkberg cycad, after the Wolkberg (“cloud mountain” in Afrikaans), is used by Species+, by SANBI and by the Virtual Cycad Encyclopedia of the Palm & Cycad Societies of Florida. No name in an African language is documented.

In the wild

Encephalartos dolomiticus is confined to a small area at the northern end of the Drakensberg, in south-eastern Limpopo. SANBI describes it as a grassland species on thin soils over dolomite ridges; according to PlantZAfrica, it grows in rock crevices and among large dolomite boulders in open grassland, up to about 1,200 m, in a summer-rainfall climate with 650 to 800 mm a year, hot summers and cool to cold winters with occasional frost.

Population figures differ between sources, but all point to collapse. SANBI's non-detriment finding (NDF, 2012, updated 2015) estimated 175 to 250 plants in 1997 and confirmed fewer than 250 in a 2012 census combining aerial and ground counts, adding that the population, surrounded by poor rural communities, was not monitored between 2004 and 2011 for lack of resources. The 2017 biodiversity management plan gives about 130 plants from the 2012 aerial survey, noting that stems rather than plants may have been counted. PlantZAfrica (2023), drawing on the 2022 IUCN assessment, gives 158 plants seen from a helicopter in 1993, 151 in 2004 (including 17 newly found), 139 in the 2012 aerial survey and only four medium to large plants in 2019, which may since have been stolen: a decline of at least 98% since 1993. It also reports a strongly male-biased sex ratio (four males to one female), attributed to the targeted collecting of female plants, and notes that the remaining plants are now too scattered for natural regeneration. The pollinators of the species have not been studied.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos dolomiticus is listed as Critically Endangered (CR) on the IUCN Red List (assessment by J. D. Bösenberg, published in 2022) under criteria A2acd+4ad; B2ab(i,ii,iv,v); C1+2a(i); D: a severe population decline, a tiny area of occupancy and very few adults. According to PlantZAfrica, the IUCN qualifies it as “possibly extinct in the wild”; the Red List itself could not be consulted directly.

According to SANBI, the main threat is collecting for horticulture and traditional medicine; all the cycads of the Limpopo Drakensberg are targeted by poachers, and the high price of this species increases demand. SANBI records 477 plants exported between 1996 and 2011, worth an estimated 2.86 million rand, with no benefit to the species.

The species is one of the fifteen covered by South Africa's Biodiversity Management Plan for critically endangered and endangered Encephalartos (Government Gazette No. 40793, 21 April 2017). The plan notes that no viable ex situ genebank existed for it, that demand for seedlings exceeded what commercial nurseries could produce, and that the effect of burning and grazing on recruitment was unknown. It calls for a ground survey, biodiversity management agreements with the landowners, and an ex situ genebank built, if feasible, from suckers taken from wild plants; recovery targets were deferred until better data are available.

Like the whole genus, it has been listed in CITES Appendix I since 4 February 1977 and, in the European Union, in Annex A of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, according to Species+, the official CITES and EU database, consulted in October 2026. In South Africa, collecting wild cycads has been banned since February 2007, and the species is Specially Protected in Limpopo, according to SANBI. Plants sold must come from cultivation and carry the required documents; check the current rules before any purchase or import.

Growing Encephalartos dolomiticus

Published growing advice is brief. According to PlantZAfrica, Encephalartos dolomiticus thrives in full sun but tolerates light shade and frost; like many blue-leaved cycads it grows slowly, needs well-drained soil and dislikes excessive moisture, and it suits subtropical and warm temperate regions. Its upright, dense crown makes it a focal point in the garden or among succulents. SANBI's NDF describes it as rare in nurseries.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

Jungle Music recommends full sun in coastal and inland southern California, with a few hours of sun at least if the position is partly shaded, and part sun or filtered light in desert areas, in soil that drains well and is mounded if possible; it warns that the species is not very tolerant of wet soil and can rot. Its habitat, crevices on thin soils in full sun under summer rainfall, points the same way: a mineral, very free-draining soil, watered mainly in the growing season. No source shows that the species needs a limy soil in cultivation, so adding dolomite or lime to the mix is guesswork.

Container cultivation

In climates with frost or wet winters, a deep pot in a mineral, very free-draining mix allows the plant to be kept dry and frost-free in winter. On the French Fous de palmiers forum, a collector reported in 2011 that a South African grower in an area marginal for cold had large plants of several species, among which Encephalartos dolomiticus was “doing well”; the place, temperatures and growing conditions were not given.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

The Huntington and the San Diego Botanic Garden in California report the species to BGCI PlantSearch, which shows that a garden has declared it at some point, not that the plant is alive today; Montgomery Botanical Center lists no accession. In Australia, the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney records it in its living collection. No British garden has been found listing it.

Propagation

The species is propagated from seed from cultivated plants, after hand-pollination, and from suckers. According to SANBI's NDF, seedlings are hard to obtain: despite good viability, only about 5% of seeds germinate. PlantZAfrica describes the method used in South Africa's national gardens. Pollen is collected as soon as it is shed and stored at −15 °C (5 °F); the female cone scales open for three days to two weeks depending on the plant. Seeds are cleaned with a table knife or after soaking in water for two to three days, tested by flotation (fertile seeds sink, though not all of them germinate), stored at room temperature for a year and sown at the beginning of summer, level with the surface of river sand, on a heated bench at 24 to 28 °C (75.2 to 82.4 °F); germination starts six to eight weeks after sowing, later without heat. Seedlings go into 3-litre bags of half compost and half river sand and are watered sparingly. Suckers are removed at about 8 cm in diameter with a clean saw, chisel or knife, the cut treated with fungicide, sulphur or a tree sealant; a sucker of at least 25 cm with its own roots can be planted straight out, while smaller or rootless ones are left to callus in a cool, dry place for a few weeks before potting. Given the risk of hybrids, seed should come from a documented source.

Pests and diseases

According to PlantZAfrica, the blue-leaved cycads of the Highveld are attacked by scale insects, beetles, chewing insects and woolly aphids, controlled with contact or systemic insecticides. Rot in soil that stays wet is the main cultural risk.

Cold hardiness

No dated record of the cold tolerance of Encephalartos dolomiticus exists. In its habitat it meets cool to cold winters with occasional frost, and PlantZAfrica states that it tolerates frost, without giving a figure.

Mediterranean and European experience

No growing report from France or Italy is documented. On the Italian Tropicamente forum, a collector judged in 2008 that the species should cause “no problem” with cold, without having grown it himself. For gardeners in comparable Mediterranean climates, a pot kept dry and frost-free in winter remains the prudent choice until a dated trial is published.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No English-language growing report with a measured value has been found, and no USDA zone is published for the species by a botanical source. Jungle Music notes that its habitat is hot but can freeze in winter. As in the rest of the genus, any frost tolerance shown by a young plant with an underground caudex, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil, would not apply to an adult whose trunk and crown are exposed. In Britain it can only be grown under glass; it grows in California and Sydney, but none of these gardens publishes cold data for it.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data are published for Encephalartos dolomiticus itself, but the risk is documented in the genus: the toxicity of Encephalartos horridus was demonstrated as early as 1944 by Van der Walt, and Encephalartos, like other cycads, contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; the seeds are the most dangerous part. The ASPCA reports vomiting, diarrhoea, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses that have eaten cycads.

Treat the plant as poisonous: wear gloves to clean seeds, pick up any that fall, and keep plants and seeds away from children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

SANBI lists traditional medicine among the reasons for collecting Encephalartos dolomiticus, without saying which parts are used or how. PlantZAfrica recalls that, in isiZulu, most cycads are called isqgiki-somkhovu and are planted at the gate of a homestead to ward off evil spirits; this belief concerns cycads in general, not this species in particular.

Frequently asked questions

Is Encephalartos dolomiticus extinct in the wild?

Possibly. The IUCN lists it as Critically Endangered, and PlantZAfrica, citing the 2022 assessment, reports only four plants in 2019, possibly stolen since. It is now far more common in private collections than in the wild.

Why are its leaves twisted?

It is a character of the species. The rachis is usually twisted and the leaves curve slightly downwards, giving the crown an untidy look that helps to recognise it.

Does it need a limy soil?

There is no evidence that it does. It grows on dolomite in the wild, but no source shows that it needs lime in cultivation. A very free-draining mineral soil matters more.

Is it frost-hardy?

Not proven. PlantZAfrica says it tolerates frost and it meets occasional frost in its habitat, but no dated trial has been published. Outside frost-free climates, grow it in a pot and keep it frost-free in winter.

What is the difference from Encephalartos verrucosus?

None: it is the same taxon. Encephalartos verrucosus, described in the same year, is treated as a synonym of Encephalartos dolomiticus by The World List of Cycads.

Can I buy one legally?

Yes, if the plant was propagated in cultivation and carries the CITES documents required for a species in CITES Appendix I and, in the European Union, Annex A. It is very expensive, and seed of uncertain parentage is common.

Is it poisonous?

It must be assumed so. No data exist for the species, but Encephalartos seeds contain toxins dangerous to people and animals.
