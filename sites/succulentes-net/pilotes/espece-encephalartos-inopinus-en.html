Encephalartos inopinus, the Lydenburg cycad, is a South African cycad with slender stems branching from the base, upright or lying along the rock, and blue-green leaves whose narrow leaflets spread and then curve downwards; the botanist who described it compared it to a Mexican Dioon. This species of the genus Encephalartos, described in 1964, has only ever been known from a small area of gorges in the Olifants and Steelpoort valleys, in the former Lydenburg district, now in Limpopo province according to The World List of Cycads. Poaching has driven it to the edge of extinction in the wild, or perhaps beyond: 677 plants were counted in 1992, 81 in 2004, and none were found during the aerial surveys of 2008 and 2012. It is listed as Critically Endangered (assessment published in 2022). In cultivation it is known to be more prone to rot than most other cycads.

Key facts about Encephalartos inopinus

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos inopinus R.A.Dyer (1964), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15), Species+ and GBIF
  • Former names and synonyms: none, according to The World List of Cycads
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Lydenburg cycad (PlantZAfrica, Species+)
  • Natural range: South Africa, Olifants and Steelpoort valleys in the former Lydenburg district; Limpopo according to The World List of Cycads and the South African Scientific Authority, Mpumalanga according to PlantZAfrica; possibly extinct in the wild
  • IUCN status: Critically Endangered (CR), assessment by J. D. Bösenberg dated 7 May 2020 and published in 2022; already CR in the IUCN action plan of 2003
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I (whole genus, since 1977); Annex A in the European Union, with a negative EU opinion on artificially propagated plants from South Africa; specially protected in Limpopo; wild collection banned throughout South Africa since February 2007
  • Documented hardiness: “relatively frost hardy” according to PlantZAfrica, without a figure; about 0 °C (32 °F) without damage at Ravenna, Italy, in 2012 (apparently a pot plant); no measured value below freezing (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, dolomite cliffs and outcrops in gorges, in sparse to thick deciduous bush, with about 375 mm of rain, mostly in summer; in cultivation, sun or semi-shade and a very free-draining soil
  • Habit and adult size: up to eight stems 17 to 25 cm across and about 2 m tall, procumbent stems reaching about 4.5 m in length; leaves 0.8 to 1.5 m long; male cones 18 to 25 cm, female cone about 31 cm (Dyer, 1964, 1965 and 1971; PlantZAfrica)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Montgomery Botanical Center (accessions of 2005, 2006 and 2021), Fairchild, Lotusland, the Huntington and Berkeley in the United States, Sydney and Brisbane in Australia (BGCI and garden catalogues); no British garden declares it
  • Toxicity: seeds poisonous, as in all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos inopinus

Look first at the foliage: blue-green leaves with an almost straight rachis, often slightly twisted towards the tip, carrying long, narrow leaflets with no teeth or almost none, spreading and then curving down, the lowest pointing downwards and reduced to a few prickles. The stems are relatively slender, branch from the base and some end up lying down. R. A. Dyer considered the foliage “among the most graceful in the genus”. His original description (Dyer, 1964), completed by the description of the cones (Dyer, 1971), gives the following characters:

  • Stems: branching from the base, about 2 m long (rarely 3 m according to Dyer, 1965) and 17 to 23 cm across including the leaf bases; in 1969 the tallest upright stem measured in the wild was about 1.5 m, and the longest, lying on the ground, nearly 4.5 m; PlantZAfrica describes single-stemmed plants and clumps of three to eight stems that sucker freely
  • Leaves: 80 to 110 cm long in the original description (up to 1.5 m according to Dyer, 1965), with a petiole of 10 to 20 cm; rachis hairless, almost straight, sometimes slightly raised and twisted towards the tip; leaf base covered with dense brown wool that wears off with age; foliage bluish green with a silver powdery bloom according to PlantZAfrica
  • Leaflets: median leaflets linear, tapering, 14 to 21 cm long and 8 to 13 mm wide, with a sharp tip, glaucous beneath when young and later green, with entire margins or rarely one or two minute teeth; the upper leaflets point slightly upwards, the median ones spread almost at right angles, the lower ones point downwards, are slightly sickle-shaped and then abruptly reduced to two to six prickles on each side
  • Cones: one to several per head, with green scales densely covered in small white papillae that give them a dull pale green look, a distinctive character among the South African species; PlantZAfrica describes up to five male cones and one to three female cones per crown, the female cones bluish green when young and greenish yellow when ripe, the male cones remaining bluish
  • Male cones: almost cylindrical, 18 to 25 cm long and 6.5 to 8 cm across, on a stalk of 6 to 8 cm
  • Female cones: broadly cylindrical, about 31 cm tall and 15 cm across for the only cone described, still immature, on a stalk of 5 to 6 cm

Hybrids

No natural hybrid of Encephalartos inopinus is documented. Dyer noted in 1964 that no other Encephalartos was known within several miles of the plants. In cultivation, hybridisation is possible whenever several species cone at the same time: to obtain pure seed, control the origin of the pollen.

Possible confusion

With its foliage, the species hardly resembles any other South African Encephalartos: Dyer (1965) thought it recalled a Mexican Dioon, and PlantZAfrica compares it with Dioon and with the Cuban Microcycas. Its relationships remain uncertain: Dyer called it an “enigma” as long as its female cones were unknown, and the South African management plan of 2017 states that no other Encephalartos is considered a close relative. The real risk of confusion concerns young plants and seeds sold under this name. In 2013 a collector reported on the French Fous de palmiers forum that a South African friend had bought four seeds labelled Encephalartos inopinus which did not germinate, and which he suspected were in fact Encephalartos longifolius.

Taxonomy

Robert Allen Dyer described the species in 1964 in Bothalia (8(2): 169–170), without knowing its cones, from two plants in the Lydenburg district, then in the Transvaal. The type (Dyer, Verdoorn and Codd 5788, holotype at Pretoria) was collected in August 1964 at Kromellenboog, about one mile east of the asbestos mine, on a south-facing cliff above the Steelpoort River. Dyer included the species in his revision of the genus in South Africa (Bothalia 8(4), 1965), then described its male and female cones, collected in January 1969 (Bothalia 10(2): 369–371). The World List of Cycads records no synonym.

Etymology and history

The Latin epithet inopinus means “unexpected”. Dyer chose it because of “the coincidence of the discovery of an ‘unexpected’ new species of Encephalartos and on a farm with the name ‘Onverwagt’”, “unexpected” in Dutch (Dyer, 1964, quoted by Haynes, 2022, and The World List of Cycads). PlantZAfrica spells the farm Onverwacht and adds that the discovery of a new cycad was itself unexpected among South African botanists at the time.

The first plant was found by chance in 1955. Dr Helmoed van Hoepen took a sucker from it to Johannesburg, where it was grown, but the parent plant, some distance from Penge, appeared to have died since. A second plant, found by C. A. Els on a cliff east of Kromellenboog, was examined in August 1964 by an expedition of the Botanical Research Institute in Pretoria, which made the description possible. In 1969 G. J. Alberts, of the Transvaal nature conservation service, explored the Olifants valley to map the range of the species and assess unauthorised collecting: he found plants rather scattered, but no colony with young plants showing regeneration from seed (Dyer, 1971).

Common names

In English the species is called the Lydenburg cycad (PlantZAfrica, Species+), after the district where it was discovered. No other name, in English or in a South African language, is documented in a verifiable source.

In the wild

Encephalartos inopinus has only been known from the Olifants and Steelpoort valleys, between Penge and Kromellenboog and a little beyond, in the former Lydenburg district. Recent sources place this area in Limpopo (The World List of Cycads, the South African Scientific Authority, the 2017 management plan), PlantZAfrica in Mpumalanga.

The species grows on dolomite cliffs and outcrops in deep gorges, in sparse to thick deciduous bush (PlantZAfrica). According to the South African Scientific Authority, the plants grow mainly in dense bush, on skeletal soil or on bare rock, on steep north-facing slopes. In 1969 about half the plants recorded grew on north-facing cliffs and rocks, the others in various aspects, some of them facing south (Dyer, 1971). Annual rainfall is about 375 mm, mostly in summer (PlantZAfrica).

Natural reproduction seems always to have been weak. PlantZAfrica reports that baboons destroy the immature female cones, and that the caterpillars of the leopard magpie moth, Zeronopsis leopardina, eat the new leaves of the suckers and stunt their growth. According to the 2017 management plan, the pollinating insect of the species was never identified, and it is highly unlikely that natural processes such as pollination still operate in the wild.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos inopinus is Critically Endangered (CR) on the IUCN Red List. The current assessment, by J. D. Bösenberg, is dated 7 May 2020 and was published in 2022 (GBIF, Crossref); The World List of Cycads gives the criteria A2ad+4ad; B1ab(v)+2ab(v); C2a(i,ii); D, which describe a massive decline in a tiny, very localised population. The previous assessment, by J. S. Donaldson (dated 2009, published in 2010), already listed it as CR. In 2003 the IUCN action plan (Donaldson, 2003) estimated the population at 250 to 300 plants, declining fast, threatened by collecting and by reproductive failure, and outside any protected area.

The non-detriment finding of the South African Scientific Authority (approved in February 2012, updated in May 2015) details the collapse. Helicopter surveys counted 677 plants in 1992, 113 in 2001 and 81 in 2004, falls of 83% and then 28%; the surveys of 2008 and 2012 found no plant, and the species may be extinct in the wild. Poaching for the horticultural trade and private collections is the cause. No permit to collect wild plants is thought ever to have been issued for this species, but plants may have been removed before the provincial laws of the 1970s, and the state nursery at Hartebeesthoek, active from 1975 to 1998, distributed plants. International trade began in 1986, and 96% of it took place after 1995; its peak, between 1995 and 2001, with a record 881 specimens exported in 1997, coincides with the collapse of the wild population. By the end of 2011, 2,149 specimens had been exported from South Africa. The document also notes that 30 to 50% of cycads dug up in the wild die within a few years, that microchips did not deter poachers, and that conservation services in Limpopo were badly understaffed. It concludes that trade in artificially propagated plants has been detrimental, and that exports of seedlings should come only from nurseries registered with CITES.

The Biodiversity Management Plan for Critically Endangered and Endangered Encephalartos species, published in the South African Government Gazette on 21 April 2017, plans a genebank at the Lowveld National Botanical Garden, built from seedlings donated by private growers and members of the Cycad Society of South Africa, and a study of the proclamation of the communal land around Penge, where the species grew, as a nature reserve. It notes that the few plants in state nurseries rarely cone and produce very few seedlings, and that recovery in the wild is not a viable option in the short to medium term; research would be needed to find out whether insects of the genus Porthetes could pollinate a restored population.

Regulation, according to Species+ (consulted through its public API on 26 October 2026) and the South African documents:

  • CITES: Appendix I since 4 February 1977, like the whole genus; seeds are covered as well as plants;
  • European Union: Annex A, current list in Regulation (EU) 2026/1383; for artificially propagated plants from South Africa, a negative opinion covering the whole genus, issued on 3 December 2010, was lifted on 11 March 2011; a negative opinion specific to the species was issued on 20 June 2011, confirmed on 7 February 2013, and remains in force; a new consultation covering the whole genus has been open since 16 September 2026;
  • South Africa: collection of wild cycads banned since February 2007; the species is specially protected under the Limpopo Environmental Management Act of 2003.

These notes do not replace the official texts: check Species+ and the legislation in force before any purchase or import, and ask for documents proving that a plant was propagated in cultivation.

Growing Encephalartos inopinus

Reliable growing information is scarce. According to PlantZAfrica, Encephalartos inopinus suits temperate and subtropical climates, takes sun or semi-shade and frost, and makes a good pot plant. It needs very little water and is more vulnerable to rot than most other cycads: PlantZAfrica advises improving the drainage or planting it on a slope, as it grows in the wild. In its habitat it lives on almost bare rock, with summer rain and a dry winter.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

The habitat points to a mineral, very free-draining soil, a raised or sloping position, summer watering and a dry winter rest. An Italian collector in Ravenna, on the Adriatic coast, gave the same advice on the Tropicamente forum in 2012: the species rots easily, drainage comes first, and in the ground it should be planted on a slope; he does not water his Encephalartos in winter. No in-ground planting outside South Africa is documented with dates and results in a verifiable source.

Container cultivation

PlantZAfrica considers it an ideal pot plant. A grower in the Czech Republic described on the Fous de palmiers forum in April 2015 how he keeps his potted Encephalartos outdoors from May to September, in a small glasshouse in spring and autumn, and from November to February in a dimly lit cellar at 7 to 10 °C (44.6 to 50 °F) at night and 15 to 18 °C (59 to 64.4 °F) by day: his small Encephalartos inopinus, with a 7 cm caudex, produced two new leaves there. In France, a grower in Agen, in the south-west, showed the new flush of leaves of his plant on the same forum in June 2011, without saying whether it was in a pot or in the ground.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

The species is uncommon in public collections. In the United States, Montgomery Botanical Center in Coral Gables, Florida, holds three accessions, received in 2005, 2006 and 2021 according to their accession numbers; Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden in Miami lists it in its catalogue, and Ganna Walska Lotusland, the Huntington, the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley, Atlanta Botanical Garden and three Hawaiian gardens declare it to BGCI PlantSearch. In Australia, the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney lists it in its online catalogue, and Brisbane Botanic Gardens Mount Coot-tha declares it to BGCI. No British garden declares it. A BGCI listing shows that a garden has declared the species at some point, not that a plant is alive today.

Jungle Music, a cycad nursery in Encinitas, California, writes on an undated page that the species wants sun on the coast and inland, and partial sun or filtered light in desert areas, in a very porous mix, as it is very likely to rot if too wet; that it grows moderately to slowly and is harder to establish from offsets than many South African Encephalartos; and that it is among the most expensive cycads to obtain, mature plants with more than a foot of trunk rarely being offered. These are a nursery's observations, not measurements.

Propagation

According to PlantZAfrica, the species is easy to propagate from seed and from suckers, which it produces freely. The young stages are nevertheless delicate: in 2011 a member of the Fous de palmiers forum who had worked in a South African nursery reported that seedlings of Encephalartos inopinus are very sensitive to fungi during germination and while forming their first leaves, and that the nursery drenched them every week with a fungicide (mancozeb or similar), which clearly improved its success rate. Jungle Music, for its part, finds offsets slow to root. In the wild, suckers contribute little to regeneration, which depends mainly on seed (South African Scientific Authority, 2015). Seeds and suckers are covered by CITES Appendix I, and no plant should ever be taken from the wild.

Pests and diseases

Root and stem rot is the main risk: PlantZAfrica considers the species more vulnerable than most cycads. Seedlings are prone to fungal attack (see Propagation). In South Africa, the caterpillars of the leopard magpie moth, Zeronopsis leopardina, eat the young leaves of suckers and slow their growth (PlantZAfrica).

Cold hardiness

The cold hardiness of Encephalartos inopinus has not been measured. PlantZAfrica calls it “relatively frost hardy” and says it tolerates both sun and frost, without giving a figure. The only measured reference point is from Ravenna: about 0 °C (32 °F) without damage in 2012, for a plant apparently in a pot and kept dry in winter. As in other Encephalartos, survival in the cold often concerns young plants whose caudex is still underground, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil; an adult plant with aerial, or even procumbent, stems does not enjoy that protection.

Mediterranean and European experience

The Ravenna plant, on the Adriatic coast of Italy, is the only dated cold report: it came through a winter minimum of about 0 °C without damage in 2012 and, according to the same grower, made no new leaves in 2013. No result from France is documented. In its habitat the winter is dry and the plant grows on bare rock: in a climate with wet winters, a plant in the ground runs a greater risk of rot than one kept in a pot sheltered from the rain, a point that matters for gardens in California, southern Australia or the Mediterranean.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No dated cold report has been published from these countries. No botanical source gives a USDA zone for the species; the zone ratings found on some websites cite no measurement. In Britain, it belongs in a glasshouse kept frost-free and dry in winter.

Toxicity and precautions

Like other cycads, Encephalartos inopinus should be treated as poisonous. Encephalartos contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; these substances affect the liver, the digestive tract and the nervous system, and the seeds are the most dangerous part. PlantZAfrica lists the species as poisonous. No study deals with the toxicity of this species in particular, but that is no reason to consider it harmless. The ASPCA reports vomiting, diarrhoea, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses that have eaten cycads.

Keep plants and seeds out of reach of children and pets, and if any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use of Encephalartos inopinus is documented. The species is sought after for gardens and collections, which has fuelled poaching.

Frequently asked questions

Does Encephalartos inopinus still exist in the wild?

Perhaps not. Counts fell from 677 plants in 1992 to 81 in 2004, and no plant was found during the surveys of 2008 and 2012. The South African Scientific Authority considers that the species may be extinct in the wild; it survives in cultivation.

Where did it grow?

In the gorges of the Olifants and Steelpoort valleys, in the former Lydenburg district of South Africa. Most sources now place the area in Limpopo, PlantZAfrica in Mpumalanga. The plants grew on dolomite cliffs, often facing north.

Why is it called “unexpected”?

Because Dyer wanted to recall the surprise discovery of a new species on a farm called Onverwagt, “unexpected” in Dutch. The Latin inopinus has the same meaning.

How can I recognise it?

By its slender stems branching from the base, often lying down, and its blue-green leaves with long, narrow, almost toothless leaflets that curve downwards. Its cones are covered with small white papillae. Few other South African Encephalartos look anything like it.

Is it difficult to grow?

It can be. It is more prone to rot than most cycads, and its seedlings are sensitive to fungi. A very free-draining soil, little water and a dry winter are essential.

Is it frost-hardy?

Only to a limited, unmeasured extent. It is described as relatively frost hardy in South Africa, and a plant took about 0 °C (32 °F) without damage in Italy. No measured value below freezing has been published.

Can I buy seeds or plants?

Only plants or seeds propagated in cultivation, with the CITES and import documents required. The European Union has a negative opinion in force on artificially propagated plants from South Africa, and the South African authority has found the trade detrimental to the species.

Is it poisonous?

Yes, it should be treated as such, like all cycads. The seeds are the most dangerous part.
