Encephalartos hirsutus is a South African cycad endemic to Limpopo province, where it grew on south-east-facing quartzite cliffs in the Soutpansberg region, between 800 and 1,000 m. This species of the genus Encephalartos, known in English as the Venda cycad, is recognised by its stiff, glaucous blue-green leaves, its dense golden woolly crown and its overlapping leaflets whose bases run down the rachis. Described in 1996 by P. J. H. Hurter and H. F. Glen, it is listed as Critically Endangered: the wild population was then estimated at 400 to 500 plants, but poaching has almost wiped it out, and by 2015 a single wild plant was known.

Key facts about Encephalartos hirsutus

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos hirsutus P.J.H.Hurter (1996), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15)
  • Former names and synonyms: none
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Venda cycad (Species+, SANBI)
  • Natural range: South Africa, Limpopo (Soutpansberg region), three widely separated localities known in 1996, between 800 and 1,000 m
  • IUCN status: Critically Endangered (CR A2ad; B2ab(v); C1+2a(i,ii); D), assessment by J. D. Bösenberg published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I since 4 February 1977; Annex A in the European Union; negative EU opinion on imports of artificially propagated plants from South Africa since 7 February 2013; specially protected in Limpopo, and wild collection banned throughout South Africa
  • Documented hardiness: no measured value; described as frost tolerant by SANBI's PlantZAfrica, without a temperature (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, exposed quartzite cliffs in semi-deciduous scrub; in cultivation, full sun or light shade and a well-drained soil (PlantZAfrica)
  • Habit and adult size: decumbent trunk up to 3.5 m (rarely 4.2 m) long and 35 to 40 cm across; leaves 1.1 to 1.4 m long (Hurter and Glen, 1996)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley, which curates poached plants seized by the US Fish and Wildlife Service, and the Huntington (BGCI); no British or Australian garden declares it
  • Toxicity: not studied for the species; treat it as poisonous like all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos hirsutus

Look for the combination of stiff, glaucous, spiny leaves, like those of several “blue” Encephalartos of northern South Africa, with three characters of its own: a decumbent habit, decurrent leaflets, whose base runs down the rachis, and prominent veins on the underside of the leaflets. The original description by Hurter and Glen (1996) gives the following characters:

  • Trunk: decumbent, suckering at the base, up to 3.5 m (rarely 4.2 m) long and 35 to 40 cm in diameter, covered with old leaf bases
  • Crown: golden, dense, very woolly, turning greyish with age
  • Leaves: numerous, almost sessile, stiff, glaucous, 1.1 to 1.2 m (up to 1.4 m) long, with a recurved tip; petiole swollen at the base, up to 13 cm, woolly; rachis woolly, then almost hairless
  • Leaflets: entire, angled towards the leaf tip at about 50°, the two rows forming an angle of about 40°; they overlap like tiles towards the tip; median leaflets 13 to 17 cm long and 2 to 2.4 cm wide, narrowly elliptic, sickle-shaped, sharp, decurrent at the base; the leaflets at the base of the leaf are reduced to a series of prickles
  • Male cones: five per stem, narrowly ovoid, about 50 cm long and 9 cm across, on a stalk 12 cm long; scales smooth, waxy, bluish green
  • Female cones: one to three per stem, ovoid, about 40 cm long and 35 cm across, apparently stalkless, the 6 cm stalk remaining hidden among the cataphylls of the crown; hairless and smooth
  • Seeds: about 200 per cone, with an orange-red fleshy coat; kernel ellipsoid, 30 to 35 mm long and 15 to 18 mm across

The PlantZAfrica account (Zondi and Mnisi, 2023) follows this description for the most part, but describes the stem as erect though often leaning, gives a petiole of 300 mm and states that the basal leaflets are reduced in size but not to a series of spines, which differs from the original description. It adds that pollen is shed in late summer (November to January) and that plants from the three localities differ in hairiness and in the angle of the leaflets. On the Italian Tropicamente forum, photographs posted in 2012 show cultivated plants with straight leaves and others with wavy leaves; no scientific source describes these forms.

Hybrids

No hybrid of Encephalartos hirsutus is reported. In collections, where most plants of this species are now found, cross-pollination with other blue Encephalartos is possible: to obtain pure seed, isolate the female cones and control the origin of the pollen.

Possible confusion

Hurter and Glen (1996) compared Encephalartos hirsutus with six species with stiff, spiny, glaucous leaves: Encephalartos eugene-maraisii, Encephalartos lehmannii, Encephalartos princeps, Encephalartos dolomiticus, Encephalartos dyerianus and Encephalartos middelburgensis. The differences lie mainly in the leaflets and cones:

  • Encephalartos eugene-maraisii, Encephalartos dolomiticus and Encephalartos princeps also have forward-pointing, overlapping leaflets, but their veins are not prominent and their leaflets are not decurrent;
  • Encephalartos lehmannii and Encephalartos dyerianus have almost sessile leaflets that point backwards and usually do not overlap; Encephalartos middelburgensis has almost sessile leaflets that point clearly backwards;
  • the male cones of Encephalartos eugene-maraisii and Encephalartos lehmannii are covered with a short down, those of Encephalartos princeps have beaked scales; the female cones of Encephalartos dolomiticus and Encephalartos princeps have warty scales, and those of Encephalartos eugene-maraisii and Encephalartos lehmannii are woolly.

According to the authors, decurrent leaflet bases had not been observed in any other Encephalartos. PlantZAfrica likewise considers the Venda cycad unmistakable, its leaves remaining very hairy for most of their life and its incubous leaflets, with prominent longitudinal ridges beneath, being unique among southern African cycads. In the wild, the only other species of the genus growing nearby was Encephalartos transvenosus, with green leaves, which cannot be confused with it.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos hirsutus is an accepted name with no synonym (The World List of Cycads). It was published in the South African Journal of Botany (volume 62, issue 1, pp. 46–48, figure 1), in an article by P. J. H. Hurter, of the Lowveld National Botanical Garden at Nelspruit, and H. F. Glen, of the National Botanical Institute in Pretoria; the name is attributed to Hurter alone. The article was received in May 1995 and revised in October 1995; IPNI and The World List of Cycads date it 1996, although the reference list of The World List of Cycads gives 1995 for the article. The type is a collection by Hurter (no. 94R/1, leaf and male cone) made on 7 June 1994 in the former Northern Province, at 1,000 m (holotype at the National Herbarium, Pretoria). To protect the plants, the authors published only approximate localities, at the level of the squares of the 1:250,000 maps of Alldays and Messina.

Etymology and history

The epithet hirsutus means “hairy” in Latin, a reference to the leaves, which stay hairy (Haynes, 2022, cited by The World List of Cycads).

The history of the species is one of very rapid disappearance. According to the South African National Biodiversity Institute (SANBI, 2015), the population, estimated at 400 to 500 plants when it was described in 1996, numbered only 219 plants in 2004; no plant was found during a helicopter survey in 2012 apart from a single individual with four stems, out of reach on the edge of a cliff in a private nature reserve. International trade began in 1999, only three years after the description, and 224 specimens had been exported from South Africa by the end of 2011. The first poachers dug up entire clumps; a large illegal shipment was seized in the United States, where 17 plants were kept in a secure place. Deloso and colleagues (2025) report that a cohort of poached plants intercepted by the US Fish and Wildlife Service is now curated at the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley; they do not say whether it is the same shipment.

Common names

In English the species is called the Venda cycad (Species+, SANBI, PlantZAfrica, The Gymnosperm Database); no source explains the origin of the name. No Afrikaans or other vernacular name is attested in a verifiable source.

In the wild

In 1996 Encephalartos hirsutus was known only from three widely separated localities in northern Limpopo (then the Northern Province), between 800 and 1,000 m. The plants grew in exposed positions on south-east-facing quartzite cliffs, in moist, semi-deciduous mixed scrub where they were hard to see beneath the trees of Androstachys johnsonii. At the type locality they occupied a dry, south-facing cliff, with Androstachys johnsonii, Adenia spinosa, Barleria bremekampii and Eragrostis superba. Annual rainfall is 350 to 650 mm, mostly in summer (Hurter and Glen, 1996). SANBI places these localities in the Soutpansberg region. The introduction of the PlantZAfrica account mentions open grassland and shrubland on low granite hills, which does not match the habitat described in the same account and in the original description.

Nothing has been published on the biology of the species in the wild. According to the 2017 Biodiversity Management Plan for South Africa's threatened Encephalartos, its pollinator was never identified, no closely related species is known, and research would be needed to find out whether insects of the genus Porthetes could serve as pollinators in a restored population.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos hirsutus is Critically Endangered. The World List of Cycads cites the 2022 IUCN Red List assessment (Bösenberg), under criteria A2ad; B2ab(v); C1+2a(i,ii); D, the last of which corresponds to a population of fewer than 50 mature individuals. The earlier assessment, cited by SANBI, used A4acd; B2ab(iii,iv,v); C1. The species is not listed as Extinct in the Wild, since at least one plant survives and others in unvisited places cannot be ruled out (SANBI, 2015). PlantZAfrica describes it as “Critically Endangered (Possibly Extinct in the Wild)”, with 0 to 10 mature individuals, and reports that no plant could be found in 2006 and that a 2020 assessment located none of the known specimens, while surveys of the rugged terrain continue; these statements are not dated consistently with the SANBI document of 2015, which still reported one plant.

SANBI (2015) attributes the collapse to poaching for gardens and private collections. It states that no permit was ever issued to collect wild plants or seeds, so that it is very likely that all plants in collections come from wild plants removed illegally. Cuts found on wild plants, at first put down to traditional medicine, are more likely the work of poachers removing suckers. No population was monitored in Limpopo between 2004 and 2011.

The 2017 Biodiversity Management Plan (Government Gazette No. 40793) confirms that only one individual remains, in an inaccessible locality, and that the 2012 helicopter survey found no other plant. It mentions unconfirmed reports that the species once occurred in protected areas managed by South African National Parks and plans surveys and isotope tests on cultivated plants said to come from the Kruger National Park. It notes that there is no viable ex situ genebank under state control, but that a small number of confiscated plants are kept in a secure, privately owned locality; it plans two secure genebanks, a confidential database of genetically pure plants in South Africa and abroad, genetic studies of the different localities and the identification of three possible reintroduction sites. It considers recovery not viable in the short to medium term.

Regulation, according to Species+ (consulted through its public API on 25 October 2026) and SANBI:

  • CITES: Appendix I since 4 February 1977, like the whole genus; artificially propagated plants are treated as Appendix II specimens;
  • European Union: Annex A, current list in Regulation (EU) 2026/1383; the EU Scientific Review Group gave a negative opinion on imports of artificially propagated plants from South Africa (source codes A and D) on 3 December 2010, again on 20 June 2011 and on 7 February 2013; this last opinion is in force;
  • South Africa: collection of wild cycads banned since February 2007 (TOPS regulations, then Government Notice No. 371 of May 2012); the species is specially protected under the Limpopo Environmental Management Act (No. 7 of 2003); SANBI considers trade in artificially propagated plants detrimental and allows the export of seedlings only from nurseries registered with CITES.

These notes do not replace the official texts: check Species+ and the legislation in force before any purchase or import, and ask for documents proving that a plant was propagated in cultivation.

Growing Encephalartos hirsutus

Encephalartos hirsutus is almost absent from public collections, and growing rules come only from PlantZAfrica, which describes it as a slow grower that does best in full sun but tolerates light shade, is frost tolerant and does well in cultivation provided it is planted in a well-drained soil; it does not like excess moisture. SANBI describes it as highly sought after and very expensive, to be seen only in elite private collections. A member of the French Fous de palmiers forum, on a placement at a CITES-registered nursery in Pretoria, reported about thirty plants of good size there in 2011; on Tropicamente, a plant seen in Europe with a single leaf was offered for 800 euros in 2012.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

The habitat, well-drained quartzite cliffs in full exposure with 350 to 650 mm of summer rain, points to a mineral, very free-draining soil, sun, watering in summer and a dry rest in winter. No planting in the ground outside South Africa is documented in a verifiable source, and no British, American or Australian garden publishes cultivation details for the species.

Container cultivation

No grower's report on container cultivation has been published. Given the value of the plants and the lack of data, a deep, free-draining container kept frost-free and dry in winter is the prudent choice outside frost-free climates.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

Only two institutions in the United States declare the species to BGCI PlantSearch: the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley and the Huntington in San Marino. At Berkeley, a cohort of poached plants intercepted by the US Fish and Wildlife Service is curated; a pilot study using RADseq DNA sequencing (Handley and Nagalingum, 2018, cited by Deloso and colleagues, 2025) found that this cohort did not hold enough genetic diversity to support a return to the wild. Montgomery Botanical Center lists no accession in its online catalogue. No British or Australian garden declares the species. A BGCI listing shows that a garden has declared the species at some point, not that a plant is alive today.

Propagation

Encephalartos hirsutus suckers at the base (Hurter and Glen, 1996), and each female cone can produce about 200 seeds. In cultivation, hand pollination is needed. PlantZAfrica describes the procedure: visit the male plants regularly to note the date a cone appears and estimate when it will be ripe; collect the pollen as soon as it starts shedding and store it at −15 °C (5 °F); watch the female cone scales, which stay open for three days to two weeks depending on the plant. The seed is left to mature for a year after harvest and sown at the beginning of summer in river sand on a heated bench at 24 to 28 °C (75.2 to 82.4 °F); germination should start four to six weeks later, longer without bottom heat. Suckers can be removed at about 80 mm in diameter, with clean cuts treated with a fungicide or sulphur; those of 250 mm or more with their own roots can be planted straight out, smaller ones should first dry and callus for a few weeks before potting. SANBI allows the export of seedlings only from registered nurseries using legally held parent plants, and would like part of the seed to serve the recovery of the species.

Pests and diseases

No disease or pest specific to Encephalartos hirsutus is described. As with other blue Encephalartos, the main risk in cultivation is rot in a cold, wet soil, which PlantZAfrica's warning against excess moisture confirms.

Cold hardiness

The cold hardiness of Encephalartos hirsutus is not measured: PlantZAfrica calls it frost tolerant without giving a temperature, and no trial or observation of plants in the cold has been published. The habitat, at 800 to 1,000 m in northern Limpopo, receives its rain in summer; no published source gives its minimum temperatures. As in other Encephalartos, survival in the cold often concerns young plants whose caudex is still underground, protected by the soil; an adult plant with a decumbent, aerial trunk does not enjoy that protection.

Mediterranean and European experience

No growing report has been published from France or Italy, where the species is mentioned only for its rarity and price. No observation from Mediterranean gardens is available to suggest how it behaves in a cool, wet winter.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No dated cold report has been published from these countries. No botanical source gives a USDA zone for the species; the zone ratings found on some websites cite no measurement. Given its value and the lack of data, keep it frost-free, in a glasshouse in Britain.

Toxicity and precautions

No study has dealt with the toxicity of Encephalartos hirsutus, but it should not be considered harmless: the toxicity of Encephalartos horridus was demonstrated as early as 1944 (Van der Walt), and Encephalartos, like other cycads, contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; these substances affect the liver, the digestive tract and the nervous system, and the seeds are the most dangerous part. The ASPCA reports vomiting, diarrhoea, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses that have eaten cycads.

Wear gloves to handle seeds, keep plants and seeds out of reach of children and pets, and if any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use of Encephalartos hirsutus is documented. SANBI (2015) notes that cuts on wild plants had been attributed in the literature to medicinal use, but that they are more likely the work of poachers.

Frequently asked questions

Is Encephalartos hirsutus extinct in the wild?

Not officially. It is listed as Critically Endangered. According to SANBI and the 2017 management plan, a single wild plant with four stems was known on the edge of an inaccessible cliff; other plants in unvisited places cannot be ruled out.

Why has it almost disappeared?

Because of poaching. The population fell from 400 to 500 plants in 1996 to 219 in 2004, and then to a single known plant. The plants were dug up for gardens and private collections, in South Africa and abroad.

How can I recognise it?

By its decurrent leaflets with prominent veins beneath. Its leaves are stiff, glaucous and spiny, its crown golden and woolly, and its trunk eventually lies down. These characters separate it from the other blue Encephalartos.

Can I buy one legally?

Only a plant propagated in cultivation, with its documents, and it is very difficult. The species is in CITES Appendix I and EU Annex A, and the EU has a negative opinion on imports from South Africa. According to SANBI, all plants in collections very probably come from illegal removals.

Is it frost-hardy?

Nobody knows for sure. PlantZAfrica calls it frost tolerant, but no observation of plants in the cold has been published. Grow it frost-free.

Where does the name come from?

From the Latin hirsutus, “hairy”. It refers to the leaves, which stay hairy, and to the very woolly crown.
