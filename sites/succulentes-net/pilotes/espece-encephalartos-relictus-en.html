Encephalartos relictus is a cycad from Eswatini (formerly Swaziland), known from a single site in the Lebombo Mountains near Siteki and the Mozambican border, where it no longer grows. This species of the genus Encephalartos was collected in 1971 but only described in 2001, “for posterity”, because no other plant had been found in more than twenty years. It is assessed as Extinct in the Wild and survives only in cultivation. It is a tree-forming cycad with stiff, waxy, blue-grey leaves, and only its male cones are known.

Key facts about Encephalartos relictus

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos relictus P.J.H.Hurter (2001), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15), IPNI, GBIF and Species+
  • Former names and synonyms: none
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: no English name is attested by a reliable source (see Common names)
  • Natural range: Eswatini, a single known site in the Lebombo Mountains (Muti-Muti farm, near Siteki), at about 1,000 m; the species no longer grows there
  • IUCN status: Extinct in the Wild (EW), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated May 2020, published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I since 1977, like the whole genus; Annex A in the European Union, with a negative EU opinion (2013) on artificially propagated plants from South Africa
  • Documented hardiness: none (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, mixed deciduous woodland; requirements in cultivation not documented
  • Habit and adult size: tree-forming, suckering from the base; trunk up to 2.5 m long and 40 to 45 cm in diameter; leaves 1 to 1.2 m, occasionally 1.4 m (original description)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: no botanic garden declares the species in BGCI PlantSearch or in the Montgomery Botanical Center catalogue
  • Toxicity: not documented for the species; toxins known in the genus (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos relictus

Encephalartos relictus is recognised by its stiff, blue-grey, waxy leaves, whose entire-margined leaflets point upwards, and by its hairless, pale yellowish-green male cones. The original description, published by Hurter and Glen in 2001 in Bothalia, gives the following characters.

The plant is tree-forming and produces suckers at the base. The trunk reaches 2.5 m in length and 40 to 45 cm in diameter, and remains covered with the bases of old leaves. The crown and the cataphylls (the scale leaves that protect the growing point) carry a golden-brown down that almost disappears with age.

  • Leaves: numerous, forming a dense, spreading crown; almost without a petiole, 1 to 1.2 m long, sometimes 1.4 m; rachis straight, barely recurved at the tip
  • Leaflets: directed towards the leaf tip, at about 60° to the rachis, the two rows raised towards each other at an angle of about 40°, so that the leaf is an open V in cross-section; median leaflets oblong-lanceolate and pungent, 20 to 25 cm long and 1.4 to 1.7 cm wide, with entire, slightly thickened margins and 20 to 25 prominent veins on the underside; towards the base of the leaf they are reduced to a few prickles
  • Male cones: one to three per trunk, subconical, hairless, pale yellowish green, 20 to 24 cm long and 12 to 15 cm wide, on a stalk 3 to 5 cm long
  • Female cone: unknown

Hybrids

No hybrid of Encephalartos relictus is documented. Since no female plant is known, the species can only take part in a cross as the pollen parent; no such cross has been published.

Possible confusion

The only species with which the original description compares Encephalartos relictus is Encephalartos heenanii, from Eswatini and Mpumalanga, which also has stiff, waxy, blue-grey leaves with prominent veins under the leaflets. Hurter and Glen separate them as follows:

  • leaflets oblong-lanceolate and raised in Encephalartos relictus, ovate-lanceolate and clearly bent downwards in Encephalartos heenanii;
  • straight leaves in a spreading crown in Encephalartos relictus, incurved leaves in a goblet-shaped crown in Encephalartos heenanii;
  • male cones subconical and hairless in Encephalartos relictus, ovoid and downy in Encephalartos heenanii;
  • mixed deciduous woodland for Encephalartos relictus, high-rainfall montane grassland for Encephalartos heenanii.

In practice, the most likely confusion is one of labelling: a plant sold under this name without a documented origin almost certainly belongs to another species, given how little material is known.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos relictus is an accepted species, without synonyms, in The World List of Cycads, IPNI, GBIF and Species+. P.J.H. Hurter described it with H.F. Glen in Bothalia 31(2): 197–199, in 2001. The holotype, a leaf and part of a male cone collected by J.J.P. du Preez on 15 March 1971 on the farm Muti-Muti near Siteki, is kept at the National Herbarium in Pretoria (PRE 33123).

The authors explain that an assessment of the material from Eswatini (Hurter, 1993) convinced them that it represented a distinct species, perhaps already extinct in the wild. As no new material had turned up in more than twenty years, they decided to describe it “for posterity”. There has therefore been no change of name: the species has been known under this name only since its description. Its exact relationships within the genus have not been studied.

Etymology and history

The epithet relictus is a Latin word meaning “abandoned” or “forsaken”; it refers to the status of the species as a relict (Haynes, 2022, via The World List of Cycads). The genus name Encephalartos comes from Greek and means “bread in the head”, a reference to the starchy pith of the trunk.

The history of the species comes down to a few dates: collection of the type in March 1971, photographs of the plant in its habitat and of its male cones by J.J.P. du Preez, and publication in 2001. The description states that the species “grew” at a single site and no longer exists there. How it came into cultivation is not documented by any primary source. In 2003, the IUCN action plan for cycads recorded that it was present in the collection of a single botanic garden.

Common names

No English common name is attested for Encephalartos relictus by a reliable source, and Species+ gives none. The name “Parlota cycad” circulates online, but no primary or institutional source supports it. No name in siSwati or Zulu is attested.

In the wild

Encephalartos relictus no longer grows in the wild. According to the original description, it lived at a single site in Eswatini, in the Lebombo Mountains, at about 1,000 m, in mixed deciduous woodland. The map published with the description already speaks of a “former distribution” and classes the species as Extinct in the Wild under the 1994 IUCN categories. The number of plants known in the wild, the causes of their disappearance and the precise climate of the site are not documented.

The Lebombo Mountains are home to other cycads, including Encephalartos lebomboensis, but the original description does not relate Encephalartos relictus to that species.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos relictus is assessed as Extinct in the Wild (EW) on the IUCN Red List. The assessment, by J.D. Bösenberg, is dated 13 May 2020 and was published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, GBIF, Crossref). The species was already classed in this way in its 2001 description and in the IUCN action plan of 2003 (Donaldson), which counted no wild plants, no population in a protected area and a single botanic garden collection. Species+ records its former presence in Eswatini as extinct.

Regulation, according to Species+ (consulted through its public API on 26 October 2026):

  • CITES: Appendix I since 4 February 1977, like the whole genus;
  • European Union: Annex A, current list in Regulation (EU) 2026/1383 (in force since 29 June 2026); negative opinions of the EU Scientific Review Group, of 20 June 2011 and then of 7 February 2013 (still current), on artificially propagated plants imported from South Africa (source codes A and D), whatever their size.

An Encephalartos relictus offered for sale without CITES (and, in the European Union, EU) documents cannot be bought legally. Beware also of seed offers: in January 2011, a collector in Salento (southern Italy) reported on the Tropicamente forum that a self-styled seller in Cameroon, offering “large quantities” of seed of very rare species, had answered “certainly” when asked whether he also had seed of Encephalartos relictus, as he had for Encephalartos woodii. As no female plant of Encephalartos relictus is known, any offer of pure seed of the species is necessarily false. These notes do not replace the official texts: check Species+ and the national authorities before any purchase or import.

Growing Encephalartos relictus

No published data describe the cultivation of Encephalartos relictus. The very few known plants are in collections, and Nong Nooch Tropical Botanical Garden in Thailand, which grows every other Encephalartos, reported in 2022 that it did not hold this species (Lindstrom, CYCADS, 2022). Its original habitat, mixed deciduous woodland at about 1,000 m, is not enough to set cultivation rules; the general advice given for the genus (a very free-draining medium, very little water in winter) remains the only available basis.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

No result of growing the species in the ground, anywhere in the world, has been published.

Container cultivation

No published account describes the species in a pot. The general rules for Encephalartos in containers apply: a deep pot, a mineral, very free-draining mix, full light and dry, frost-free conditions in winter.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

No institution declares Encephalartos relictus in BGCI PlantSearch (consulted on 26 October 2026), and the Montgomery Botanical Center catalogue in Florida has no accession of it; no British, American or Australian botanic garden is known to hold it. The only collection mentioned by the IUCN action plan (2003) is that of a single, unnamed botanic garden. On the French forum Fous de palmiers, a member returning from South Africa in 2011 noted only that the species was practically impossible to find in the trade.

Propagation

Sexual reproduction is impossible today because no female plant is known: no pure seed of the species can be produced. The only possible method is the separation of suckers, since the plant offsets at its base. No published source describes attempts at tissue culture or crossing with another species.

Pests and diseases

No disease or pest specific to Encephalartos relictus is documented. As with other cycads, cold, waterlogged soil is the main cause of root rot in cultivation.

Cold hardiness

No cold-hardiness data exist for Encephalartos relictus: no grower and no garden has published a temperature reached by the species, and the climate of its former site is not documented.

Mediterranean and European experience

No result of cultivation has been documented in France, Italy or elsewhere in Europe; the European forums mention the species only as a rarity or a wish-list plant. For other Encephalartos, survivals observed in cold areas often concern young plants whose caudex is still underground, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil or by snow; an adult plant with an aerial trunk, as in this species, would not benefit from this protection.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No hardiness data have been published in any of these countries, where no garden is known to hold the species. USDA zone estimates found online rest on no observation; no USDA zone is documented for Encephalartos relictus in a reliable source. Given the rarity of the plant, growing it frost-free is essential.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data have been published for Encephalartos relictus, but the risk is documented in the genus: the toxicity of Encephalartos horridus was demonstrated as early as 1944 (Van der Walt), and Encephalartos contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; these substances affect the liver, the digestive tract and the nervous system. Treat the plant as poisonous and keep it out of reach of children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet.

Traditional uses

No traditional use of Encephalartos relictus is documented.

Frequently asked questions

Does Encephalartos relictus still exist in the wild?

No. It has been classed as Extinct in the Wild since its description. It was found at a single site in Eswatini, and no other plant has been reported since the 1971 collection.

Why does it not produce seed?

Because no female cone is known. The original description mentions only male cones; without a female plant of the same species, no pure seed can be obtained. Its case recalls that of Encephalartos woodii, known from a single male plant.

What does relictus mean?

“Abandoned” or “forsaken” in Latin. The name stresses that the species is a relict, known from a single site.

How can it be told from Encephalartos heenanii?

By the leaflets and the cones. Those of Encephalartos relictus are raised and oblong, those of Encephalartos heenanii bent downwards; the male cones are hairless in the former, downy in the latter. Their habitats also differ: mixed woodland for one, montane grassland for the other.

Can you buy an Encephalartos relictus?

In practice, almost never. Very few plants are known, and any trade requires CITES documents and, in the European Union, a certificate for an Annex A species. An offer without a documented origin, and any offer of seed, should be treated with the greatest suspicion.

Is it frost-hardy?

Nobody knows. No observation has ever been published, and the plant should be grown frost-free.

Is it poisonous?

It should be treated as such. No data exist for the species, but Encephalartos contain toxins that are dangerous to people and animals, especially in their seeds.
