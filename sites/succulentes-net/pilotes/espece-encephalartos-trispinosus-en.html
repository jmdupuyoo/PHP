Encephalartos trispinosus, the Bushman's River cycad, is a small South African cycad with glaucous, recurved leaves and leaflets armed with one or two spiny lobes, which grows in the dry thicket of the lower Bushmans and Great Fish river valleys, in the Eastern Cape. This species of the genus Encephalartos was long confused with its neighbours: described in 1863 as a variety of Encephalartos horridus (Encephalartos horridus var. trispinosus), then included in Encephalartos lehmannii, it was recognised as a species only in 1965. With time it forms clumps of short stems, and its cones are the best way to tell it from these two species. Assessed as Vulnerable by the IUCN, it is said to withstand frost in its homeland, yet in Italy young potted plants did not survive nights of −4 to −7 °C (24.8 to 19.4 °F).

Key facts about Encephalartos trispinosus

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos trispinosus (Hook.) R.A.Dyer (1965), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15), GBIF and Species+
  • Former names and synonyms: Encephalartos horridus var. trispinosus Hook. (1863, published with the spelling trispinosa), the basionym; long included in Encephalartos lehmannii (Hutchinson and Rattray 1933, Henderson 1945)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Bushman's River cycad (PlantZAfrica, Species+)
  • Natural range: South Africa, Eastern Cape: lower valleys of the Bushmans and Great Fish rivers and the valleys in between (Alexandria, Bathurst and Albany districts)
  • IUCN status: Vulnerable (VU A2acd+4acd; B1ab(iii,v)+2ab(iii,v)), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated 29 May 2020 and published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I since 1977, like the whole genus; Annex A in the European Union, with a positive EU opinion (2011) for artificially propagated plants from South Africa with a caudex up to 15 cm in diameter
  • Documented hardiness: regular frosts in its natural range (PlantZAfrica); in Italy, a young potted plant came through −3 °C (26.6 °F) but died after −7 °C (19.4 °F) in a cold glasshouse (Ravenna, 2010); a Californian nursery claims an absolute limit of about −5.6 to −6.7 °C (20 to 22 °F) (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: full sun, well-drained soil, little water once established (PlantZAfrica)
  • Habit and adult size: stems up to 1 m tall and 25 to 30 cm in diameter, often branching from the base; leaves 0.75 to 1.25 m long (Dyer, 1965)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Oxford Botanic Garden (BGCI); the Montgomery Botanical Center (three accessions), the Huntington, Lotusland, Fairchild, Longwood, the Chicago Botanic Garden and others in the United States; Royal Botanic Garden Sydney and Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria (outdoor bed in Melbourne)
  • Toxicity: seeds toxic, as in all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos trispinosus

It is a modest-sized Encephalartos with short, often clustered stems, glaucous leaves that curve back and twist towards their tip, and leaflets bearing one or two spiny lobes on their lower margin. R.A. Dyer (1965) describes it as follows, completed by PlantZAfrica:

  • Stems: single or, more often, branching from the base, up to 1 m tall and 25 to 30 cm in diameter; according to PlantZAfrica they tend to lean, and an adult plant may have four to six, with suckers at the base;
  • Leaves: 0.75 to 1.25 m long, including a petiole of 15 to 22 cm, spreading then recurved and often twisted towards the tip, hairless; glaucous green when young, then green with age (Dyer), more or less bluish grey depending on the population (PlantZAfrica); a dirty white collar surrounds the woolly swelling at the base;
  • Leaflets: the median ones linear-lanceolate, often slightly curved, 10 to 18 cm long and 1.5 to 2.5 cm wide, ending in a spine up to 6 mm long; some or all carry on the lower margin one or two pungent lobes 1 to 3 cm long, sometimes twisted; towards the base of the leaf the leaflets are smaller, spaced out and entire;
  • Cones: solitary, bluish green or yellowish green according to Dyer, bright yellow according to PlantZAfrica, on a short, thick stalk; one per stem, sometimes two in males;
  • Male cones: almost cylindrical, narrowed at both ends, 25 to 35 cm long and 6.5 to 7.5 cm in diameter, the face of the scales extended into a beak about 7 mm long;
  • Female cones: broadly cylindrical or ovoid, 45 to 50 cm long and 16 to 18 cm in diameter; the face of the scales, projecting 2.5 to 3 cm, is much wrinkled and pimpled, rarely almost smooth, and their recurved side lobes are 2 to 2.5 cm long;
  • Seeds: red to yellowish orange, about 5 cm long and 2 cm across.

Dyer points out that his cone measurements rest on little material. According to PlantZAfrica, the cones appear in midsummer in the southern hemisphere, the male cones are harvested in autumn (April), and the female cones are pollinated in April and May. The same page describes leaflets with three lobes, each tipped with a spine. Jungle Music, a nursery in southern California, stresses that it is one of the most variable Encephalartos: generally a “blue” cycad, nearer to white in its words, but sometimes green, more or less spiny, with greyish-green female cones and pale to bright yellow male cones.

Hybrids

Natural hybrids are reported. Dyer (1965) observed, two miles upstream on the Bushmans River and in the lower Great Fish valley, plants that seem to combine the characters of Encephalartos trispinosus and Encephalartos altensteinii, both present on the spot: some looked to him like first-generation hybrids, others like second-generation offspring close to one parent or the other. He did not rule out the involvement of Encephalartos arenarius and Encephalartos latifrons, which grow a few miles away, but considered the dwarf Encephalartos caffer unconnected with these crosses. Some plants from the Bathurst district have, in his view, a parentage impossible to unravel. The Montgomery Botanical Center lists an Encephalartos altensteinii × trispinosus hybrid in its catalogue.

In cultivation, the risk of hybridisation is high. On the French Fous de palmiers forum, a grower warned in 2011 that plants sold as Encephalartos trispinosus are often crossed with Encephalartos lehmannii when the pollen used is not controlled, and that some show leaflets with folded lobes like those of Encephalartos horridus, a frequent sign of hybridisation.

Possible confusion

Of all the species, Encephalartos trispinosus is “the most difficult to define”, wrote Dyer (1965). He compares it with the three species with which it has been confused, relying first on the face of the female cone scales: almost smooth in Encephalartos lehmannii, ribbed in Encephalartos horridus, wrinkled in Encephalartos trispinosus, warty in Encephalartos princeps.

  • Encephalartos horridus: the closest species. Its stems are mostly underground, its leaflets usually broader (rarely less than 2.5 cm), with deeper lobes more regularly twisted out of the plane of the leaflet; the face of its female cone scales is fairly smooth, with ribs towards the tip, and dark reddish brown before maturity, whereas it is much wrinkled and pimpled, bluish green or yellowish, in Encephalartos trispinosus, whose scales also have longer side lobes. Dyer admits that the boundary between variants of the two species is not always clear;
  • Encephalartos lehmannii: more robust, with straight or almost straight leaves, a large basal swelling, almost always entire leaflets and almost smooth female cone scales; it grows further west, inland;
  • Encephalartos princeps: leaflets usually without lobes, warty female cone scales; it grows further east, in the Great Kei basin.

According to Dyer, the ranges of these four species do not overlap: neither typical Encephalartos horridus, confined to the Uitenhage and Port Elizabeth districts, nor Encephalartos lehmannii reaches the Bushmans River. Without cones, the origin of the plant is therefore the best clue, but it is seldom known for plants in the trade. Finally, Dyer warns that natural hybrids, such as those between Encephalartos altensteinii and Encephalartos trispinosus, fall outside his identification key and may lead to the wrong species.

Taxonomy

The plant was first described by William Jackson Hooker in 1863, in Curtis's Botanical Magazine (plate 5371), as a variety of Encephalartos horridus: Encephalartos horridus var. trispinosa, an epithet that The World List of Cycads and Dyer (1965, in Bothalia) spell trispinosus. The type is this plate, with a specimen kept at Kew in 1880. Schuster (1932) still placed it in Encephalartos horridus, while Hutchinson and Rattray (1933) and then Henderson (1945, “form B1”) included it in Encephalartos lehmannii.

Robert Allen Dyer raised it to species rank in 1965, in the Journal of South African Botany (31: 112–116), after extensive fieldwork: he judged the plants of the lower Bushmans and Great Fish valleys “sufficiently distinct, numerous, widespread and fairly uniform” to form a species, closer to Encephalartos horridus than to Encephalartos lehmannii. The same year he divided Henderson's broad Encephalartos lehmannii among three species: Encephalartos lehmannii, Encephalartos trispinosus and Encephalartos princeps. He acknowledged that Hooker's plant cannot be proved to have come from this region, and regarded as typical a collection made by Galpin in 1898 (no. 3083), with a female cone, identified at the time from the Botanical Magazine plate. The World List of Cycads cites no synonym other than the basionym. For the reader, this means that old plants may be labelled Encephalartos horridus or Encephalartos lehmannii.

Etymology and history

The epithet comes from the Latin tri- (“three”) and spinosus (“spiny”), because the median leaflets usually carry two marginal spines and a terminal one (Haynes, 2022, via The World List of Cycads). Hooker already explained it in 1863: the leaflets have “a strong tendency to bear, on their lower margin, two large spiny lobes which, with the terminal spine, justify the name”.

Dyer also recalls that the first botanist to wonder about the relationships between the cycads of the Eastern Cape was Charles Zeyher, whose notes, read in 1852, already invited recognition of this “play of nature” between populations. At Kirstenbosch National Botanical Garden, a living collection of the species serves education and conservation, supplying seeds and young plants to botanic gardens and growers (PlantZAfrica).

Common names

In English, PlantZAfrica and Species+ use “Bushman's River cycad”, after the river whose lower valley the species occupies. No Afrikaans name is documented. Jungle Music describes it as a “smaller blue South African cycad”, a description rather than a name.

In the wild

Encephalartos trispinosus is endemic to the Eastern Cape, South Africa. Dyer (1965) places it in the karroid thicket of the lower valleys of the Bushmans and Great Fish rivers and the valleys in between, and cites collections from the former divisions of Alexandria, Bathurst, Albany, Victoria East and Peddie. PlantZAfrica describes it as fairly common in the Bathurst, Alexandria and Albany districts, among rocks in full sun or in the shade of bush. The climate there is hot in summer and cold in winter, with regular frosts, and the area receives 600 to 725 mm of rain a year, mostly in summer (PlantZAfrica); Jungle Music's mention of “very little precipitation” understates this. Dyer also notes that it grows mainly on shales, whereas Encephalartos lehmannii prefers sandstones and Encephalartos princeps dolerites.

According to PlantZAfrica, pollination is assumed to be by wind, but insect activity is intense when the pollen is shed, and insects may contribute.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos trispinosus is assessed as Vulnerable (VU) on the IUCN Red List, under criteria A2acd+4acd; B1ab(iii,v)+2ab(iii,v), which reflect a past and projected decline in numbers and a small, declining range. The assessment, by J.D. Bösenberg, is dated 29 May 2020 and was published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, GBIF, Crossref). In 2003 the IUCN cycad action plan (Donaldson, 2003) already classed it as Vulnerable (VU A4cd; C1), with 3,500 to 10,000 plants, a population in a reserve, a high decline caused mainly by over-collecting and minor habitat loss; it was also held in public and private collections and in a gene bank.

Regulation, according to Species+ (consulted through its public API on 26 October 2026):

  • CITES: Appendix I since 4 February 1977, like the whole genus;
  • European Union: Annex A, current list in Regulation (EU) 2026/1383 (in force since 29 June 2026); for artificially propagated plants from South Africa (source codes A and D), a negative EU opinion covering the whole genus, issued on 3 December 2010, was lifted on 11 March 2011; a positive opinion specific to the species, limited to plants with a caudex up to 15 cm in diameter, has applied since 20 June 2011, and a new consultation on the whole genus has been open since 16 September 2026;
  • South Africa: the collection of wild cycads (plants and seeds) has been prohibited since 2012, except for conservation purposes (Government Notice no. 371, cited by SANBI's non-detriment finding for Encephalartos horridus).

Seeds are covered in the same way as plants. Any plant offered for sale must come from cultivation and carry the CITES documents required by the countries concerned (and, in the European Union, the EU documents). These notes do not replace the official texts: check Species+ and the national authorities before any purchase or import.

Growing Encephalartos trispinosus

This is a cycad for dry, sunny climates, slow but easy to grow, and long-lived in a sunny, well-drained position. PlantZAfrica recommends it for hot, dry climates and for areas exposed to frost, as an accent plant in rock gardens, desert gardens, lawns or containers, and lists it for all five of South Africa's horticultural zones, from the frost-free coasts to the frosty Highveld; it suggests low-growing aloes such as Aloe arborescens, Carpobrotus and Arctotis as companions.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

PlantZAfrica's advice:

  • Position: full sun;
  • Soil: well drained; a thick layer of well-rotted compost in autumn and a balanced fertiliser in spring (inorganic, with bonemeal and an organic fertiliser) keep the plant in good condition;
  • Watering: the plant needs little water once established;
  • Habit: low-growing, it suckers freely and forms large grey clumps.

Jungle Music, at Encinitas in southern California, grows it in full or part-day sun on the coast, in sun in the Californian deserts but under protection or filtered light in places like Arizona, and advises planting it on a mound where the soil is heavy or clay, somewhere the watering can be controlled. It is one of the slowest species: a grower in Ravenna (Emilia-Romagna, northern Italy) also ranked it among his slowest in 2009, and a grower in the Canary Islands found in 2008 that it stayed “waiting” there while other species grew fast (Tropicamente forum).

Container cultivation

PlantZAfrica recommends it as a container plant, but in a pot excess water is the main danger. On the Tropicamente forum, an Italian grower found in 2010 that the main root of his young plant had completely rotted after he added potting compost and peat to the original mineral mix, and a grower in Ravenna recounted in 2013 that, when he threw away his plant, killed by cold in 2010, he found its main root long dried out, which explained its poor growth. The young plant that replaced it, in a free-draining mix and drip-watered daily in the warm season, produced leaves two and a half times larger than the previous ones in 2011. In south-west France, a grower in the Tarn reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that a small potted plant in afternoon sun had its caudex slightly blackened in the heat of June 2012, without losing its leaves, and was producing on average two leaves a year in 2014.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

BGCI PlantSearch lists 22 institutions holding the species (two records). In the United Kingdom, Oxford Botanic Garden declares it, without stating whether it grows under glass. In the United States, the Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida) lists three accessions in its online catalogue, one of whose numbers points to 1964 and another to 2012; BGCI also lists the Huntington, the San Diego Zoo, Descanso Gardens, the Cal State Fullerton Arboretum, UC Berkeley, the Atlanta Botanical Garden, the Chicago Botanic Garden, Longwood Gardens, the Houston Botanic Garden, the Naples Botanical Garden and two Hawaiian gardens; Lotusland, in Santa Barbara, names it among the notable species of its cycad garden, and Fairchild lists it in its online collection catalogue. In Australia, Royal Botanic Garden Sydney declares it, and Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria grows it in an outdoor bed at Melbourne Gardens according to its living collection census. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not a living plant on display today. In Europe, the Palmengarten in Frankfurt grows it under glass, as does the Parc de la Tête d'Or in Lyon (photographs posted on the Fous de palmiers forum in January 2008); the Naples botanic garden held three specimens in 1993–1994 (Gaudio et al., 1997). Jungle Music describes it as rare but fairly affordable for a blue cycad, and usually has plants from seedlings to coning size.

Propagation

According to PlantZAfrica, the species is propagated from seed or by removing well-formed suckers:

  • Seed: to obtain true seed, the female cone must be hand-pollinated with pollen from a male cone of the same species; the average germination rate is 30 to 40%. Seeds are sown in spring on clean coarse sand with bottom heat at 25 to 28 °C (77 to 82.4 °F); they germinate in two months, and the seedlings can be potted on ten months later into 3-litre sleeves of free-draining mix, with sparing watering;
  • Suckers: in early spring, remove suckers about 25 cm in diameter after cutting off all their leaves; let the wound dry for two weeks before planting them in clean sand, then plant them out once rooted.

In Corse-du-Sud (Corsica), a grower on the Fous de palmiers forum obtained viable-looking seeds in July 2013 from a female plant (stem 26 cm in diameter), hand-pollinated a year earlier by injecting a mixture of pollen and water into the cone; until then he had thought he was growing an Encephalartos princeps, the name under which he had received the plant. In 2014 the seeds he had distributed did not germinate, probably because fertilisation had failed. On the same forum, a grower reported excellent results in 2012 with unrooted suckers treated with fungicide. For the general rules, see the article on growing cycads from seed.

Pests and diseases

PlantZAfrica cites scale insects and mealybugs, which settle under the leaves, night-active weevils that destroy the seeds, and a tiny mite that damages the young leaves as they emerge; it recommends insecticide treatments, to be adapted to local regulations. In cultivation, root rot caused by excess water is the most frequently reported cause of failure (see Container cultivation).

Cold hardiness

In its natural range the species experiences regular frosts, and PlantZAfrica describes it as hardy and frost-resistant. European reports, all on young potted plants, are more mixed, and no measurement exists for an adult plant in the ground. As with all Encephalartos, survivals in cold areas often concern young plants whose caudex is still underground, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil; a plant with an aerial stem does not benefit from this protection.

Mediterranean and European experience

  • Northern Italy: a grower in Ravenna reports on the Tropicamente forum that he had grown Encephalartos trispinosus in a pot since 2005, slightly sheltered from winter rain, and that it showed no scorch at −3 °C (26.6 °F) in winters 2006–2007 and 2007–2008. In February 2010, after one night at −7 °C (19.4 °F) and another at −5 °C (23 °F), with days below freezing, in a cold glasshouse, several leaves were slightly scorched; the plant was dead in March 2010.
  • Italy, location not stated: another grower reports that a single night at −4 °C (24.8 °F), in winter 2010–2011, “scorched” all his Encephalartos lehmannii and Encephalartos trispinosus, without saying whether they were in pots or in the ground.
  • France: no temperature record has been published; the only reports concern potted plants in the Tarn (heat of 2012) and in Corse-du-Sud (cones and seeds in 2013).

This Mediterranean experience is relevant to readers in comparable climates, such as coastal California or southern Australia: the plant should be tried in the ground only in a very free-draining soil, in sun and sheltered from winter rain; elsewhere, a container overwintered frost-free is safer.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

  • United States: Jungle Music writes that the species “easily withstands a freeze” and claims an absolute cold tolerance of about −5.6 to −6.7 °C (20 to 22 °F); this is a nursery's rating, not accompanied by dated observations.
  • Australia: a plant grows in an outdoor bed in Melbourne (Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria census), without published winter data.
  • United Kingdom: no outdoor result is documented.

In a tropical climate, by contrast, the Nong Nooch Tropical Botanical Garden in Thailand finds that the species grows but only rarely cones (Lindstrom, CYCADS, 2022). No USDA hardiness zone is documented for Encephalartos trispinosus in a botanical source.

Toxicity and precautions

Like other cycads, Encephalartos trispinosus contains toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a toxin affecting the liver and the nervous system; the seeds are the most dangerous part. Its leaflets are stiff and their lobes sharp. Wear gloves to handle the seeds, and keep plants and seeds out of reach of children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use specific to the species is described; PlantZAfrica presents it as an ornamental and collector's plant.

Frequently asked questions

Is Encephalartos trispinosus frost-hardy?

Not reliably. It experiences regular frosts in its homeland, but in Italy a young potted plant died after −7 °C (19.4 °F) in a cold glasshouse, and another grower lost his plants after a night at −4 °C (24.8 °F). In mild Mediterranean-type climates it can be tried in the ground in a very free-draining soil; elsewhere, prefer a container overwintered frost-free.

How can it be told from Encephalartos horridus?

Mainly by the cones. The female cone scales of Encephalartos trispinosus are much wrinkled and pimpled, those of Encephalartos horridus fairly smooth; its leaflets are also narrower, with less regularly twisted lobes, and its stems more often above ground. Origin helps too: Encephalartos trispinosus grows further east.

And from Encephalartos lehmannii?

By the leaflets. Those of Encephalartos lehmannii are almost always entire, while those of Encephalartos trispinosus carry one or two spiny lobes; Encephalartos lehmannii is also more robust, with straighter leaves.

What does “trispinosus” mean?

“Three-spined”, in Latin: the leaflets often carry two spines on the margin and one at the tip.

Does it grow fast?

No. It is one of the slowest species, but a young plant watered well in summer can produce two leaves a year.

Can it be grown in a pot?

Yes. PlantZAfrica recommends it as a container plant. The mix must be very free-draining: the reported failures mostly come from rotten roots.

Is it poisonous?

Yes, especially its seeds, like all cycads.
