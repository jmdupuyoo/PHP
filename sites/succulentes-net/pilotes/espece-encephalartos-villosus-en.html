Encephalartos villosus is an understorey cycad of the coastal forests and wooded ravines of eastern South Africa, from around East London to KwaZulu-Natal, and of Eswatini. Described in 1867 by the French botanist Charles Lemaire from plants probably shipped from Natal to a nurseryman in Ghent, this species of the genus Encephalartos owes its name, “hairy”, to the white wool covering its young leaves. Its stem usually stays underground, and its glossy green leaves, 1.25 to 3 m long, spread gracefully. It is by far the most widespread Encephalartos in southern Africa, and so common in cultivation there that it is known as the poor man's cycad. The IUCN lists it as Least Concern. In Europe it has been grown since the nineteenth century, outdoors at La Mortola, on the Italian Riviera, by 1897.

Key facts about Encephalartos villosus

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos villosus Lem. (1867), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15), GBIF and Species+
  • Synonyms: Encephalartos striatus Stapf & Burtt Davy (1926) and Encephalartos villosus f. intermedius Henn. (1890), according to The World List of Cycads; Encephalartos mackenii, a horticultural name referred to the species by Dyer (1965)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: poor man's cycad (PlantZAfrica, Species+)
  • Natural range: South Africa (Eastern Cape, KwaZulu-Natal) and Eswatini (The World List of Cycads); Species+ also lists Mozambique
  • IUCN status: Least Concern (LC), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated 20 May 2020 and published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I since 1977, like the whole genus; Annex A in the European Union, with a positive EU opinion for artificially propagated plants from South Africa with a caudex up to 7 cm in diameter
  • Documented hardiness: no measured value; according to PlantZAfrica, frost burns the leaves but does not easily kill established plants (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: light or partial shade, well-drained soil, sufficient moisture, frost-free conditions (PlantZAfrica)
  • Habit and adult size: stem underground or barely emerging, rarely up to 40 cm; leaves 1.25 to 3 m long; clumps up to 6 m across
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Kew, the Royal Botanic Garden Edinburgh, Glasgow and RHS Wisley (BGCI); the Montgomery Botanical Center (nine accessions), Fairchild, the Huntington and some twenty other American gardens; Melbourne (outdoor bed), Sydney, Adelaide, Cairns and Mount Tomah in Australia
  • Toxicity: seeds toxic, as in all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos villosus

It is a dwarf Encephalartos with no visible trunk, whose long, glossy green leaves form a broad rosette and whose lower leaflets are reduced to a row of prickles along the petiole. R.A. Dyer (1965) describes it as follows, with some additions from PlantZAfrica:

  • Stem: single, sometimes branched, perhaps after injury; underground or with the crown slightly exposed, about 30 cm long and 20 cm in diameter; PlantZAfrica reports above-ground stems up to 40 cm tall and 25 cm in diameter, occasional suckers and plants often growing in clusters;
  • Leaves: 1.25 to 2.5 m long (up to 3 m according to PlantZAfrica), almost erect or gracefully arching and spreading; the petiole is at first covered with white wool, which gives the species its name;
  • Leaflets: glossy green, paler beneath, spreading to recurved, reduced towards the base of the leaf to a row of prickles; the median ones linear to linear-lanceolate, 15 to 25 cm long (rarely 30), 1.5 to 2.5 cm wide, usually with one to three forward-pointing spines on each margin (PlantZAfrica gives a width of 15 to 20 mm);
  • Cones: one to several together, greenish yellow to orange-yellow; male cone tapering, up to about 65 cm long and 6.5 to 12 cm in diameter (60 to 70 by 12 to 15 cm, lemon yellow and up to five per plant according to PlantZAfrica, which notes an unpleasant smell at maturity); female cone subcylindrical, 30 to 40 cm long and 12 to 13 cm in diameter according to Dyer (30 to 50 by 20 to 25 cm, deep yellow, one to four per plant according to PlantZAfrica);
  • Seeds: scarlet to dark red, 2.5 to 3.3 cm long and 1.8 to 2 cm wide.

Size varies greatly from one region to another: plants from Eswatini are sometimes so robust that a separate variety was proposed for them, which Dyer (1965) does not accept. According to PlantZAfrica, the species grows fast and makes a substantial plant in five to eight years. Jungle Music, a nursery in southern California, describes golden yellow female cones and paler yellow male cones, and petioles armed with long prickles, while the soft leaflets make the plant not very “pokey”.

Hybrids

Dyer (1965) reports intermediate plants in two areas where Encephalartos villosus grows alongside another species: near East London with Encephalartos altensteinii, and at the south-eastern tip of the former Transvaal, near Pongola Poort, with Encephalartos lebomboensis. He concludes that natural hybridisation there is “all but proved”, and warns that an altensteinii × villosus hybrid would key out in his key to Encephalartos natalensis or Encephalartos lebomboensis. Viljoen and van Staden (2006), who chose it as the outgroup in their genetic study of Encephalartos natalensis and Encephalartos woodii, describe it as a closely related species able to cross with Encephalartos natalensis. No hybrid raised in cultivation has been described.

Possible confusion

The best-known confusion is with Encephalartos umbeluziensis, from southern Mozambique and Eswatini, long regarded as a variety of Encephalartos villosus. Dyer (1951, 1965) separates them as follows:

  • Encephalartos villosus: a more robust plant with longer, arching leaves; glossier, larger leaflets lying in the plane of the rachis; base of the leaf armed with a row of prickles; larger cones; rather cool, moist forests;
  • Encephalartos umbeluziensis: leaves 1 to 2 m long, with a straight, smooth rachis; median leaflets 10 to 20 cm by 8 to 15 mm, arranged “like a venetian blind half open”; one or two prickles at most at the base of the petiole; smaller, sulphur-yellow cones; hot, dry forest of the Umbeluzi valley.

Dyer adds that both species grow in Eswatini without their ranges overlapping, and that no intermediate plant is known. According to Braun and Bouché (1874), Encephalartos hildebrandtii, from East Africa, differs from it in its smaller proportions and its shorter, broader leaflets. Finally, a label is not enough: in 2011, on the Italian forum Tropicamente, a collector judged that a large cycad photographed in Ghana under the name Encephalartos villosus was in fact an Encephalartos laurentianus.

Taxonomy

Charles Lemaire published the name in 1867 in L'Illustration horticole (vol. 14, Miscellanées, pp. 79–80), with a plate in volume 15 (1868); IPNI dates the publication to 1868. Lemaire stated that the plant already bore this name in the trade, under the label Zamia villosa of the Ghent nurseryman Ambroise Verschaffelt. Dyer (1965) showed that the name was not taken from Gaertner's Zamia villosa (1788), which referred to a cone of Encephalartos caffer: Lemaire's name is therefore valid. The location of the type is unknown (The World List of Cycads).

In 1890 Hennings distinguished several forms of Encephalartos villosus, among them f. intermedius, now a synonym, and f. hildebrandtii, now recognised as the species Encephalartos hildebrandtii. Encephalartos striatus, described by Stapf and Burtt Davy in 1926, is also a synonym. Conversely, Encephalartos umbeluziensis, described by Dyer in 1951, has remained a distinct species despite its reduction to a variety of Encephalartos villosus in the Flora Zambesiaca, which Dyer (1965) refuted.

Etymology and history

The epithet comes from the Latin villosus, “covered with soft hairs”, in reference to the densely woolly crown and young leaves (Haynes, 2022, via The World List of Cycads).

The history of the species is tied to the horticultural trade. Dyer (1965) traced the register of M.J. McKen, curator of the Durban botanic garden from 1851 until his death: from 1865 to 1872 McKen shipped 670 Encephalartos from Natal all over the world. On 22 February 1867 he sent 25 plants collected on the Umtwalumi, about 50 miles (80 km) south of Durban, to Ambroise Verschaffelt in Ghent; Dyer considers it very likely that this consignment, or an earlier one, contained the plants Lemaire described the same year. McKen himself first used the name villosus in 1869, for four plants sent to Melbourne. Dyer (1965) and PlantZAfrica both recall that the species was exported in large numbers in the nineteenth century and can be found in many European glasshouses.

In Italy, the 1897 catalogue of plants grown outdoors in Thomas Hanbury's garden at La Mortola, near Ventimiglia, lists it with a reference to plate 6654 of Curtis's Botanical Magazine (1882), and the Hortus Mortolensis of 1912 mentions it again. In 1977 its leaves were the subject of a physics study published in Science on pyroelectricity in plant tissues (Lang and Athenstaedt, 1977).

Common names

In English, PlantZAfrica and Species+ use “poor man's cycad”: according to PlantZAfrica, the species is one of the commonest cycads in cultivation in southern Africa and does not sell at as high a price as the rarer species. No name in a local language is attested in a verifiable source.

In the wild

Encephalartos villosus grows from around East London, in the Eastern Cape, through the former Transkei and KwaZulu-Natal as far as Eswatini (PlantZAfrica), which makes it by far the most widespread Encephalartos in southern Africa. Dyer (1965) associates it with low forest in temperate situations; near East London it sometimes grows alongside Encephalartos altensteinii, and near Pongola Poort alongside Encephalartos lebomboensis. In Eswatini it seeks relatively high, cool and moist areas. The Flora of Mozambique reports a record for Maputo district, but recalls that, according to Goode (1989), the species stops “almost at the north-eastern border of Swaziland with Mozambique”.

It is a forest understorey species (Donaldson, 1997), receiving 900 to 1,300 mm of rain a year, in summer (PlantZAfrica). Recent studies place it in the scarp forests of KwaZulu-Natal, for example in the Umtamvuna, Vernon Crookes and Oribi Gorge reserves and in Hlathikhulu forest, in the Lebombo Mountains, on poor, sandy, acidic soils (Ndabankulu et al., 2025). Its lifespan is estimated at about 150 years (Raimondo and Donaldson, 2003, cited by Sadiki et al., 2024). At Oribi Gorge, plants are taller in closed habitats than in open ones, and their size increases with altitude (Sadiki et al., 2024).

Its pollination is one of the best studied in the genus. Through five years of observations and experiments, Donaldson (1997) showed that excluding wind reduces ovule fertilisation little, whereas excluding insects reduces it sharply: the main pollinator is a small undescribed weevil of the genus Porthetes, while the beetle Antliarhinus zamiae, which lays its eggs in the ovules, contributes little to pollination and behaves rather as a parasite. Suinyuy, Donaldson and Johnson (2012) then analysed the scent of the cones in ten populations: northern populations emit mainly unsaturated hydrocarbons, southern ones nitrogen-containing compounds and terpenoids, without any change in the pollinating insects. The red seeds attract birds such as the purple-crested turaco (lourie) and the trumpeter hornbill; in KwaZulu-Natal, young seedlings are abundant on steep, wooded slopes, which suggests that these birds disperse them (PlantZAfrica).

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos villosus is listed as Least Concern (LC) on the IUCN Red List. The assessment, by J.D. Bösenberg, is dated 20 May 2020 and was published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, GBIF, Crossref). In 2003 the IUCN cycad action plan (Donaldson, 2003) gave it the same status, with about 100,000 plants, a slight decline, populations in protected areas and two main threats, over-collection and habitat destruction. Dyer (1965) already noted that the species regenerates well in the wild. Sadiki et al. (2024) recommend avoiding any disturbance that opens the forest canopy, as seed predation by weevils is higher there.

Regulation, according to Species+ (consulted through its public API on 26 October 2026):

  • CITES: Appendix I since 4 February 1977, like the whole genus;
  • European Union: Annex A, current list in Regulation (EU) 2026/1383 (in force since 29 June 2026); for artificially propagated plants from South Africa, a negative EU opinion covering the whole genus, issued on 3 December 2010, was lifted on 11 March 2011; a positive opinion specific to the species, limited to plants with a caudex up to 7 cm in diameter, has applied since 20 June 2011, and a new consultation on the whole genus has been open since 16 September 2026;
  • Mozambique: a negative EU opinion of 2006, EU suspensions for wild plants (2008–2017) and CITES suspensions of all exports of Zamiaceae (2012–2019, lifted by notification 2019/027), none of them still in force.

Seeds are covered in the same way as plants. Any plant offered for sale must come from cultivation and carry the CITES documents required by the countries concerned (and, in the European Union, the EU documents). These notes do not replace the official texts: check Species+ and the national authorities before any purchase or import.

Growing Encephalartos villosus

Encephalartos villosus is a shade plant and, for an Encephalartos, a fast grower. According to PlantZAfrica, it does well in cultivation provided it is placed in light or partial shade, in well-drained soil, with sufficient moisture and in frost-free conditions; it suits subtropical and warm temperate regions.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

PlantZAfrica's advice:

  • Establishment: grow young seedlings in pots until their leaves are about 1 m long before planting them out;
  • Planting: dig a hole half a metre square and deep, half fill it with well-rotted compost with a cup of bone meal and an organic fertiliser, mix, then plant so that the growing point is just above ground level;
  • Space: the plant can reach 6 m across; PlantZAfrica suggests Veltheimia bracteata, Scadoxus multiflorus subsp. katharinae, Asparagus densiflorus and Plectranthus as companions.

Jungle Music, at Encinitas near San Diego, calls it the cycad most used in South African landscaping and recommends shade or filtered light: as an understorey plant it tolerates water but not boggy soil, and it prefers a well-drained, slightly acidic topsoil. The nursery advises leaving at least a metre between it and its neighbours, and notes that some plants produce many basal suckers.

Container cultivation

PlantZAfrica advises raising young plants in pots before planting them out. On the French Fous de palmiers forum, a grower in Poland described a plant sown at the end of 2009: with its stem underground, it left the seedling stage in 2013 with three leaves, grew again in 2014 and was repotted in January 2015 into a deeper pot, to leave room for the caudex; he grew it in a fairly rich, mulched compost and brought it indoors in winter.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

BGCI PlantSearch lists 84 institutions declaring Encephalartos villosus, one of the highest figures in the genus. In the United Kingdom, Kew, the Royal Botanic Garden Edinburgh, Glasgow Botanic Gardens and RHS Garden Wisley declare it; no source says whether these plants grow under glass. In the United States, the Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida) lists nine accessions in its online catalogue, whose numbers point to 1964 (two), 1980, 1992 (two), 1995 and 1997 (three); BGCI also lists Fairchild, the Huntington, Lotusland, the Los Angeles County Arboretum, Descanso Gardens, the San Diego Botanic Garden, the San Diego Zoo, UC Berkeley, the United States Botanic Garden, the Marie Selby and Naples gardens and the Edison and Ford Winter Estates in Florida, conservatories in Chicago (Lincoln Park), Pittsburgh (Phipps) and Omaha (Lauritzen Gardens), the Matthaei Botanical Gardens at Ann Arbor and several Hawaiian gardens. In Australia, Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria grows it in an outdoor bed at Melbourne Gardens according to its living collection census, the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney lists it in its Garden Explorer, and Adelaide, Cairns and the Blue Mountains Botanic Garden at Mount Tomah declare it. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not a living plant on display today. Jungle Music usually offers it in all sizes, from seedlings to coning plants.

Its history in cultivation is long: McKen sent plants to Melbourne as early as 1869 (Dyer, 1965), and in Italy it grew outdoors at La Mortola in 1897 and 1912; the Naples botanic garden had four specimens from South Africa and Swaziland in 1993–1994 (Gaudio et al., 1997) and listed it in 2007 among the outdoor cycads of its gymnosperm area (Menale and De Luca, 2011). The Messina botanic garden also lists it.

Propagation

Seed is the usual method. Fresh seeds, cleaned of their fleshy coat with gloves, are sown in a warm, barely moist mineral compost; producing seed in cultivation requires a male and a female plant and, in the absence of the pollinating insects, hand pollination. In the wild, many seeds are damaged by the larvae of Antliarhinus zamiae: seeds collected from exposed plants should be checked. Suckers, which some plants produce (PlantZAfrica, Jungle Music), can also be detached, but no published source gives a method specific to the species. Seeds and suckers are covered by CITES Appendix I as well. For general advice, see the article on growing cycads from seed.

Pests and diseases

The best documented pest in the wild is Antliarhinus zamiae, a small beetle that lays its eggs in the ovules and whose larvae develop in the seeds (Donaldson, 1997; Sadiki et al., 2024). In cultivation, PlantZAfrica mentions scale insects, to be treated with an oil-based insecticide. As with all Encephalartos, the main risk remains rot of the roots and caudex in cold, waterlogged compost.

Cold hardiness

No measured, dated cold limit is documented for Encephalartos villosus. According to PlantZAfrica, frost burns the leaves but does not easily kill established plants; the page places it in South Africa's frost-free coastal horticultural zones. Ndabankulu et al. (2025) describe warm, frost-free winters for Hlathikhulu forest, in the Lebombo Mountains. As with other Encephalartos, survivals in cold areas often concern young plants whose caudex is underground, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil; the underground stem of this species gives it that advantage, but its large leaves remain exposed.

Mediterranean and European experience

  • South-eastern France: at Fréjus (Var), a grower showed a new flush of leaves on his Encephalartos villosus on the Fous de palmiers forum in September 2012, while judging it sensitive to cold, “of the order of −4 °C, I think” (24.8 °F), without any measurement.
  • Corsica: in 2013 a collector in Corse-du-Sud reported that his Encephalartos villosus imported from the southern hemisphere had never started growing again, whereas his Encephalartos arenarius had resumed at once: a failure linked to establishment, not to cold.
  • Italy: the species has long been grown outdoors at La Mortola (Liguria, 1897 and 1912) and in Naples (2007), but the minimum temperatures these plants went through are not known. Conversely, in December 2008 a collector in Salento ranked it on Tropicamente among the species that, in his view, have “no chance of surviving a frost”, without citing a temperature.
  • Madeira: a French grower observed in 2012 a female plant bearing thirteen cones in a garden on the island (Fous de palmiers).

For readers in comparable climates, such as coastal California or southern Australia, these observations suggest a sheltered, partly shaded position in a very free-draining soil; elsewhere, a container overwintered frost-free is safer.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

  • Australia: a plant grows in an outdoor bed at Melbourne Gardens (Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria census), without published winter data.
  • United States: Jungle Music gives no cold limit for the species; no dated, measured report was found.
  • United Kingdom: no source says whether the plants declared by Kew, Edinburgh, Glasgow and Wisley grow under glass or outdoors, and no outdoor result is documented.

No USDA hardiness zone is documented for Encephalartos villosus in a botanical source.

Toxicity and precautions

Like other cycads, Encephalartos villosus contains toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a toxin affecting the liver and the nervous system; the seeds are the most dangerous part. No study deals with the toxicity of this species in particular, but the plant must not be considered harmless. Wear gloves to clean the seeds, and keep plants and seeds out of reach of children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use specific to the species is described. PlantZAfrica presents it as an ornamental plant, excellent for landscaping shady gardens.

Frequently asked questions

Why is it called villosus?

Because its crown and young leaves are covered with dense white wool. Villosus means “covered with soft hairs” in Latin.

Why is it called the poor man's cycad?

Because it is very common in cultivation in southern Africa and sells for much less than the rare species, according to PlantZAfrica.

How can it be told from Encephalartos umbeluziensis?

By the base of the leaf. In Encephalartos villosus the lower leaflets are reduced to a row of prickles along the petiole; in Encephalartos umbeluziensis there are only one or two, or none. Encephalartos villosus is also more robust, with longer, arching leaves.

What pollinates its cones?

Mainly a small weevil of the genus Porthetes, according to Donaldson's exclusion experiments (1997). Wind plays a minor role, and the beetle Antliarhinus zamiae behaves rather as a seed parasite.

Should it be planted in full sun?

No. It is an understorey species that prefers light or partial shade, a well-drained soil and sufficient moisture.

Can it stay outdoors in winter?

Only in the mildest, most sheltered sites, and without guarantee: no measured cold limit has been published, and frost burns its leaves. In Italy it has been grown outdoors at La Mortola (1897, 1912) and in Naples (2007), and Melbourne grows it in an outdoor bed.

Is it endangered?

No, according to the IUCN, which lists it as Least Concern; the population was estimated at about 100,000 plants in 2003. Like all Encephalartos, it is nevertheless protected by CITES Appendix I.

Is it poisonous?

It should be treated as such, like all cycads, especially its seeds.
