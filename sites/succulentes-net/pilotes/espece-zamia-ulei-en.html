Zamia ulei is a stemless cycad of the lowland forests of western Amazonia, found in Bolivia, Brazil, Colombia, Ecuador and Peru. This species of the genus Zamia is recognised by its petiole bristling with straight prickles and by its large, broad leaflets, toothed towards the tip. It was described in 1907 by the German botanist Udo Dammer from a plant collected in 1901 on the upper Juruá River by the naturalist Ernst Ule, after whom it is named. The descriptions published since then disagree about its size, a sign that the species is still poorly known. The IUCN rates it Least Concern.

Key facts about Zamia ulei

  • Accepted name: Zamia ulei Dammer (1907), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and by GBIF; POWO could not be consulted directly
  • Former names and synonyms: Zamia cupatiensis Ducke (1922); Zamia ulei subsp. ulei (1935)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: no English name attested; no Portuguese or Spanish name verified
  • Natural range: Bolivia (Pando), Brazil (Acre, Amazonas, Rondônia), Colombia (Amazonas, Caquetá, Guainía, Putumayo, Vaupés), Ecuador (Napo, Pastaza, Sucumbíos), Peru (Loreto, Madre de Dios, Ucayali), according to The World List of Cycads
  • IUCN status: Least Concern (LC), 2022 assessment
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (whole genus, since 1977; seeds exempt under annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: no data (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: natural habitat poorly described (understorey of lowland forest); in cultivation, according to a French specialist nursery, a free-draining, neutral to acid compost, constant warmth and very humid air
  • Habit and adult size: underground, tuberous stem 4 to 6 cm in diameter; 1 to 4 leaves, 1 to 1.5 m long according to the Flora de Colombia, up to 4 m according to Segalla and Calonje (2019); leaflets up to 50 cm long in the original description
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Marie Selby Botanical Gardens (Florida) and the botanical garden of the University of Hawaii at Hilo declare it to BGCI; a collector with collections in California and Guatemala reports growing it; no British or Australian record
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; treat it as poisonous, like all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Zamia ulei

No visible trunk: a few leaves rise straight from the ground on a long, prickly petiole and carry broad, elliptic leaflets toothed in their upper half. The spiny petiole is the most reliable character. On size, however, the three available descriptions differ markedly, and they are best given separately.

The original description by Dammer (1907), written in Latin and German from the type collection and read in the digitised volume, gives:

  • Habit: a stemless plant with large leaves, broadly oval in outline.
  • Petiole: rounded, about 1 m long or more, covered, mainly in its lower part, with many straight, round prickles 1 to 4 mm long, standing at right angles.
  • Rachis and leaflets: rachis 30 to 45 cm long, carrying 5 to 8 pairs of almost opposite leaflets set 4 to 5 cm apart; leaflets slightly sickle-shaped, strongly narrowed towards the base, with almost parallel edges, pointed, with 12 to 15 small spiny teeth on each side in the upper part, crossed by about sixty veins, about 50 cm long and 6 cm wide.
  • Female cone: stalked, cylindrical, ending in a point, about 20 cm by 6 cm; hexagonal scales 12 mm high and 22 mm wide, densely covered with russet-brown hairs, in 9 vertical rows of 13 or 14 scales.
  • Seeds: two per scale, ovoid-oblong, obscurely three-angled, 15 mm long and 8 mm thick according to the Latin text (5 mm in the German text, probably a misprint).

The Flora de Colombia (Stevenson, 2001), based mainly on Colombian collections, describes a smaller plant:

  • Stem: underground and tuberous, narrowed at the top, 4 to 6 cm in diameter.
  • Leaves: 2 to 4 per plant, 1 to 1.5 m long, broadly oval; cylindrical petiole up to 1 m, usually armed with many prickles; rachis up to 50 cm, with 3 to 6 pairs of almost opposite leaflets, usually prickly in its lower third.
  • Leaflets: oblong-elliptic to elliptic-ovate, sometimes slightly curved, 12 to 20 cm by 6 to 10 cm (middle leaflets), not grooved, papery to almost leathery, stalkless, with a blunt, narrowed base and 12 to 15 teeth on each side in the upper half.
  • Male cones: usually 2 to 5, light brown, cylindrical, 6 to 10 cm by 1 to 2 cm, on a stalk 6 to 8 cm long.
  • Female cones: usually solitary, brown, cylindrical, 18 to 20 cm by 4 to 6 cm, on a stalk 8 to 10 cm long.
  • Seeds: red, ovoid to oblong, 15 mm by 8 mm.
  • Chromosomes: 2n = 26 (Caputo and colleagues, 1996, cited by the flora).

Segalla and Calonje (2019), who gathered field measurements from a population in Acre, Brazil (municipality of Marechal Thaumaturgo), to compare it with Zamia brasiliensis, give much larger figures for adult plants: 1 or 2 leaves per plant, up to 4 m long; 8 to 50 leaflets per leaf, 16 to 50 cm by 3 to 7 cm; a petiole armed with sturdy prickles; female cones plain red-brown when ripe, with 20 to 22 rows of scales; male cones covered in tawny felt, with 14 to 18 pollen sacs per scale arranged in two well-separated groups along the edges; sclerotesta (the hard shell of the seed) 18 to 19 mm by 8 to 10 mm.

The same authors explain that young plants have small leaves with leaflets like those of Zamia brasiliensis, whereas adults produce much larger leaves and leaflets. This change with age, added to the small number of studies on Amazonian Zamia, probably explains part of the differences between descriptions; none of the sources consulted says so explicitly. The 50 cm leaflets of the original description are, in any case, closer to the 2019 measurements than to those of the Flora de Colombia.

Hybrids

No hybrid, natural or raised in cultivation, has been documented.

Possible confusion

The spiny petiole is enough to rule out the two neighbouring species of southern Amazonia and the Cerrado, which have a smooth petiole. Segalla and Calonje (2019) give a simple key: petiole armed, Zamia ulei; petiole smooth and leaflets 2.4 to 3.5 cm wide, Zamia brasiliensis; petiole smooth and leaflets 0.8 to 2 cm wide, Zamia boliviana. They add:

  • Zamia brasiliensis, from Mato Grosso and Rondônia: leaves 1.12 m long at most, 10 to 24 leaflets; its herbarium specimens were often taken for young Zamia ulei, whose leaflet size and shape they share, but the smooth petiole separates them even in young plants;
  • Zamia boliviana, from the Cerrado of Brazil and Bolivia: leaves 1.19 m long at most, 16 to 40 narrow leaflets.

The ranges of the three species are close but do not overlap. Within Amazonia itself, the distinction is trickier. Zamia amazonum also has an underground stem; according to the Flora de Colombia, it differs in its longer leaves (up to 2.5 m), lanceolate leaflets and small female cones. Zamia lecointei was attached to Zamia ulei as a subspecies by Adolpho Ducke in 1935 (see Taxonomy). The Flora de Colombia also compares Zamia ulei with Zamia obliqua, which has asymmetric, oblique leaflets and becomes tree-like when adult.

Taxonomy

Zamia ulei Dammer is a name accepted by The World List of Cycads and by GBIF; POWO, the site’s nomenclatural authority, could not be consulted directly. The species was published by Udo Dammer in the Verhandlungen des Botanischen Vereins der Provinz Brandenburg, 48th year (1906), pages 117–118, in the second part of the “Beiträge zur Flora der Hylaea nach den Sammlungen von Ule’s Amazonas-Expedition”, edited by Ernst Ule; this part appeared on 8 March 1907. The Flora de Colombia cites volume 47 in error: the first part of the series had appeared in that volume, as a note in volume 48 points out.

The original description cites a single collection: Ule 5523, on the upper Juruá River near “Cachoeira”, in Amazonas (Brazil), dated May 1901 by The World List of Cycads. The sources disagree on the status of the Hamburg specimen (HBG): The World List of Cycads gives it as the holotype, with isotypes in Leiden (L), Geneva (G), Belém (MG) and Kew (K); the Flora de Colombia presents it as a lectotype chosen by Stevenson and Sabato in 1986, with isolectotypes at F (fragment), G, K, L and MG. Neither source explains the discrepancy.

The main synonym is Zamia cupatiensis, described by Adolpho Ducke in 1922 in the Archivos do Jardim Botânico do Rio de Janeiro (3: 20, plate 1) and also lectotypified by Stevenson and Sabato (1986). In 1935 Ducke reduced his own Zamia lecointei to a subspecies of Zamia ulei, which automatically created the name Zamia ulei subsp. ulei, now a synonym. According to the Flora de Colombia, Ducke’s idea of Zamia ulei did not match the type; Zamia lecointei is once again treated as a species.

More broadly, Segalla and Calonje (2019) stress that the Zamia of the Amazon basin remain poorly understood: the limits of most accepted species are unclear, and several populations may represent undescribed species. The transcriptome-based study by Lindstrom and colleagues (2024), which sampled a plant from Acre, places the species in clade VII, that of the Zamia of the eastern Andes and northern Colombia: Zamia ulei forms a pair with Zamia amazonum, sister to a group made up of Zamia muricata, Zamia lecointei and Zamia orinoquiensis.

Etymology and history

The epithet honours Ernst Heinrich Georg Ule (1854–1915), a German plant collector who carried out long research in Amazonia and was the first to collect the species, in Brazil (The World List of Cycads, after Haynes, 2022). The type collection bears number 5523 of his Amazon expedition, whose plants were described by several Berlin botanists; Dammer dealt with the cycads and palms.

Dammer also reports, from what Ule told him, that the species grew elsewhere than on the upper Juruá: as far as “Boca de Moa” and “Jurimaguas” (Yurimaguas, in Peru), near Tarapoto at “St. Antonio”, where it grew alongside Zamia lindenii, and on the Huallaga near “Shapaga”. These place names are read from a text digitised by optical character recognition; some remain uncertain, and the sources consulted do not confirm these localities with herbarium collections.

Common names

No English name is attested, and no Portuguese or Spanish name has been verified for the species.

In the wild

Zamia ulei is widely distributed in the Amazon basin (Segalla and Calonje, 2019). The World List of Cycads records it in five countries: Bolivia (Pando), Brazil (Acre, Amazonas, Rondônia), Colombia (Amazonas, Caquetá, Guainía, Putumayo, Vaupés), Ecuador (Napo, Pastaza, Sucumbíos) and Peru (Loreto, Madre de Dios, Ucayali). In 2001 the Flora de Colombia knew it in Colombia only from the departments of Amazonas and Guainía, and also gave it for eastern Peru and western Brazil, with an isolated population north of the Amazon, in the state of Pará, which The World List of Cycads does not mention.

According to the same flora, the species is relatively common, in scattered populations throughout its range, and seed production and seedling establishment appear to be abundant. The sources consulted describe neither the habitat nor the altitude precisely; pollinators and phenology have not been documented for this species.

To give an idea of the climate, Simon Lavaud, of the specialist nursery Cycadales.eu, quotes that of Iquitos, in Peru, where several Zamia grow, including Zamia hymenophyllidia, Zamia macrochiera and Zamia ulei: night-time minima of 23 °C (73.4 °F), daytime maxima of 30 °C (86 °F), 2,900 mm of rain a year and air humidity of 88 to 91 % (Lavaud, 2023).

Conservation status and regulation

Zamia ulei is rated Least Concern (LC) on the IUCN Red List, in an assessment by C. López-Gallego and M. Calonje published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads; GBIF gives the same category; the Red List itself could not be consulted). This fits the Flora de Colombia, which already described it as relatively common.

Like all Zamia except Zamia restrepoi, Zamia ulei falls under CITES Appendix II, where the genus has been listed since 4 February 1977. International trade in the plants is therefore allowed, but requires an export permit issued by the country of origin. The listing carries annotation #4: seeds, pollen, seedlings and tissue cultures obtained in vitro and transported in sterile containers are not covered by CITES. In the European Union the species falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, whose current list is set by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026.

Species+ has no separate entry for this species: it is covered by the listing “Zamia spp.” (CITES and EU status checked on Species+ on 3 October 2026). This general information does not replace the official texts: check the regulations in force before buying or exchanging plants, and ask the seller for proof of origin.

Growing Zamia ulei

No botanic garden has published growing advice for the species. Simon Lavaud, of the specialist nursery Cycadales.eu in Montpellier (southern France), places Zamia ulei in the group of lowland forest Zamia of Central and South America, which he rates “difficult” outside the tropics (Lavaud, 2023). For this group he recommends:

  • a free-draining, neutral to acid compost, and neutral to acid water;
  • at night, about 22 °C (71.6 °F), with brief dips to about 15 °C (59 °F) tolerated;
  • by day, 28 to 30 °C (82.4 to 86 °F), up to 34–35 °C (93.2–95 °F) for most species, with possible damage to young leaves during heat peaks;
  • high air humidity, 70 to 90 %, for best growth.

According to the same source, European climates are too cool and too dry for these species, even in summer (in Montpellier, July and August nights are around 19 °C, or 66.2 °F), and a glasshouse, a conservatory or a bright, heated room are the recommended solutions; keeping the compost dry in winter, which protects species from seasonally dry climates against rot, works less well for those of the humid tropics. This is advice from experience for a group of species, not measurements made on Zamia ulei. The leaves, which can reach 4 m in adults according to Segalla and Calonje (2019), also need a great deal of room.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

No outdoor cultivation outside the tropics has been documented. In its range the species lives in a hot, very wet equatorial climate, the only kind in which planting out can be considered; this is a deduction from the climate of its habitat, not a tested result.

Container cultivation

Jay Vannini (2019), a collector who grows Mesoamerican and South American cycads in the United States (California) and in Guatemala, counts the upper Amazonian Zamia ulei, like Zamia dressleri, among the “sometimes challenging” underground-stemmed rainforest cycads. To improve their vigour he tried, over the previous fifteen years, a bacterial inoculant (Streptomyces lydicus) in California and commercial mycorrhizal fungi in Guatemala, with inconclusive results: the products did no apparent harm, but he saw no tangible benefit. In practice, a cool-climate grower should plan for a heated glasshouse or a warm room all year, in a large container, with compost that never dries out completely.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In short: very rare in collections; two American gardens declare it to BGCI; no British or Australian record.

  • United States: Marie Selby Botanical Gardens (Sarasota, Florida) and the Botanical Garden at the University of Hawaii at Hilo declare it to BGCI (queried on 24 October 2026). The species is not in the online catalogue of Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida), consulted the same day. Jay Vannini (2019) reports growing it, in trials made in his collections in California and Guatemala.
  • Australia: no record in BGCI or in the online catalogues of Australian gardens included in the inventory compiled on 19 October 2026.
  • United Kingdom: no record in BGCI or in the inventory of British garden catalogues (Edinburgh, Cambridge, Chelsea Physic Garden).

A BGCI declaration shows that a garden held the plant at some point, not that it is alive today, and the sources do not say how these plants are grown.

Propagation

No propagation data specific to the species exist from cultivation. Like all Zamia, it has male and female plants and is propagated from fresh seed after pollination; in the wild, according to the Flora de Colombia, seed production and seedling establishment are high. Seeds are exempt from CITES under annotation #4, but they must come from cultivated plants of legal origin.

Pests and diseases

No pest or disease specific to the species has been documented. For the group of humid-tropical species, Lavaud (2023) mainly warns of root rot when plants are kept too cool; scale insects are, as in all cycads, the commonest pest in cultivation.

Cold hardiness

There are no data on the cold tolerance of Zamia ulei, either in the wild or in cultivation. The climate of Iquitos quoted by Cycadales.eu (minima around 23 °C, or 73.4 °F) and its advice for the lowland group (nights around 22 °C, or 71.6 °F, with brief dips to about 15 °C, or 59 °F) describe the conditions for healthy growth, not a survival limit.

Mediterranean and European experience

No in-ground cultivation of Zamia ulei has been reported in France, and no dated frost episode concerns it. The Jardin zoologique tropical at La Londe-les-Maures, in the Var, has published no observation of this species. The only reference point for the genus there, a Zamia integrifolia from Florida that survived −7 °C (19.4 °F) in February 2012 with damage to its foliage, cannot be transferred to an Amazonian species that has never been tested in cold conditions. Even in a Mediterranean climate, then, Zamia ulei is a plant for a warm glasshouse.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No cold record has been found for the species, on forums or in botanic gardens.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity study specific to Zamia ulei has been found. The risk is, however, documented in the genus and in other cycads: Zamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, and for Zamia furfuracea the ASPCA cites cycasin and BMAA, a neurotoxic amino acid, with vomiting, bloody diarrhoea and liver damage in dogs, cats and horses; the seeds are the most dangerous part. In the absence of evidence to the contrary, treat the plant as poisonous: keep seeds and leaves out of reach of children and animals, and wear gloves to clean seeds. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet straight away.

Traditional uses

No traditional use specific to Zamia ulei is documented in the sources consulted.

Frequently asked questions

How can I tell Zamia ulei from Zamia brasiliensis and Zamia boliviana?

By the petiole. That of Zamia ulei is armed with sturdy prickles, even in young plants; that of the other two species is smooth. As an adult, Zamia ulei is also much larger.

How big are its leaves?

It depends on the source. The Flora de Colombia gives 1 to 1.5 m and 3 to 6 pairs of leaflets; Segalla and Calonje (2019), on plants from Acre, up to 4 m and 50 leaflets; the original description, leaflets about 50 cm long. Young plants have much smaller leaves.

Do young leaves differ from adult ones?

Yes, in size. According to Segalla and Calonje (2019), young plants have small leaves with leaflets like those of Zamia brasiliensis, and adults much larger leaves and leaflets.

Where does it grow?

In western Amazonia. The World List of Cycads records it in Bolivia, Brazil, Colombia, Ecuador and Peru; the Flora de Colombia also mentioned an isolated population in Pará, Brazil.

Is it endangered?

No. The IUCN rates it Least Concern (2022), and the Flora de Colombia described it as relatively common across its vast range.

Can I grow it in the UK?

Only in a warm glasshouse or a heated room, on the information available. A specialist nursery places it among the difficult Zamia of the humid tropics, which need nights around 22 °C (71.6 °F) and very humid air, and a collector describes it as sometimes challenging.

Is it poisonous?

It must be assumed so. No analysis concerns the species, but the cycads studied contain serious toxins, especially in their seeds.
