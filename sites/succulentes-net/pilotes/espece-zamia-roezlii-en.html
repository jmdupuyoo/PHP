Zamia roezlii is the largest Zamia in South America: a tree-like cycad whose trunk can exceed 5 m and whose leaves reach 3 m, native to the Pacific coastal plains of Colombia and north-western Ecuador. This species of the genus Zamia, described in 1873 in the catalogue of Jean Linden’s horticultural firm in Brussels, lives in very wet forests and even in swamps on the edge of mangroves, where some plants can be flooded at high tide. The IUCN rates it Least Concern. Outside the tropics it can only be grown in warmth all year round.

Key facts about Zamia roezlii

  • Accepted name: Zamia roezlii Linden (1873), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and by GBIF; the World Checklist of Vascular Plants (WCVP), on which POWO is based, also accepts it (consulted through GBIF on 31 October 2026)
  • Former names and synonyms: Aulacophyllum roezlii (Linden) Regel (1876); the name was first published with the spelling “Zamia roezlei”
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: no English name attested; “chigua” in Spanish (Species+; Jones, 1993), a name that is also the epithet of another species
  • Natural range: Pacific coast of Colombia (Chocó, Valle del Cauca, Nariño) and north-western Ecuador (Esmeraldas, Imbabura), from sea level to about 300 m
  • IUCN status: Least Concern (LC), 2022 assessment
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (seeds exempt under annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: no measurement; described as very sensitive to frost and drought (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, equatorial forests and coastal swamps, on brackish mud according to one monograph; in cultivation, a free-draining, neutral to acid compost that is always slightly moist
  • Habit and adult size: upright trunk 5 to 7 m tall; leaves 1 to 3 m long; female cones among the largest in the genus
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Montgomery Botanical Center (Florida) holds five accessions; Colombian plants were grown at Fairchild in the 1990s; several American gardens, the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney, Noosa and Cairns declare it to BGCI; no British record
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; treat it as poisonous, like all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Zamia roezlii

An upright trunk several metres tall, very long leaves and narrow, sickle-shaped, deeply grooved leaflets are enough to tell it from the other Zamia of its region. The Flora de Colombia presents it as the largest Zamia in South America, easy to recognise by the size of its trunk, leaves and cones and by its sickle-shaped, linear, entire leaflets. The measurements, however, vary a great deal from one source to another:

  • Trunk: unbranched, upright or reclining, up to 7 m long and 15 cm in diameter (Jones, 1993, as cited by The Gymnosperm Database); up to 7 m tall (Flora de Colombia); stiff and upright up to 5 m and 30 cm in diameter, sometimes lying down after a fall (Dodson, 1998).
  • Leaves: 5 to 10, 1 to 3 m long (Flora de Colombia); 6 to 20, 2 to 3 m long, arching, bright green (Jones); 15 to 45 on adult plants (Dodson). Young leaves emerge pale green and hairless (Jones).
  • Petiole: 30 to 50 cm long, swollen at the base, shortly hairy and densely covered in prickles (Jones); 0.5 to 1 m, with scattered to dense prickles (Flora de Colombia); the rachis also carries a few prickles.
  • Leaflets: 10 to 20 pairs (Flora de Colombia) or 20 to 54 in all (Jones), stalkless, linear to lanceolate, slightly sickle-shaped, 30 to 40 cm long (Jones) or 30 to 50 cm (Flora de Colombia), 3 to 4 cm wide (Jones), with an entire margin and a pointed tip; the upper surface, dark glossy green, is deeply grooved between the veins, which makes it look pleated; the paler lower surface has raised veins carrying scattered red-brown hairs no more than 0.5 mm long (Dodson).
  • Male cones: cylindrical and upright; 30 to 50 cm by 4 to 6 cm, brown and shortly hairy (Jones); 20 to 30 cm by 4 to 6 cm, yellowish to light brown (Flora de Colombia); fertile part up to 40 cm by 6 cm, pinkish grey, on a straight stalk up to 30 cm long (Dodson).
  • Female cones: cylindrical to barrel-shaped, brown to red-brown; 35 to 55 cm by 10 to 14 cm (Jones), 30 to 60 cm by 10 to 20 cm (Flora de Colombia), up to 90 cm when ripe (Dodson). According to Jones, they are the largest in the genus.
  • Seeds: ovoid, with red, scarlet or orange-red flesh (sarcotesta); 3.5 to 4 cm by 1.7 to 2.5 cm according to Jones, up to 3.3 cm by 1.5 cm according to Dodson.

No recent revision has reconciled these figures, which probably come from plants of different ages and regions (Colombia for the Flora de Colombia, Ecuador for Dodson). The leaflet width of 12 to 15 cm given by the Flora de Colombia, incompatible with the other sources, is not used here. The same flora cites chromosome numbers of 2n = 22, 24, 25 and 26 (Norstog, 1981).

Hybrids

No hybrid of Zamia roezlii is documented. Calaway Dodson, who described Zamia gentryi, found no sign of hybridisation where the two species grow together, between 300 and 400 m, on the road from Lita to San Lorenzo (Esmeraldas, Ecuador) (Dodson, 1998).

Possible confusion

The most likely confusion is with Zamia gentryi, the closest species according to Dodson (1998), which lives in north-western Ecuador between 300 and 1,800 m and meets Zamia roezlii at the edge of its populations. Dodson notes the following differences:

  • Habit: a stiff, upright trunk up to 5 m in Zamia roezlii; a short stem lying on the ground, or U-shaped when the plant grows as an epiphyte, in Zamia gentryi;
  • Leaves: 15 to 45 on adults of Zamia roezlii, 5 to 13 in Zamia gentryi;
  • Leaflets: dark green above, with raised, hairy veins beneath, in Zamia roezlii; pale yellow-green on both sides, with veins sunk in the blade and hairless, in Zamia gentryi;
  • Male cones: pinkish grey and upright in Zamia roezlii; wine-red, on a reclining stalk, in Zamia gentryi;
  • Female cones: up to 90 cm in Zamia roezlii, no more than 30 cm in Zamia gentryi.

A member of the French forum Fous de palmiers who saw both species in their habitat, in Ecuador, reported in September 2012 that in young plants the difference lies mainly in the depth of the grooves of the leaflets, and that in coning plants the male cones, white in Zamia roezlii and red in Zamia gentryi, separate them.

According to Jones (1993), as cited by The Gymnosperm Database, Zamia roezlii also resembles the Colombian Zamia chigua, but has grooved leaflets that are fewer and much more widely spaced. The name “chigua”, which Species+ assigns to Zamia roezlii, is also the epithet of Zamia chigua: a vernacular name is therefore not enough to identify a plant.

Colombia, Panama and Ecuador are home to other Zamia with pleated leaflets, such as Zamia amplifolia, Zamia wallisii, Zamia dressleri and Zamia neurophyllidia (see Taxonomy). Faced with a plant of unknown origin, identification rests on all the characters of the trunk, leaflets and cones together.

Taxonomy

The World List of Cycads and GBIF accept the name Zamia roezlii, which The World List of Cycads attributes to Jean Linden; the World Checklist of Vascular Plants (WCVP), on which POWO is based, also accepts it (consulted through GBIF on 31 October 2026). The species was published in 1873 in catalogue no. 90 of the Linden establishment in Brussels, page 10, as “Zamia roezlei”: “a large and magnificent species with very long fronds, up to 3 metres, narrow, very long, sickle-shaped, deeply channelled pinnae. Native to the coasts of the Pacific Ocean, where it was discovered by Mr Roezl.” The same year, the Illustration horticole (volume 20) devoted plates 133 and 134 to it, which The World List of Cycads attributes to Édouard André and The Gymnosperm Database to Eduard Regel.

The typification has been debated. The Flora de Colombia gives as neotype the plates of the Illustration horticole, designated by Stevenson and Sabato in 1986. The World List of Cycads, which cites a work by Lindström (2009) for the typification, regards instead as holotype a sterile leaf specimen collected by Roezl in New Granada (present-day Colombia) in April 1872 and kept at Kew (K), and states that the plates are not the current type.

In 1876 Regel moved the species into the genus Aulacophyllum, as Aulacophyllum roezlii (Gartenflora 25: 141), now a synonym. Regel grouped in that genus the Zamia with pleated leaflets that produce their leaves one at a time rather than in flushes; according to the Flora de Colombia, no shared derived character confirms that Aulacophyllum is a genus distinct from Zamia (Sabato, 1990). The published sources do not say which other species Regel himself placed in the genus.

The Flora de Colombia places Zamia roezlii in a species complex centred on the Chocó biogeographic province, with Zamia dressleri, Zamia neurophyllidia and the “Zamia skinneri” of Panama (in the broad sense used before the 2008 revision), Zamia wallisii from northern Antioquia and Zamia amplifolia. According to the molecular tree of Caputo and colleagues (2004), cited by Taylor and colleagues (2008), Zamia roezlii and Zamia wallisii would be sister species, and pleated leaflets would have arisen at least twice in the genus. The study by Lindstrom and colleagues (2024), which analysed a plant from Nariño, places the species in the clade of the western Andes and cites it as an example of a species living in coastal swamps.

Etymology and history

The epithet honours Benedikt Roezl (1824–1885), a Czech plant collector who travelled through Mexico, Central America and South America in the second half of the 19th century and was the first to collect this species (Haynes, 2022, as given by The World List of Cycads; Flora de Colombia). The original spelling “roezlei” has been corrected to “roezlii”, as the nomenclatural note of The World List of Cycads indicates.

The plant reached Europe as a commercial novelty: the Linden catalogue of 1873 offered it as seedlings 30 cm tall, at 25 francs, or as trunks, sold for 100 to 500 francs.

Common names

No English name is attested. Species+ and Jones (1993) give the Spanish name “chigua”, which, as seen above, is also the epithet of another Colombian species.

In the wild

According to The World List of Cycads, Zamia roezlii grows in Colombia, in the departments of Chocó, Valle del Cauca and Nariño, and in Ecuador, in the provinces of Esmeraldas and Imbabura. Dodson (1998) gives it from the Colombian coast to north-western Ecuador, from sea level to 300 m in Ecuador; the herbarium specimens cited by the Flora de Colombia were collected at sea level or between 50 and 100 m. The Flora de Colombia also cites a specimen from Colombian Amazonia (department of Amazonas, 200 m), which The World List of Cycads does not record; Species+ also lists Panama.

Jones (1993), as cited by The Gymnosperm Database, describes its habitat: equatorial forests and mangrove swamp forests of the Pacific coastal plains, usually a little above sea level; some plants are even flooded at high tide, and the soil has been described as brackish mud. Gutiérrez-Ortega and Villarreal (2024) cite its ability to live in the swamps and mangroves of Colombia among the remarkable traits of some species of the genus.

Where it grows alongside Zamia gentryi, the cones of both species appear in December, with the start of the heavy rains; male cones ripen in about 90 days, female cones become receptive about 90 days after they appear, and the seeds ripen in about a year (Dodson, 1998). Jones, on the other hand, states that in this equatorial climate the plants produce cones all year round, with no seasonal cycle. Dodson noticed no scent from the cones and saw no beetle visiting the male cones of the plants grown at the Río Palenque scientific centre. Jones adds that the sperm cells of this species are the largest known in cycads.

Conservation status and regulation

Zamia roezlii is rated Least Concern (LC) on the IUCN Red List, in an assessment by C. López-Gallego and M. Calonje published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads); GBIF gives the same category. The Flora de Colombia describes it as locally abundant, with large female cones and good production of seeds and seedlings, but warns that habitat destruction leaves the populations very vulnerable, since tree-like plants establish poorly in disturbed areas.

Like all Zamia except Zamia restrepoi, Zamia roezlii falls under CITES Appendix II, where the genus has been listed since 4 February 1977. The listing carries annotation #4 (version from CoP20, in force on 5 March 2026): seeds, pollen, seedlings and tissue cultures obtained in vitro and transported in sterile containers, as well as cut flowers of artificially propagated plants, are not covered by CITES. International trade in the plants themselves, on the other hand, requires an export permit. In the European Union the species falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, whose current annexes are set by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026. Species+ names the species and confirms this status (consulted on 13 October 2026).

This general information does not replace the official texts: check the regulations in force before buying or exchanging plants, ask the seller for proof of origin and prefer plants raised from seed in cultivation.

Growing Zamia roezlii

Jones (1993) states that the species is grown from seed and is very sensitive to frost and drought. Simon Lavaud, of the specialist nursery Cycadales.eu in Montpellier (southern France), places it among the lowland forest Zamia of Central and South America, which he considers difficult to grow outside the tropics: European climates are too cool and too dry for them, even in summer, and he recommends a glasshouse, a conservatory, a bright heated room or even a growth chamber (Lavaud, 2023). For this group he gives:

  • a free-draining, neutral to acid compost, and neutral to acid water;
  • at night, 22 °C (71.6 °F), with brief dips to about 15 °C (59 °F) tolerated;
  • by day, 28 to 30 °C (82.4 to 86 °F), up to 34–35 °C (93.2–95 °F) for most species, with possible damage to young leaves during heat peaks if the air is dry;
  • air humidity of 70 to 90 % for best growth.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

No outdoor cultivation outside the tropics has been documented. In its native range the species lives in a hot, very wet equatorial climate; a frost-free, humid tropical climate is the only one in which in-ground cultivation can be considered, which is a deduction from its habitat rather than a tested result.

Container cultivation

The most detailed growing report found comes from the Fous de palmiers forum: a grower based in Poland followed, from 2012 to 2015, a plant received in 2009 as a germinated seed. Kept for two years in front of a west-facing window, in a heated room, and watered almost daily, it had after two and a half years an abundant root system and stood 1.40 m tall including the pot. In his view the plant does not suffer from the dry air of a flat, but the compost must always stay at least slightly moist; he used soft water, sometimes from his aquarium, and little fertiliser. In summer the plant moved to a conservatory, almost always in shade, then outdoors under an apple tree, and came back in at the start of autumn so as not to interrupt its growth; in September 2014 its stem, still bulb-shaped, was 11 cm across. He considered it fast-growing and “not too fussy”. For a British grower, this suggests a heated conservatory or a bright, warm room all year, with at most a summer spell outdoors in shade during the warmest months.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In short: present in several American collections and declared by three Australian gardens; no British record.

  • United States: the online catalogue of Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida) lists five accessions, one from 1994 and four from 2003 according to their accession numbers (consulted on 24 October 2026). Dodson (1998) mentions plants of Colombian origin, from around Buenaventura, grown at Fairchild Tropical Garden (Florida). The Huntington (San Marino, California), San Diego Botanic Garden (Encinitas, California), the Missouri Botanical Garden (St Louis), Naples Botanical Garden, the Edison and Ford Winter Estates (Fort Myers, Florida), Phipps Conservatory (Pittsburgh), the National Tropical Botanical Garden (Kalāheo, Hawaii), Waimea Valley (Oahu), the Botanical Garden at the University of Hawaii at Hilo and the Four Seasons Resort on Lāna‘i declare it to BGCI.
  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney (online catalogue and BGCI), Noosa Botanic Gardens and the Cairns Botanic Gardens (Queensland) declare it to BGCI.
  • United Kingdom: no record in BGCI or in the online catalogues of British gardens (Edinburgh, Cambridge, Chelsea Physic Garden) checked on 19 October 2026.

A BGCI declaration shows that a garden held the plant at some point, not that it is alive today, and the sources do not say whether these plants grow outdoors or under glass.

Propagation

Like all Zamia, the species has male and female plants and is propagated from seed (Jones, 1993); a plant of each sex is needed to obtain seeds in cultivation. No germination data specific to the species have been published. Seeds are exempt from CITES permits; make sure they come from cultivated plants.

Pests and diseases

No pest or disease specific to the species is documented. For its group, the nursery Cycadales.eu notes that in poor conditions, too cool and too dry, plants grow little, are weak and are at risk of root rot, and that reducing watering in winter, a useful remedy for species from climates with a dry season, works poorly for those from the humid tropics (Lavaud, 2023). Cultivated Zamia can also host scale insects.

Cold hardiness

There is no measurement of cold tolerance for Zamia roezlii. Jones (1993) describes it as very sensitive to frost, and the nursery Cycadales.eu gives for its group a night temperature of 22 °C (71.6 °F), with brief dips to about 15 °C (59 °F) tolerated (Lavaud, 2023).

Mediterranean and European experience

No result of in-ground cultivation has been published in France, and no dated frost episode concerns the species. Simon Lavaud notes that in Montpellier, even in July and August, night-time minima are around 19 °C (66.2 °F), at the limit for the species of this group, and that the air there is much drier than in their habitat (Lavaud, 2023). Even in a Mediterranean climate, then, Zamia roezlii is a plant for a warm glasshouse or a heated room all year, with at most a summer spell outdoors in shade during the hottest months, as the grower in Poland did. The Jardin zoologique tropical at La Londe-les-Maures, in the Var, has published no observation of this species.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No cold record has been published for the species, on forums or in botanic gardens. Dodson (1998) mentions plants grown at the Río Palenque scientific centre in Ecuador and at Fairchild Tropical Garden in Florida, without temperature data.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity study specific to Zamia roezlii has been published. The risk is, however, well documented in cycads: Zamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, and for Zamia furfuracea the ASPCA cites cycasin and BMAA, a neurotoxic amino acid, with vomiting, bloody diarrhoea and liver damage in dogs, cats and horses; the seeds are the most dangerous part. In the absence of evidence to the contrary, treat the plant as poisonous: keep seeds and leaves out of reach of children and animals, and wear gloves to clean seeds. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet straight away.

Traditional uses

According to Jones (1993), as cited by The Gymnosperm Database, the large ripe seeds are an important source of food for local people, who pound them into flour and wash it repeatedly in water to remove the toxic compounds. These preparations rely on local knowledge and must never be copied.

Frequently asked questions

Is Zamia roezlii the largest Zamia?

In South America, yes, according to the Flora de Colombia: its trunk reaches 5 to 7 m and its leaves up to 3 m. Jones (1993) adds that its female cones are the largest in the genus.

Where does it grow?

On the Pacific coast of Colombia and north-western Ecuador, from sea level to a few hundred metres, in very wet forests and in swamps on the edge of mangroves.

How can I tell it from Zamia gentryi?

By its upright trunk, more numerous leaves, dark green leaflets with raised, hairy veins beneath, and pale male cones; Zamia gentryi has a reclining stem and wine-red male cones (Dodson, 1998).

Is it endangered?

No, according to the IUCN (2022), which rates it Least Concern. Habitat destruction remains a risk for its populations. It falls under CITES Appendix II.

Can I grow it in the UK?

Only in warmth: a heated glasshouse or a warm room, with compost that is always slightly moist. No in-ground cultivation is documented outside the tropics; a grower in Poland succeeds with it in a flat.

Is it hardy?

No. It is described as very sensitive to frost and drought; a specialist nursery recommends nights around 22 °C (71.6 °F) for its group.

Is it poisonous?

It must be assumed so. No analysis exists for the species, but the cycads studied contain serious toxins, especially in their seeds; local people eat the seeds only after repeated washing.
