Encephalartos gratus is a cycad of tropical south-eastern Africa, known from the Mulanje massif in southern Malawi and the neighbouring province of Zambézia in Mozambique, where it grows mainly in rocky ravines and along rocky river banks. This species of the genus Encephalartos usually has a short, rounded trunk, sometimes entirely underground, and large, dull dark green leaves with sickle-shaped, toothed leaflets. It was described in 1916 by David Prain, Director of the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, and is known in English as the Mulanje cycad. It is listed as Vulnerable by the IUCN, because of habitat loss and the removal of plants from the wild.

Key facts about Encephalartos gratus

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos gratus Prain (1916), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and GBIF
  • Former names and synonyms: no heterotypic synonym; Encephalartos gratus var. gratus (1938), created automatically when Encephalartos manikensis was first described as a variety of this species
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Mulanje cycad (English), according to Species+ and the Flora of Malawi; no local name was known to Prain in 1916
  • Natural range: southern Malawi (Mulanje massif) and Mozambique (Zambézia)
  • IUCN status: Vulnerable (VU), criterion A4cd, assessment by J. D. Bösenberg published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I (whole genus, since 1977); Annex A in the European Union; positive EU opinion for artificially propagated plants from South Africa with a caudex up to 7 cm in diameter, negative opinion above that size
  • Documented hardiness: no measured value published; minimum temperatures of 7.2 to 10 °C (45 to 50 °F) in its habitat according to Prain (1916); no outdoor trial with temperatures documented in Europe, the US or Australia (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, rocky ravines and river banks, often in crevices with hardly any soil, in strong sun; more rarely in savanna woodland or dense forest on rich soil; in southern California, sun or part sun on the coast and filtered light inland, in a well-drained soil (Jungle Music)
  • Habit and adult size: trunk usually 30 to 40 cm tall and 30 cm across, sometimes up to 1.25 m, mainly in male plants; leaves 1.2 to 1.8 m long with 30 to 70 pairs of leaflets (Prain, 1916; Melville, 1957)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Cambridge University Botanic Garden (accession of 1958 of wild origin); Montgomery, Fairchild, the Huntington, Lotusland and some twenty other US gardens; Brisbane, Adelaide, Melbourne, Townsville, Noosa and Hobart in Australia (BGCI)
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; treat it as poisonous like all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos gratus

Look for large, dull dark green leaves with broad, clearly sickle-shaped leaflets toothed on both margins, borne on a usually short trunk, and for seeds with a dull vermilion coat. Two reliable descriptions exist: Prain's original description of 1916, based on specimens and field notes sent to Kew from Malawi by J. B. Davy, and the more detailed account by R. Melville (1957) in his revision of the Encephalartos of Central Africa. According to Melville:

  • Trunk: rounded, or up to 120 cm tall and 60 cm in diameter, covered with leaf bases and cataphylls clothed in a flaky felt; cataphylls triangular, long-pointed, 8 to 12 cm long and 3 to 4 cm wide at the base
  • Leaves: 90 to 180 cm long and 34 to 44 cm wide, with 30 to 70 pairs of leaflets; swollen base of the petiole with a close brownish-buff felt
  • Leaflets: median leaflets lanceolate and long-pointed, 18 to 26 cm long and 23 to 35 mm wide, dull green, rigid and leathery, with a sharp tip; the upper margin bears 2 to 7 or more spreading spiny teeth, often 3 or 4 on the basal curve, the lower margin 0 to 6; 30 to 48 faint parallel veins on the underside; the lowest leaflets are reduced to three-pronged and then simple spines
  • Male cones: narrowly ovoid to cylindrical, 10 to 40 cm long and 4.5 to 10 cm across, greenish-yellow, finally reddish-brown or dull brown, on a stalk 3.5 to 19 cm long
  • Female cones: cylindrical to almost conical, 55 to 68 cm long and 15 to 20 cm across, greenish-yellow, finally brown, on a stalk 10 to 14 cm long
  • Seeds: angular-ellipsoid, 30 to 40 mm long and 14 to 20 mm across, cinnabar red

Prain's description adds that the trunk is usually very short, rounded or ellipsoid, sometimes wholly underground, 30 to 40 cm tall and 30 cm across, rarely rising more than a foot (30 cm) above the soil; in shade on rich soil it tends to become cylindrical and may reach 4 ft (about 1.2 m), most often in male plants. His leaflets, 30 to 70 pairs, are ovate-lanceolate, distinctly sickle-shaped, 17 to 25 cm long and 3 cm wide, with a very oblique base; in young plants their tip may end in four or five spines. He describes the male cones as greenish-yellow spotted with red, turning dirty brown, and stresses the dull dark green foliage, which separates the species from Encephalartos woodii (see Possible confusion).

These measurements date from 1916 and 1957. The demographic study carried out on Mount Mulanje by Bayliss and colleagues (2010), on 532 wild plants, found that the range of measurements differed from that of the published literature; the abstract of the article does not say in what way. The San Diego nursery Jungle Music describes plants in its collection with trunks that can reach about 3 m (10 ft) over many decades and leaves of 1.8 to 2.4 m (6 to 8 ft), larger than the figures of Prain and Melville, with orange-brown female cones and green male cones flecked with brown; its spines are described as fairly flexible and soft.

Hybrids

No natural hybrid of Encephalartos gratus is documented. In collections, cross-pollination with other Encephalartos remains possible: to obtain pure seed, isolate the female cones and control the origin of the pollen.

Possible confusion

Prain (1916) compared Encephalartos gratus with the other African cycads then known:

  • Encephalartos woodii, from Zululand, whose leaflets have the same size, shape and toothing: grown side by side, the two are easily told apart, because the foliage of Encephalartos woodii is bright green and glossy, that of Encephalartos gratus dark green and dull
  • Encephalartos laurentianus, of Uganda and the Congo, whose leaflets are similar in shape; the scales of the male cones, however, are quite different and come closer to those of Encephalartos barteri and Encephalartos poggei, two species with very different foliage
  • Encephalartos ferox, from Mozambique, whose leaflet base is similar but whose leaflets are much shorter, broader and not sickle-shaped; Prain, who knew it only from Bertoloni's description, credited it with black seeds

The most likely confusion today concerns Encephalartos manikensis, from the border mountains of Zimbabwe and Mozambique, which H. B. Gilliland first described in 1938 as a variety of Encephalartos gratus before raising it to species rank the following year. According to Dyer and Verdoorn (1969), Encephalartos gratus is more robust, with longer, broader median leaflets (up to 26 cm by 2.3 to 3.5 cm) bearing two to seven prickles on the upper margin, and relatively small cone scales with a distinctive dark down on the outer face; Melville (1957) also describes the exposed part of the scales as finely hairy. On young plants without cones and of unknown origin, the distinction remains difficult. Jungle Music also compares the species with Encephalartos hildebrandtii and other tropical species, which it resembles in general appearance.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos gratus is an accepted name (The World List of Cycads; GBIF). David Prain published it in 1916 in the Bulletin of Miscellaneous Information of the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew (1916: 181–182), as no. 1600 of a series of “Diagnoses africanae”. He cited collections from Nyasaland, now Malawi: those of J. Mahon from south-eastern Mulanje, between the Puchila and Ruo rivers, at 850 m; those of J. B. Davy from the lower slopes of Mount Mulanje, between 650 and 900 m; and cultivated plants at Zomba. The World List of Cycads gives as type the collection Davy 417 from “Mt Mlanji”, kept at Kew; it is a lectotype, designated by Melville in 1957 in the Kew Bulletin (12(2): 255).

The only associated name is the autonym Encephalartos gratus var. gratus, which arose in 1938 when Gilliland described Encephalartos gratus var. manikensis (Journal of South African Botany 4: 153); this variety became the species Encephalartos manikensis in 1939. No other name change is recorded.

Etymology and history

The epithet gratus comes from Latin and means “pleasing”, apparently referring to the pleasing appearance of the plant (Haynes, 2022, cited by The World List of Cycads). Prain gives no reason for the name in the original description.

The history of the species is linked with Kew. According to Prain, J. Mahon sent specimens of a male plant from Nyasaland in 1899; living plants reached Kew in 1903, sent by McClounie, and in 1914, sent by J. B. Davy. In 1916 Davy supplied copious notes, material of cones of both sexes and a series of photographs, which made a complete description possible. Davy had been unable to find any use or any definite vernacular name for the plant.

The most recent field study of the species is that of Bayliss, Burrow, Martell and Staude (2010), devoted to its relationship with the moth Callioratis grandis, whose caterpillars feed only on Encephalartos gratus (see Pests and diseases).

Common names

The English name Mulanje cycad is given by Species+, the Flora of Malawi and the Flora of Mozambique, and appears in the title of the study by Bayliss and colleagues (2010). According to Prain (1916), Davy found no definite vernacular name in the region, and no recent source gives one. Names such as “Mozambique cycad” or “pleasing cycad” that appear on some websites are not attested by these sources.

In the wild

Encephalartos gratus grows in southern Malawi, on the Mulanje massif, and in the neighbouring province of Zambézia in Mozambique (The World List of Cycads); the Flora of Malawi and the Flora of Mozambique describe it as known only from southern Malawi and Mozambique. Jungle Music places it in “northern Mozambique”, which does not match these sources. From Davy's notes, Prain described it as most common in rocky ravines and along rocky river banks, often in crevices between rocks without any apparent soil; it is usually exposed to intense sun and is often under water for a few hours during heavy floods. Occasional plants are found in savanna woodland and even in dense forest on rich soil; in savanna the plants are usually defoliated by bush fires every year.

Davy's notes also give an idea of the climate of the lower slopes of Mulanje: 1,250 to 1,750 mm of rain a year and a minimum temperature of 45 to 50 °F, that is 7.2 to 10 °C. The text also gives a “mean temperature for the year” of 95 to 100 °F (35 to 37.8 °C), a value impossible for an annual mean, which most probably refers to maximum temperatures.

No study of the pollination of the species has been published.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos gratus is listed as Vulnerable (VU). The World List of Cycads cites the IUCN Red List assessment published in 2022 (J. D. Bösenberg) under criterion A4cd: a past and future decline of at least 30 % over three generations, caused by habitat loss and exploitation. GBIF gives the same category; an earlier assessment, by J. S. Donaldson, was published in 2010. The Red List itself could not be consulted directly.

The IUCN cycad action plan (Donaldson, 2003) already listed the species as Vulnerable under the same criterion, with an estimated population of 100,000 plants, a moderate decline, no population known in a protected area and three threats: over-collection, habitat destruction and reproductive failure. It names Encephalartos gratus among the species whose populations have been reduced by clearing for agriculture in Mozambique. Bayliss and colleagues (2010) consider the species “severely threatened” at its type locality on Mulanje, through habitat destruction and the growth of the human population.

Like the whole genus, it has been listed in CITES Appendix I since 4 February 1977 and, in the European Union, in Annex A of the wildlife trade regulations (current list: Regulation (EU) 2026/1383), according to Species+. For artificially propagated plants from South Africa, Species+ records a positive opinion of the EU Scientific Review Group for plants with a caudex up to 7 cm in diameter (since 20 June 2011) and a negative opinion above that size (since 7 February 2013). Species+ also records past suspensions of trade in Zamiaceae from Mozambique, no longer in force: CITES suspended all exports from 2012 to 2019 (lifted by Notification No. 2019/027), and the European Union, through decisions taken from 2008 to 2017, imports of wild specimens. Buy only nursery-propagated plants with the required CITES documents, and check the current rules before any purchase or import.

Growing Encephalartos gratus

No growing rules specific to Encephalartos gratus appear in the primary botanical sources. Its habitat, rocks in full sun where water drains away quickly but which are sometimes flooded for a few hours, suggests a plant that tolerates water as long as it does not stagnate, in a warm, humid, frost-free climate. Jungle Music, in San Diego, calls it a fast and easy species to grow in southern California: on the coast it takes half a day of sun or full sun, inland it prefers part sun or filtered light, and in desert areas it needs filtered light or shade. The nursery describes it as tolerant of water, in a well-drained topsoil; it can stand drying out, as in the savanna, but will not then keep a full crown. It produces basal suckers, but sparingly.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

Outdoors, the species needs a frost-free climate. Jungle Music grows it in the open and recommends it as a showpiece that needs room for its large crown, but publishes no temperature. In Europe no planting in the ground is documented for the species. For readers in Mediterranean climates, the only firm data are those of its habitat, which knows no frost.

Container cultivation

A grower in Agen, south-western France, showed a new flush of leaves on an Encephalartos gratus in a container collection on the Fous de palmiers forum in June 2010; the pots are put out in the sun in the warm season. On the Italian Tropicamente forum, a collector in the Salento (Apulia) showed in October 2009 the new leaves produced over the summer by his plant, and in June 2012 another grower reported a seedling with a single leaf that had not grown for two years in a cold greenhouse. A container kept frost-free and on the dry side in winter remains the prudent choice outside the tropics.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

The species has long been grown in Britain: Kew received living plants in 1903 and 1914. In 1984 the list of rare and threatened plants in botanic gardens compiled by the IUCN Threatened Plants Unit at Kew recorded it in thirteen gardens, among them Kew and Cambridge University Botanic Garden in Britain, Fairchild Tropical Garden, the Huntington, the Foster Botanical Garden in Honolulu and the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley in the United States, and Kings Park in Perth, Western Australia. Today, Cambridge University Botanic Garden lists in its online catalogue an accession of 1958 of wild origin, and it is the only British garden that declares the species to BGCI PlantSearch.

In the United States, Montgomery Botanical Center in Coral Gables lists seven accessions in its online catalogue. Twenty-four US gardens declare the species to BGCI, among them Montgomery, Fairchild, the Huntington, Lotusland, the San Diego Botanic Garden, the San Diego Zoo, Naples Botanical Garden, the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley, the Los Angeles County Arboretum, Marie Selby Botanical Gardens, the National Tropical Botanical Garden, Honolulu, the United States Botanic Garden and the New York Botanical Garden. In Australia, seven gardens declare it: Brisbane Botanic Gardens Mount Coot-tha, the Botanic Garden of South Australia in Adelaide, the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria and the University of Melbourne System Garden, James Cook University in Townsville, Noosa Botanic Gardens and the Royal Tasmanian Botanical Gardens in Hobart. A BGCI listing shows that a garden has declared the species at some point, not that a plant is alive today; the current census of the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria does not list it. In Britain, cycads are grown almost exclusively under glass.

In Italy, the Botanic Garden of Naples grew two plants in 1993–1994, one from Malawi and one from Mozambique (Gaudio and colleagues, 1997), and the species appears in the 2009 cycad list of the Orto Botanico of Messina; in France, the Les Cèdres botanic garden at Saint-Jean-Cap-Ferrat held it in 1984. Their present survival has not been confirmed.

Propagation

Encephalartos gratus is propagated mainly from seed, which requires plants of both sexes and, in collections, hand pollination. No protocol specific to the species has been published; seedlings can be slow in cool conditions, as the Italian seedling stalled for two years in a cold greenhouse shows. Prain noted that male plants more often develop an erect trunk; Jungle Music notes that suckers are produced sparingly. Seeds, like plants, fall under CITES Appendix I.

Pests and diseases

In the wild, Encephalartos gratus is the only known food plant of the Mulanje tiger moth, Callioratis grandis. Bayliss and colleagues (2010) found caterpillar damage on about half of the 532 plants studied on Mulanje, more often on old plants, with no preference between male and female plants; leaves were sometimes severely damaged, but with no apparent effect on cone production or survival. In cultivation no disease specific to the species is described; as with other Encephalartos, the main risk is root and caudex rot in a cold, wet substrate.

Cold hardiness

No reliable measurement of the cold hardiness of Encephalartos gratus exists. In its habitat, the minimum temperatures reported by Prain are 7.2 to 10 °C (45 to 50 °F): the species does not experience frost in the localities described in 1916, which says nothing precise about its limit. As in other Encephalartos, survival in the cold often concerns young plants whose caudex is still underground, protected by the soil; the often buried trunk of this species could play that role, but no observation confirms it.

Mediterranean and European experience

No outdoor trial is documented in France or Italy. The only French report is that of a container plant in Agen, without temperatures, and the presence of the species at Les Cèdres in 1984 says nothing about how it was grown. On Tropicamente, a grower near Naples had heard in 2012 that the species was not very frost-resistant, while a grower in Ravenna had read that it was one of the most cold-tolerant of the non-South African species, but did not buy it for fear of sudden cold snaps; no temperature is given in these messages.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No dated cold report with a temperature has been published for the species. In coastal southern California, Jungle Music grows it outdoors without reporting any cold damage or minimum temperature. No USDA zone is published for the species by a botanical source; the zone ratings found on some websites cite no measurement. In Britain it is a plant for a heated glasshouse or conservatory, and in Australia the gardens that declare it do not say whether their plants are grown outdoors.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data have been published for Encephalartos gratus, and Prain knew of no use for it. The risk is nevertheless documented in the genus: the toxicity of Encephalartos horridus was demonstrated as early as 1944 (Van der Walt), and Encephalartos, like other cycads, contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; these substances affect the liver, the digestive tract and the nervous system, and the seeds are the most dangerous part. The ASPCA reports vomiting, diarrhoea, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses that have eaten cycads.

Treat the plant as poisonous: wear gloves to handle seeds and keep plants and seeds away from children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use is known. In 1916, Prain stated that no economic properties were attributed to Encephalartos gratus, and Davy had been unable to find any local use.

Frequently asked questions

Where does Encephalartos gratus grow in the wild?

In southern Malawi, on the Mulanje massif, and in the neighbouring province of Zambézia in Mozambique. It grows mostly in rocky ravines and along rivers, often in crevices with almost no soil.

What does its name mean?

“Pleasing” in Latin, apparently for its attractive appearance. In English it is called the Mulanje cycad, after the mountain where it was collected.

How can I tell it from Encephalartos woodii?

By the colour and sheen of the leaves. The leaflets are almost the same shape, but those of Encephalartos gratus are dark green and dull, those of Encephalartos woodii bright green and glossy.

How is it different from Encephalartos manikensis?

With difficulty when there are no cones. According to Dyer and Verdoorn (1969), Encephalartos gratus is more robust, with longer and broader leaflets, and its cone scales carry a dark down. The origin of the plant (Mulanje and Zambézia, or the Manica highlands for manikensis) is another clue.

Is it endangered?

It is listed as Vulnerable. In 2003 the IUCN estimated its population at about 100,000 plants, in moderate decline because of land clearing, collection and poor reproduction.

How big does it get?

Its trunk rarely exceeds 40 cm and often stays underground, but it can reach about 1.2 m. The leaves are 1.2 to 1.8 m long, longer in some cultivated plants.

Is it frost-hardy?

Nobody knows. No outdoor trial with temperatures has been published, and the species does not experience frost in its habitat. Grow it frost-free.

Can I buy one legally?

Yes, if it is a nursery-propagated plant with the required CITES documents. For artificially propagated plants from South Africa, the European Union accepts only those with a caudex up to 7 cm in diameter.
