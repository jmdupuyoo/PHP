Macrozamia montana is a cycad, usually without a visible trunk, from north-eastern New South Wales, in eastern Australia, which grows on ridges and steep slopes of the ranges of the eastern escarpment, between the Macleay and Manning river catchments. It belongs to the genus Macrozamia and was recognised as a species only in 1998: until then it was confused with Macrozamia communis, to which it remains very close. Its name recalls its mountain habitat, higher and cooler than that of its coastal relative. It is still very rarely grown, in Europe as elsewhere.

Key facts about Macrozamia montana

  • Accepted name: Macrozamia montana K.D.Hill (1998), published in the Flora of Australia 48: 717, accepted by The World List of Cycads and the Australian Plant Census
  • Former names and synonyms: no synonym; plants formerly referred to Macrozamia communis (PACSOA)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: “Mountain Burrawang” (Flora of Australia)
  • Natural range: Australia, north-eastern New South Wales, Macleay and Manning river catchments, as far as Big Hill, south-east of Wollomombi
  • IUCN status: Least Concern (LC), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated September 2020 and published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union; no listing under Australian threatened-species law (Atlas of Living Australia)
  • Documented hardiness: no measured value and no documented cultivation report (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, wet sclerophyll forest under eucalypts, on well-drained soils, from about 200 m to over 750 m (PACSOA); in cultivation, not documented
  • Habit and adult size: underground caudex, sometimes a short trunk up to 0.6 m, 25 to 45 cm across; 20 to 70 leaves 1 to 2 m long (up to 2.4 m observed)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: declared to BGCI by 13 institutions, including the National Botanic Garden of Wales, Lotusland and the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; seeds and young leaves toxic throughout the genus (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Macrozamia montana

It looks like a Macrozamia communis, but its petiole is short and broad, set with leaflets reduced to spines almost to its base, and its female cones, borne on a long stalk, often end up lying down. Unless stated otherwise, the measurements come from the Flora of Australia (Hill, 1998).

  • Caudex: usually underground, sometimes extended into a short trunk 0 to 0.6 m tall, 25 to 45 cm across.
  • Leaves: 20 to 70 per crown, 100 to 205 cm long, flat in section, dark green, semi-glossy to highly glossy, with an untwisted rachis. According to the Palm and Cycad Societies of Australia (PACSOA), young leaves are bright green, obliquely erect at first then arching; leaves of 2.4 m have been measured in the Mount George area.
  • Petiole: short and broad, 6 to 15 cm long and 10 to 20 mm wide at the lowest leaflet; the lower leaflets are reduced to spines, sometimes almost to the point where the petiole leaves the crown (PACSOA).
  • Leaflets: 70 to 140 per leaf, simple, 20 to 35 cm long and 7 to 10 mm wide, much paler beneath, with a sharp tip, set at 45 to 60° to the rachis with a whitish callus at the base (PACSOA).
  • Male cones: spindle-shaped, 35 to 40 cm long and 6 to 8 cm across; up to four per plant (PACSOA).
  • Female cones: ovoid, 24 to 34 cm long and 12 to 14 cm across, with apical spines 20 to 110 mm long; one or two per plant. The PACSOA has seen cones up to 28 cm long and 16 cm across, on a stalk (peduncle) up to 24 cm long: as they ripen, they move from an almost upright to an almost horizontal position, and sometimes rest on the ground.
  • Seeds: oblong, 33 to 42 mm long and 25 to 30 mm wide, with red flesh.

Hybrids

No hybrid is known. According to the PACSOA, Macrozamia montana is geographically isolated from other Macrozamia, and no hybrid exists in the wild. No garden hybrid has been documented.

Possible confusion

The only common confusion is with Macrozamia communis, the species from which it was separated.

  • Macrozamia communis: the Flora of Australia separates Macrozamia montana by its shorter, broader petioles, set with more reduced, slightly glossier, spine-like leaflets, and by the often longer spines of its cone scales. The PACSOA puts figures on the difference: a petiole of 10 to 40 cm in Macrozamia communis, 6 to 15 cm in Macrozamia montana. Habitat helps too: coastal sites for the first, inland ridges for the second.

On the French Fous de palmiers forum, a specialist pointed out in 2010 that Macrozamia montana, like Macrozamia reducta and Macrozamia mountperriensis, is one of the species close to Macrozamia communis, which complicates the identification of plants in the trade.

Taxonomy

Macrozamia montana K.D.Hill is accepted by The World List of Cycads and the Australian Plant Census. Ken D. Hill described it in 1998 in volume 48 of the Flora of Australia (p. 717). The type (holotype in the herbarium of the Royal Botanic Gardens Sydney, NSW; isotypes at Kew, Canberra and Brisbane) was collected on 10 March 1995 by K.D. Hill and L.C. Stanberg (no. 4766) at Big Hill, about 24 km south-east of Wollomombi, on the Armidale–Kempsey road.

This is not a change of name but a separation: according to the PACSOA, these plants were previously placed under Macrozamia communis. In the same year, Hill and Jones also described Macrozamia reducta, another species split from this group. The PACSOA places Macrozamia montana in section Macrozamia, that of the large species with spiny lower leaflets; the phylogenomic study by Habib et al. (2022) shows, however, that the traditional division of the genus into sections does not reflect its evolutionary history. According to the PACSOA, the species is closest to the large Macrozamia communis of the south coast of New South Wales, rather than to the smaller plants north of Newcastle.

Etymology and history

The epithet comes from the Latin montanus, “of the mountains”, after its sites on often high and steep slopes (Flora of Australia; Haynes, 2022, as given by The World List of Cycads). The Flora of Australia notes that the name contrasts it with Macrozamia communis, generally found in coastal sites.

The history of the species in cultivation is not documented. The first collections cited by the Flora of Australia are old: one was made at Moona Plains, East Cunderang, on 17 July 1906, long before the species was described.

Common names

The Flora of Australia gives “Mountain Burrawang”, in contrast to the coastal “burrawang”, Macrozamia communis. The word “burrawang” applies to several Macrozamia of New South Wales and is ambiguous. No common name is attested in other languages.

In the wild

Macrozamia montana grows in the Macleay and Manning river catchments, in north-eastern New South Wales; it is locally abundant in wet sclerophyll forest on ridges in the ranges of the eastern escarpment of the Northern Tablelands (Flora of Australia), as far as Big Hill, south-east of Wollomombi (PlantNET). The PACSOA places its stands between the southern part of the Manning valley and the northern part of the Macleay valley, from the Mount George area in the south to the edges of the Armidale–Kempsey road in the north. It grows under a eucalypt canopy, on well-drained soils, at about 200 m around Mount George and sometimes above 750 m in the north. Around Mount George it is abundant, often at the top of roadside cuttings overrun by Lantana camara. According to the PACSOA, plants in the south are sturdier than those in the north.

No weather station lies close to the populations. The PACSOA refers to two distant stations: Armidale, on the tablelands at 980 m, where the absolute minimum is −9.3 °C (15.3 °F), with an average of 34 days a year below 0 °C (32 °F); and Taree, near the coast, at 18 m, where the records are −5.0 °C (23.0 °F) in 1970 and 45.3 °C (113.5 °F) in 2004, with 1,177 mm of rain a year. Mount George lies about 30 km from Taree. These figures frame the climate of the range without measuring it.

The PACSOA reports an unusual observation: whereas in most Macrozamia marsupials wait for the cones to break up before eating the flesh of the seeds, in Macrozamia montana they sometimes pull scales off the base of cones that are still intact.

Conservation status and regulation

Macrozamia montana is not threatened. The IUCN Red List classes it as Least Concern (LC), in the assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated 10 September 2020 and published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, GBIF and Crossref, checked on 26 October 2026). The Atlas of Living Australia, which compiles the national and state threatened-species lists, shows no listing for it.

For international trade, like other Macrozamia, it falls under CITES Appendix II, with annotation #4, which exempts seeds among other items (listing in force on 5 March 2026), and under Annex B in the European Union (Species+, checked on 26 October 2026). Plants sold must come from seed or legal cultivation; check the regulations in force before any purchase or import.

Growing Macrozamia montana

No published source describes the cultivation of Macrozamia montana, and no grower has published an account of it. The pointers below rest only on its habitat and on the cultivation of its close relative Macrozamia communis.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Soil: well-drained, as on its ridge-top sites.
  • Light: in the wild, under a eucalypt canopy in wet forest; in cultivation, not documented.
  • Water: at the Jardin zoologique tropical in La Londe-les-Maures, in the Var (southern France), experience with the genus Macrozamia shows that watering once a week in summer, when there is no rain, is a minimum and twice a week ideal, and that frost on waterlogged soil is more dangerous than dry frost. This Mediterranean experience, gained with other species of the genus, is useful for gardens with a similar climate, such as coastal California or southern Australia.
  • Space: allow room for leaves 1 to 2 m long, and keep it away from paths because of the spines on the lower part of the leaves.

Container cultivation

No container-growing report has been published. A deep pot for the fragile taproot, a free-draining mix and a dry winter are the safest precautions.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney lists one plant, from an accession of 2024, in its Garden Explorer (updated 1 October 2026), and the Australian National Botanic Gardens and the Botanic Garden of South Australia declare it to BGCI. The species does not appear in the census of Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria.
  • United States: Lotusland (Santa Barbara), San Diego Botanic Garden and the San Diego Zoo declare it to BGCI. The Montgomery Botanical Center does not list it in its online catalogue.
  • United Kingdom: the National Botanic Garden of Wales is the only British garden that declares it to BGCI.

Thirteen institutions in all declare the species to BGCI (checked on 26 October 2026), the others in China, the Czech Republic, France and the Netherlands. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not necessarily a living plant on display today. No English-language grower's report has been found.

Propagation

Propagation is by seed, the only method documented for the genus. A male plant, a female plant and, outside Australia, hand pollination are needed. Clean the seeds wearing gloves, as the flesh is toxic, and sow in a deep pot, as the taproot is fragile.

Pests and diseases

No pest or disease specific to the species is documented. In Macrozamia, growers' forums mention scale insects above all, and soil that stays wet in winter encourages rot.

Cold hardiness

The cold hardiness of Macrozamia montana is unknown: no measured value has been published and no cultivation report is documented. No source gives a USDA zone for it.

Mediterranean and European experience

No trial is documented in Europe. In 2014, a member of the Fous de palmiers forum relayed the ranking of an Australian nurseryman specialising in cycads, who placed Macrozamia montana among the “relatively hardy” species of New South Wales, with Macrozamia communis, behind the inland species; this ranking gives no temperature. For want of better, the experience gained with Macrozamia communis, which loses its leaves in France at about −7 to −9 °C (19.4 to 15.8 °F) before regrowing (see the genus page), can serve as a guide, with no guarantee that Macrozamia montana behaves in the same way.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No result has been published. The climates of Armidale and Taree (see In the wild) frame the conditions of the range without saying what the plant can withstand.

Toxicity and precautions

It should be treated as toxic, even though no data have been published for this species. Macrozamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a toxin of the liver, and their leaves, especially young ones, cause nervous damage in cattle (Healy, 1967), irreversible according to McKenzie (2006). Keep the plant and its seeds out of reach of children, dogs and cats, wear gloves to clean seeds and, in case of ingestion, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No use specific to Macrozamia montana is documented. The historical sources brought together by Asmussen (2011) on the eating of Macrozamia seeds in New South Wales predate the description of the species and do not single it out.

Frequently asked questions

How can Macrozamia montana be told from Macrozamia communis?

By the petiole. That of Macrozamia montana is short (6 to 15 cm) and broad, set with spines almost to the base; that of Macrozamia communis is 10 to 40 cm long. The female cones of Macrozamia montana, on a long stalk, often end up lying down.

Is Macrozamia montana hardier than Macrozamia communis?

Nobody knows. Its habitat is higher, but no measurement or cultivation trial has been published. An Australian specialist places it in the same hardiness group as Macrozamia communis.

Does it form a trunk?

Rarely. The caudex is usually underground; it sometimes forms a short trunk no taller than 60 cm according to the Flora of Australia.

Is the species endangered?

No. The IUCN classes it as Least Concern (2022), and it is locally abundant. Like all cycads, it is nevertheless covered by CITES.

Where can Macrozamia montana be found?

With difficulty: it is very rarely grown. Insist on a seed-raised plant and the necessary CITES documents for any purchase from abroad.

Is Macrozamia montana poisonous?

Yes, like all Macrozamia. The seeds and young leaves are the most dangerous parts. Keep it out of reach of children and pets, and handle the seeds with gloves.
