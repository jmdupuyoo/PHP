Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi is a small cycad from south-eastern Queensland, in eastern Australia, that grows in heath and open woodland on coastal sands, the “wallum” of the Wide Bay district, from Maryborough to Tin Can Bay and K'gari (Fraser Island). A species of the genus Macrozamia, it has only an underground stem and a few erect leaves, very strongly twisted in a spiral and set with needle-like leaflets. It has also been published as Encephalartos pauli-guilielmi and Macrozamia plumosa. Its young leaves paralyse cattle, which earned it the farmers' name “Twisted Ricketts Weed”. It is listed as Endangered both by the IUCN and under Australian law.

Key facts about Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi

  • Accepted name: Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi W.Hill & F.Muell. (1859), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and the Australian Plant Census
  • Former names and synonyms: Encephalartos pauli-guilielmi (W.Hill & F.Muell.) F.Muell. (1859); Macrozamia plumosa W.Bull (1874); Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi subsp. pauli-guilielmi (Johnson, 1959)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales; type species of section Parazamia
  • Common names: “Pineapple Zamia”, “Twisted Ricketts Weed” (Flora of Australia); “Wild Pineapple”, “Zamia Palm” (Australian Plant Census)
  • Natural range: endemic to south-eastern Queensland, Wide Bay district, mainly in the coastal lowlands
  • IUCN status: Endangered (EN, criteria A2cd), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated August 2020 and published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union; Endangered under the Australian EPBC Act 1999 (since 16 July 2000) and in Queensland
  • Documented hardiness: no cultivation data; a species of the subtropical coast (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, heath and open woodland on deep siliceous sands of old dunes
  • Habit and adult size: underground stem 10 to 25 cm across; 1 to 6 erect leaves 60 to 90 cm long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: grown at the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney and Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria; declared to BGCI by 11 institutions, including RHS Garden Wisley and Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden
  • Toxicity: young leaves toxic to cattle; seeds toxic as throughout the genus (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi

It is recognised by its few erect leaves with a very strongly twisted axis, and by its extremely narrow leaflets, 2 to 4 mm wide and channelled, which give the plant a feathery look. David Jones and Paul Forster (1994) describe it as follows.

  • Stem: underground, more or less ovoid, 10 to 20 cm across, sometimes branched, with up to 19 heads in one clump.
  • Leaves: 1 to 6 in a sparse crown, erect, 60 to 90 cm long, hairless; young leaves bright green.
  • Leaflets: 130 to 190 per leaf, linear, 15 to 40 cm long and 2 to 4 mm wide, inserted at 20 to 45° on the axis, curved in their upper half, deeply concave on the upper side, fairly thick, with a cream to white callus at the base; the lowest leaflets are not reduced to spines.
  • Male cones: 8 to 14 cm long and 3.5 to 5 cm across, on a stalk 8 to 12 cm long, with almost spineless scales.
  • Female cones: 9 to 12 cm long and 5 to 6.5 cm across, the scales bearing a spine up to 2.5 cm long.
  • Seeds: obovoid, 2 to 2.5 cm long and 1.5 to 2 cm wide, with red flesh according to the Flora of Australia.

Leaf colour is described inconsistently. Jones and Forster (1994) call the leaves dark green and dull; the Flora of Australia (Hill, 1998) describes them as mid-green and highly glossy, yet in its taxonomic note it separates the species by its “very narrow, pale green pinnae”. A member of the Palm and Cycad Societies of Australia (PACSOA) describes them as pale to mid-green. He adds that a plant usually carries two to five leaves whose axis twists about every 7.5 cm, so that a leaf a metre long can make up to ten full turns, and that the petiole, about 2 cm wide at the base, is keeled on one side and flat on the other. Below ground, the stem continues into one to three parsnip-like roots.

Hybrids

No hybrid of Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi has been reported under the present definition of the species. The Atlas of Living Australia lists a name “Macrozamia lomandroides x Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi”, without a description. In 1959, L.A.S. Johnson noted transitional forms between what he called subspecies pauli-guilielmi and subspecies plurinervia in southern Queensland, but those populations now belong to other species (see Taxonomy).

Possible confusion

Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi belongs to a small group of south-eastern Queensland species with strongly twisted leaves and narrow, concave leaflets. Jones and Forster (1994) placed in it two species described from plants that passed for forms of Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi:

  • Macrozamia parcifolia, from around Biggenden, has even narrower and finer leaflets, dark green, set more obliquely, with an inconspicuous, greenish callus; its leaves look tousled and wispy.
  • Macrozamia crassifolia, from the Mundubbera area, has much thicker, dark green leaflets, male cones with conspicuous terminal spines and larger female cones.

Both species grow in other habitats: according to Jones and Forster, Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi is confined to the wallum of the coastal lowlands, on deep siliceous sands. A PACSOA member also recalls that Macrozamia lomandroides, very similar in habit and growing on comparable ground, was long taken for a form of Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi before David Jones described it in 1991.

Macrozamia flexuosa, of the Hunter Region of New South Wales, was long a subspecies of Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi. Besides its range, the Flora of Australia separates it by its long, more or less terete petioles (20 to 35 cm, against 12 to 20 cm), fewer leaflets (80 to 150, against 130 to 190) and broader ones (3 to 7 mm, against 2.5 to 4 mm). Without cones, the plant hardly looks like a cycad at all: the PACSOA notes that many judges at horticultural shows would not recognise it as one.

Taxonomy

Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi is accepted by The World List of Cycads and the Australian Plant Census. Walter Hill, Colonial Botanist of Queensland, and Ferdinand von Mueller described it in 1859 in the first volume of the Fragmenta Phytographiae Australiae, from a plant collected by Hill “near Moreton Bay, where it is rare”; the holotype is held at the Melbourne herbarium (MEL). The same year, Mueller moved it to Encephalartos, giving the synonym Encephalartos pauli-guilielmi. The name Macrozamia plumosa, published in 1874 by the nurseryman William Bull, refers to the same plant: Mueller pointed this out in a letter of 1875, and The World List of Cycads treats it as a synonym.

The circumscription of the species has changed a great deal. In 1959, L.A.S. Johnson recalled that Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi is the type species of section Parazamia, the small species with an underground stem, and divided it into three subspecies: pauli-guilielmi, plurinervia and flexuosa. The last two are now distinct species: Macrozamia plurinervia, raised to species rank by David Jones in 1991, and Macrozamia flexuosa. In 1994, Jones and Forster described Macrozamia parcifolia and Macrozamia crassifolia from plants until then referred to this species. In practice, older records of Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi from Stanthorpe, the Darling Downs or New South Wales usually concern these related species. According to Jones and Forster (1994), the species in its present sense is confined to the Wide Bay district.

The online Flora of Australia still gives a distribution “in the Burnett, Darling Downs and western Moreton districts”, a wording taken from Johnson (1959), although its representative specimens come from Tin Can Bay and Maryborough. This page follows the circumscription of Jones and Forster.

Etymology and history

The epithet is a Latin genitive, “of Paul William”. Ferdinand von Mueller explained it himself in a letter of 25 August 1875: the species was named after “Prince Paul William, the brother of the King of Wuertemberg, who in 1858 visited Australia for ornithologic researches”. Johnson (1959) added that the original spelling, “Pauli Guilielmi”, must not be altered and that the final “i” should not be doubled. The World List of Cycads, after Haynes (2022), gives the prince's dates as 1785–1852, which does not fit a visit in 1858; the exact identity of the dedicatee has not been settled by a reliable source.

In his 1886 supplement to the Queensland flora, F.M. Bailey described seeds with a “golden yellow” coat and recorded the species near Stanthorpe and at Tinana Creek, near Maryborough; his concept of the species was broader than today's. Johnson noted in 1959 that the plant was already fairly often grown, in Australia and abroad.

Common names

The Flora of Australia gives two English names, “Pineapple Zamia” and “Twisted Ricketts Weed”, and the Australian Plant Census adds “Wild Pineapple” and “Zamia Palm”. According to the PACSOA, the second name is used by local farmers, because the young leaves cause paralysis of the hind legs in cattle, known locally as “rickets”. The Royal Botanic Garden Sydney labels its plant “Pineapple Zamia”. No Aboriginal name specific to the species is attested.

In the wild

Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi is endemic to the Wide Bay district of south-eastern Queensland, where it lives mainly in the coastal lowlands (Jones and Forster, 1994). The specimens cited by the Flora of Australia come from south of Tin Can Bay, the Tin Can Bay road and east of Maryborough. A PACSOA member records it on K'gari (Fraser Island), within the national park, and on the mainland from Rainbow Beach to perhaps Hervey Bay, as well as around Gympie, where it grew in abundance under slash pine plantations (Pinus elliottii).

It grows scattered in open woodland and heath, almost always on siliceous sands of old beach dunes (Flora of Australia). The PACSOA describes a whitish sandy soil over a fawn-yellow subsoil, among small Callistemon and Xanthorrhoea, in a wallum that flowers profusely in spring.

Fire is part of this habitat. According to the Flora of Australia, which cites the national recovery plan (Queensland Herbarium, 2007), adult plants resprout from their underground stem after a fire, whereas seedlings and unburied seeds are usually killed; synchronised coning often follows a fire.

Conservation status and regulation

Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi is listed as Endangered (EN) on the IUCN Red List under criteria A2cd, a population reduction linked to habitat loss and collection, in the assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated 14 August 2020 and published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, GBIF and Crossref, checked on 26 October 2026). In Australia it has been listed as Endangered under the Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act 1999 since 16 July 2000, and in Queensland under the Nature Conservation (Plants) Regulation 2020 (Flora of Australia; Atlas of Living Australia). With five other Queensland cycads, it is covered by a national recovery plan published in 2007 by the Queensland Herbarium.

The PACSOA points to the pressure of urban development around Tin Can Bay and Rainbow Beach. Some English-language pages describe the species as Critically Endangered (CR): this is wrong, the only Critically Endangered species of the genus being Macrozamia humilis.

Like other Macrozamia, it has been listed in CITES Appendix II since 4 February 1977, with annotation #4: seeds and pollen are exempt, but plants are not. In the European Union it falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026 (Species+, checked on 26 October 2026). Check the regulations in force before any purchase or import, and buy only seed-raised plants of known origin.

Growing Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi

Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi is above all a collector's plant for a pot. A PACSOA member, in an article of 1993, rates it at least as highly as Bowenia serrulata as a pot plant, for the symmetry and elegance of its feathery leaves. His advice, that of an experienced amateur, has not been confirmed by another source.

Container cultivation

  • Pot: very deep, to hold the stem and the parsnip-like roots; for a plant of coning size, about 40 cm deep and 30 cm across.
  • Mix: like the natural soil, very sandy: coarse, well-washed river sand, fine composted pine bark and about a quarter of coarse perlite or diatomite.
  • Light: no precise recommendation; in the wild the plant lives in heath and open woodland, in bright light.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

No result in the ground has been documented outside its range. In frost-free gardens with a climate close to its own, a deep, sandy, perfectly drained soil and bright light would mimic its wallum habitat; this is a deduction from its ecology, not a documented result.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney's Garden Explorer lists one plant of wild origin in bed 102i (updated 1 October 2026). The census of Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria, downloaded on 19 October 2026, records one plant at Cranbourne, planted in 2012. The Botanic Garden of South Australia and the University of Melbourne's System Garden also declare it to BGCI.
  • United States: Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden (Miami, Florida) declares it to BGCI. The species does not appear in the online catalogue of Montgomery Botanical Center.
  • United Kingdom: RHS Garden Wisley, in Surrey, is the only British garden that declares it to BGCI.

Eleven institutions in all declare the species to BGCI (checked on 26 October 2026), the others in China, Mexico, New Zealand, Russia and Spain. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not necessarily a living plant on display today. No English-language grower's report has been found.

Propagation

Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi is propagated by seed. The same PACSOA member advises testing seeds by flotation, those that sink being in principle viable, while noting that some seeds float because the embryo has shrunk and may still germinate after a few days' soaking. With seeds freshly cleaned of their flesh, he obtains quicker germination after soaking them for 48 hours in a solution of indolebutyric acid, other nurserymen using gibberellic acid; untreated seeds kept moist germinate within a few months. Wear gloves to remove the flesh, which is toxic.

Pests and diseases

No pest or disease specific to Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi is documented. For a potted plant, waterlogging in too fine a mix is the likeliest risk, hence the emphasis on a very sandy mix.

Cold hardiness

The cold hardiness of Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi is not documented: no cold trial has been published anywhere. No source gives a USDA zone for it.

Mediterranean and European experience

No cultivation result is documented in Europe, either in the ground or in a pot. In 2014, a member of the French Fous de palmiers forum relayed the ranking of an Australian cycad specialist, who placed Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi among the “not very hardy” species of the Queensland coast, without giving a temperature. Grow it in a pot and keep it frost-free in winter.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

The species lives in the subtropical coastal lowlands of south-eastern Queensland. No measured value has been published; in Britain and frost-prone parts of the United States, it is a plant for a pot under glass.

Toxicity and precautions

Yes, Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi is toxic. The Flora of Australia notes that cattle can suffer paralysis of the hind legs after eating the new leaves, which is what its name “Twisted Ricketts Weed” refers to. Macrozamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a poison of the liver (Healy, 1967); no analysis specific to this species has been published.

No data on dogs and cats have been published for the species, but poisoning of pets by cycads is well known. Wear gloves to handle the seeds and keep plants and seeds out of reach of children, dogs and cats. In case of ingestion, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi is one of the five Macrozamia species whose consumption by Aboriginal people is recorded in Asmussen's review (2011). Walter Hill, who described the species, annotated a herbarium sheet collected between 1860 and 1870 with the words “the nuts of this plant are the only food the natives store”, as quoted by Asmussen after the recovery plan of 2007. In the Wide Bay region, nineteenth-century accounts describe long leaching of the kernels in water, often in a bag, before cooking, without always naming the species. This knowledge is not a recipe: incomplete preparation causes severe poisoning. Never eat cycad seeds.

Frequently asked questions

Why are the leaves of Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi so twisted?

It is a fixed character of the species. The leaf axis twists in a spiral about every 7.5 cm, according to a PACSOA member, so that a leaf a metre long can make up to ten turns. The very narrow leaflets add to the feathery effect.

Is Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi Critically Endangered?

No, it is Endangered (EN). The IUCN listed it as EN in 2022, as does Australian law. Some pages wrongly call it Critically Endangered; in the genus, only Macrozamia humilis has that status.

Is it dangerous to animals?

Yes. The young leaves paralyse the hind legs of cattle, hence the name “Twisted Ricketts Weed”, and the seeds are toxic as in all cycads. Keep plants and seeds away from dogs and other animals.

Can it be grown outdoors in a frosty climate?

Nobody knows, and it is unlikely without shelter. No trial has been published, and the species comes from subtropical coastal lowlands. Grow it in a deep pot and keep it frost-free in winter.

How does it differ from Macrozamia flexuosa?

Mainly by its leaflets, petiole and range. Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi has more numerous and narrower leaflets (2.5 to 4 mm) and a shorter petiole; Macrozamia flexuosa has broader leaflets and long, rounded petioles, and grows in New South Wales rather than Queensland.

What pot does Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi need?

A very deep one. Its underground stem continues into parsnip-like roots; an Australian amateur recommends about 40 cm deep for an adult plant, with a very sandy mix.
