Encephalartos septentrionalis, the Nile cycad, is a cycad of the wooded savannas and granite hills of central Africa, found in South Sudan, northern Uganda, the Central African Republic and the north-east of the Democratic Republic of the Congo. It is one of the two species of the genus Encephalartos that reach furthest north, hence its name, “the northern one”. Although the explorer Georg Schweinfurth proposed the name as early as 1871, the species is still poorly known: its trunk is described as anything from a buried globe to a stem 2 m tall, and botanists have wondered whether the name covers more than one species. Adult plants survive bush fires and may lose all their leaves in the dry season. The IUCN assesses it as Least Concern.

Key facts about Encephalartos septentrionalis

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos septentrionalis Schweinf. (1871), according to The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15), Species+ and GBIF; IPNI attributes it to “Schweinf. ex Eichler” (1887) (see Taxonomy)
  • Synonyms: none; a silver-leaved form circulates in cultivation as 'Sudan Silver', a name with no botanical standing
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Nile cycad (Species+); local names recorded in 1871: Mwüe-piäh (Zande country) and Kägga-Kunda (Bongo)
  • Natural range: South Sudan (Western Equatoria), northern Uganda, Central African Republic, north-eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo
  • IUCN status: Least Concern (LC), assessment dated 4 March 2020 and published in 2024 (GBIF, Crossref); The World List of Cycads still cites the previous assessment, also LC (Bösenberg, 2010); Data Deficient in the IUCN action plan of 2003
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I since 1977, like the whole genus; Annex A in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: no measured or reported value (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, open woodland, grassland and rocky hills, often among granite boulders in full sun; in cultivation, a free-draining soil and a drier winter
  • Habit and adult size: trunk “globose or up to 2 m high and 30 cm diameter”, leaves 90 to 150 cm long (Melville, 1957); Schweinfurth (1871) saw only buried, globe-shaped trunks about 30 cm across
  • In the UK, US and Australia: no British garden declares it; in the United States, only the USDA National Plant Germplasm System (BGCI); in Australia, Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria, the University of Melbourne System Garden and the Adelaide Botanic Garden (BGCI)
  • Toxicity: not documented for the species; seeds toxic, as in all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos septentrionalis

Encephalartos septentrionalis is a medium-sized, green-leaved cycad whose leaflets are well spaced and often carry several teeth crowded towards the base of the margin. In the wild, adults often have fire-blackened trunks and may be leafless for part of the dry season.

The first account is Schweinfurth's, who saw the plant in its habitat during his journey: a trunk always globe-shaped, about one foot (30 cm) across, hidden in the ground and covered, as if with scales, by the bases of old leaves, and leaves up to five feet (about 1.5 m) long. The first full description is Ronald Melville's, in his 1957 study of the Encephalartos of central Africa for the Flora of Tropical East Africa. Melville warned that he had pieced it together from several collections, none of them complete; the figures below were checked against the printed page:

  • Trunk: globose or up to 2 m tall and 30 cm in diameter;
  • Leaves: linear-oblong, 90 to 150 cm long and 20 to 28 cm wide, grey-woolly at first, then almost hairless; the swollen base of the petiole keeps a dense grey to greyish-buff wool; the rachis is distinctly grooved between the leaflets;
  • Median leaflets: oblong to lanceolate, 10 to 18 cm long and 17 to 28 mm wide, with slightly turned-back margins and two to seven spiny teeth, often crowded towards the base; the lower margin curves up to a forward-pointing, pungent tip; 26 to 43 parallel veins beneath;
  • Lower leaflets: coarsely spiny, then three- or two-forked, then reduced to simple spines just above the base of the petiole;
  • Male cones: four to ten, narrowly ellipsoid, 12 to 20 cm long and 3 to 4.5 cm in diameter, on slender stalks 10 to 15 cm long;
  • Female cones: cylindrical, about 23 cm long and 11 cm in diameter, on a short stalk;
  • Seeds: ellipsoid to almost globular, 23 to 34 mm long, scarlet.

Later descriptions, compiled by the online encyclopaedia LLIFLE from the Flora of Tropical East Africa and the IUCN assessment, give a trunk 30 to 200 cm tall (exceptionally 250 cm) and 30 to 75 cm in diameter, branching at the base, rarely in clumps of more than three stems, leaves 1.5 to 2 m long, up to 15 male cones and up to four female cones, yellowish green, turning brown when ripe. Vorster and Heibloem (1995) add that the cones are yellowish green and that Goode's book (1989) shows a female cone on a very long stalk, up to 30 cm, a detail they had found nowhere else in the literature. The Californian nursery Jungle Music notes that the new leaves are covered with a fine whitish down that falls as they mature, except on the petiole, where it lasts longer.

Hybrids

No natural or garden hybrid of Encephalartos septentrionalis is documented.

Possible confusion

The most detailed published comparison concerns Encephalartos imbricans, described from Uganda in 1995 and now included in Encephalartos equatorialis by The World List of Cycads. Vorster and Heibloem note that both plants often have up to four teeth crowded near the base of the upper margin of the leaflets, but that Encephalartos septentrionalis has well-spaced, not overlapping, leaflets, yellowish-green rather than glaucous-green cones, and a stalked female cone.

In northern Uganda the species grows near Encephalartos macrostrobilus, described in 1997 and named for its large female cones, compared with those of Encephalartos septentrionalis (Haynes, 2022). Its authors, Jones and Wynants, found it within 50 km of at least one population of Encephalartos septentrionalis and expected the two to be confused. According to them, Encephalartos macrostrobilus has a well-developed, upright trunk, against a very short, reclining or even underground one; a densely leafy crown, against the often tattered dozen or two leaves of Encephalartos septentrionalis; female cones about 80 cm long, olive green when ripe, with yellow to ochre seeds, against cones 27 to 36 cm long, manila brown, with bright red seeds; and remarkably slender male cones. Even the seedlings differ: the first leaf of Encephalartos septentrionalis has four or five pairs of leaflets, toothed along much of their upper margin, against six pairs with few or no teeth in Encephalartos macrostrobilus.

In 1957 Melville also included in Encephalartos barteri plants from north-western Uganda and the neighbouring part of Sudan, where they sometimes grow with Encephalartos septentrionalis, and he called for fuller collections to see whether the two intergrade. In his keys, Encephalartos barteri has linear-lanceolate leaflets with a straight tip and 20 to 24 veins, against oblong-lanceolate leaflets with forward-arching tips and 26 to 43 veins in Encephalartos septentrionalis. The World List of Cycads now restricts Encephalartos barteri to West Africa.

Plants sold as 'Sudan Silver', with silvery grey leaves, are presented by LLIFLE as a form collected near Moyo, in northern Uganda; their status is unresolved, and The World List of Cycads does not recognise them as a separate taxon.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos septentrionalis is an accepted name with no synonyms. The World List of Cycads and Species+ attribute it to Georg Schweinfurth, who proposed it in 1871 in the Botanische Zeitung (29: 334). IPNI attributes it instead to “Schweinf. ex Eichler”, with publication in Engler and Prantl's Die natürlichen Pflanzenfamilien (II, 1: 22) in 1887. The 1871 text proposes the name on a condition: if nothing similar had been found elsewhere in Africa, the plant had to be new, “and in that case I propose the name Encephalartos septentrionalis”. No published source explains the difference of attribution, which does not change the name of the plant.

The types are two of Schweinfurth's collections, no. 2952 from Gumango hill, in South Sudan, and no. 3992 from near Nganye, both at Kew and treated as syntypes by Melville (1957), who listed earlier treatments by Prain (1917), Schuster (1932) and others. Melville gave the distribution as Uganda, Sudan, Ubangi-Uele (then in the Belgian Congo) and Haut-Chari. Vorster and Heibloem (1995) stress that, despite its age, the species is poorly known and that, given its unusually wide range, “more than one species may be involved”. No later revision on this point is documented.

Etymology and history

The epithet is the Latin septentrionalis, “northern”. Schweinfurth chose it “to establish an important phytogeographical fact”: as far as he knew, no traveller had collected anything similar in eastern, western or southern Africa (Haynes, 2022, citing Schweinfurth, 1871).

Schweinfurth's report, written after his first journey into the land of the Niam-Niam (the Azande), is the first account of the plant. He describes it as widespread but local and scattered in the savanna woodland, particularly frequent in the forest on the right bank of the Tondj river, on the granite hill of Gumango and east of the upper Huüh, with its northern limit apparently near the village of Ngoli. He notes that at the beginning of February new leaves appear, after the plant has survived the bush fire “hidden like a dead bulb”. Despite generous rewards offered, he obtained no cones; at the end of July, south of Ngoli, he found only seeds of the previous year, already stripped of their fleshy coat, similar in shape and size to those of Zamia spiralis, an Australian cycad. He dug up three plants and sent one to Berlin, which lost much of its weight on the way (it first weighed 25 pounds); he gave up shipping the second, bored right through by an insect gallery, and planted the remaining two in his garden on the spot.

In 1997 the journal Encephalartos, publishing the description of Encephalartos macrostrobilus, quoted a manuscript by Roy Osborne on the history and distribution of the species: seed from the Moyo locality, in northern Uganda, had been distributed fairly widely in 1995 under the name Encephalartos septentrionalis; at about the same time, seed from the Didinga Hills, in southern Sudan, circulated as “Encephalartos sp. Sudan”; and another population had been reported from the Imatong Mountains, on the Uganda–Sudan border. According to Osborne, these seed collections had made the species somewhat more common than a decade earlier. More recently, photographs taken between 2022 and 2024 in the Haute-Kotto and Mbomou prefectures of the Central African Republic have been added to the gallery of The World List of Cycads. The history of the species' introduction into cultivation is not documented; according to LLIFLE, the 'Sudan Silver' form was collected near Moyo, in Uganda, by a collector.

Common names

In English the species is called the Nile cycad by Species+ and LLIFLE, and Jungle Music uses the same name. Schweinfurth (1871) recorded two local names, transcribed in German spelling: “Mwüe-piäh” among the Niam-Niam (Azande) and “Kägga-Kunda” among the Bongo. No recent source confirms their present use or spelling.

In the wild

Encephalartos septentrionalis has a wide range shared by four countries: Western Equatoria in South Sudan, northern Uganda, the Central African Republic and the former Orientale province of the Democratic Republic of the Congo (The World List of Cycads). According to the texts compiled by LLIFLE, it grows between 500 and 2,500 m above sea level, in rocky lowland and medium-altitude country, in open woodland, grassland and open bushland, in a hot, dry climate, often among granite boulders in full sun. Schweinfurth had already seen it on a granite hill and in savanna woodland. Jungle Music, by contrast, describes the climate as hot and fairly humid, with a wet season in summer, and writes that its populations are relatively small and far apart.

Fire shapes its life. Frequent fires blacken the large trunks, but the bases of old leaves protect the stem, and the plant soon produces new leaves if the old ones have burnt. Adults sometimes lose their leaves for a while and rarely keep them for a second season. According to the same sources, regeneration from seed is poor because of heat, drought and grass fires.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos septentrionalis is assessed as Least Concern (LC) on the IUCN Red List. The current assessment, by the Eastern Arc Mountains and Coastal Forests CEPF Plant Assessment Project with J. Kalema and H.J. Beentje, is dated 4 March 2020 and was published in 2024 (GBIF, Crossref); The World List of Cycads still cites the previous one, by J.D. Bösenberg (2010), which was also LC. Some secondary websites, including LLIFLE, give Near Threatened while citing the 2010 assessment; neither of the two published assessments supports that category. In 2003 the IUCN action plan (Donaldson, 2003) classed the species as Data Deficient and recorded it in at least one protected area. According to LLIFLE, the species is relatively common in the area studied and faces no known threat, while farming, droughts and collecting are possible ones.

Regulation, according to Species+ (consulted through its public API on 26 October 2026):

  • CITES: Appendix I since 4 February 1977, like the whole genus;
  • European Union: Annex A, current list in Regulation (EU) 2026/1383 (in force since 29 June 2026); for artificially propagated plants imported from South Africa (source codes A and D), the EU Scientific Review Group gave a positive opinion for caudices up to 7 cm in diameter (20 June 2011) and a negative opinion above that size (7 February 2013), both still current;
  • past measures: CITES trade suspensions concerning the Democratic Republic of the Congo, in 2014 and 2015, are no longer in force.

Seeds are covered in the same way as plants. Any plant offered for sale must come from cultivation and carry the CITES documents required by the countries concerned (and, in the European Union, the EU documents). These notes do not replace the official texts: check Species+ and the national authorities before any purchase or import.

Growing Encephalartos septentrionalis

Encephalartos septentrionalis is grown in a very free-draining soil, in good light, with a drier spell in winter. Cultivation data come mainly from two secondary sources, to be read with caution, and from a single grower's report.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

LLIFLE, an online encyclopaedia that compiles, among other things, the literature of the cycad societies, describes a plant suited to warm temperate and subtropical climates, to be grown in full sun without scorching, in a gravelly, well-drained soil, watered generously in the growing season and kept rather dry in winter. In cultivation the plant is said to develop a deep taproot, and young plants often lose the previous year's leaves before the new ones appear; dead leaves, if left, hang like a skirt around the trunk. The first cone would take 15 to 20 years.

Jungle Music, a nursery at Encinitas in southern California, reports a more mixed experience: the species grows at a moderate rate but seems to have “hardiness issues in subtropical climates” and often looks shabby for part of the year. In coastal and inland southern California it can take sun but looks better with some protection; in desert areas the nursery recommends filtered light or shade, and a loamy soil that drains well. It sees the plant as best suited to a garden with an established canopy, set back from paths, since it takes up room.

Container cultivation

No container-specific advice has been published for this species. Its deep taproot calls for a deep pot, and growing it in a container is the practical way to keep it dry and frost-free in winter where the climate is cool or wet. The only report from Europe concerns a plant whose caudex rotted, which shows the risk of a cold, wet medium (see Propagation).

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

The species is rare in collections. BGCI PlantSearch lists eight institutions holding it: in Australia, Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria (Melbourne Gardens), the University of Melbourne System Garden and the Botanic Garden and State Herbarium of South Australia in Adelaide; in the United States, only the USDA National Plant Germplasm System; the others are in China, Singapore, South Africa and the Democratic Republic of the Congo. No British garden declares it, and the Montgomery Botanical Center catalogue (Florida) has no accession of it. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not a living plant on display today. Jungle Music describes it as very rare and occasionally has a few older plants, with no seed available in recent years.

Propagation

Sowing is the usual method; LLIFLE ranks the seeds among the easiest to germinate. Deep pots suit the taproot. For the general rules, see the article on growing cycads from seed. The plant can also regrow from its roots: on the French Fous de palmiers forum, a grower in Agen (Lot-et-Garonne, south-west France) reported in December 2009 that he had saved a plant whose caudex had rotted and whose mother plant had died; keeping practically only the root part, he obtained a new leaf and a new caudex after about a year (messages of 2009, 2010 and 2011). The cause of the rot and whether the plant was in a pot or in the ground are not stated. Jungle Music notes that the species can sucker but generally does not. Seeds and plants are covered by CITES Appendix I, and no plant should be taken from the wild.

Pests and diseases

Caudex rot is the only cultivation problem reported (see Propagation). In the wild, Schweinfurth found a plant bored right through by an insect gallery, without identifying the insect. No other disease or pest specific to the species is documented.

Cold hardiness

No cold-tolerance value, measured or reported by a grower, is documented for Encephalartos septentrionalis. LLIFLE advises keeping the plant quite dry in winter, at about 10 °C (50 °F), and writes that it may withstand light, short frosts if dry, while hard frosts would probably be fatal; these indications rest on no dated episode. As with all Encephalartos, survivals in cold areas often concern young plants whose caudex is still small or underground, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil and sometimes by snow; adult plants with an aerial trunk do not benefit from this protection.

Mediterranean and European experience

No frost episode is documented in Europe. The only published report, from Agen in south-west France (see Propagation), concerns caudex rot, not frost. No result has been published for this species by Mediterranean growers, whether in France or in Italy.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

  • United States: in southern California, Jungle Music finds the species not very robust and prone to a shabby look in a subtropical climate, without giving temperatures.
  • Australia: the gardens that declare it, in Melbourne and Adelaide, have published no winter data for it.
  • United Kingdom: no data; a heated glasshouse is the safe choice.

No USDA hardiness zone is documented for Encephalartos septentrionalis in a reliable source.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data have been published for Encephalartos septentrionalis. Like other cycads, Encephalartos contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a toxin affecting the liver, the digestive tract and the nervous system; the seeds are the most dangerous part. Treat the plant as toxic, wear gloves to clean the seeds, and keep plants and seeds out of reach of children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use is documented. Schweinfurth wrote in 1871 that the local people knew of no use for the plant, which he thought infrequent, and whose pith, very reduced, is surrounded by a thick woody sheath.

Frequently asked questions

Why is it called septentrionalis?

Because it lives very far north for an Encephalartos. Schweinfurth chose this name, “northern”, to stress that no similar plant had been found elsewhere in Africa in his day. With Encephalartos mackenziei, it is one of the species that reach furthest north, into South Sudan.

Does it have a trunk?

It depends on the source. In 1871 Schweinfurth described a globe-shaped trunk, always buried; Melville (1957) gives a trunk globose or up to 2 m tall, and later descriptions up to 2.5 m. This variation is one reason why botanists wonder whether the name covers more than one species.

Is it normal for it to lose its leaves?

Yes, to some extent. In the wild, adults sometimes lose their leaves in the dry season or after a fire, then produce new ones. In cultivation, young plants often lose their old leaves before producing new ones, according to LLIFLE.

What is 'Sudan Silver'?

A form with silvery grey leaves, collected near Moyo in northern Uganda according to LLIFLE. Some present it as a new species, but it has never been described, and The World List of Cycads does not recognise it.

How can it be told from Encephalartos macrostrobilus?

By its trunk and cones: Encephalartos macrostrobilus has a real upright trunk and female cones about 80 cm long, whereas Encephalartos septentrionalis has a short or underground trunk and much smaller female cones.

Is it endangered?

No. The IUCN assesses it as Least Concern (assessment published in 2024). Like all Encephalartos, it is nevertheless protected by CITES Appendix I.

Is it frost-hardy?

Nobody knows. No measurement has been published; the sources advise overwintering it dry, at about 10 °C (50 °F), and fear hard frosts.

Is it poisonous?

It should be treated as such. No data exist for the species, but Encephalartos contain toxins dangerous to people and animals.
