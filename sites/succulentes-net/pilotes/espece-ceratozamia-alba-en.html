Ceratozamia alba is a cycad of northern Chiapas, in southern Mexico, known only from the Selva El Ocote Biosphere Reserve, in the mountains of the north of the state. Described in 2024, this species of the genus Ceratozamia takes its name from its whitish-green young leaves, which turn bluish green and then light green or olive green as they mature. It grows out of cracks in karst limestone rocks, in tropical rainforest between 700 and 800 m (about 2,300 to 2,600 ft). Its populations were first taken for Ceratozamia robusta, and its authors consider it Critically Endangered.

  • Former names and synonyms: no synonyms; the populations were previously referred to Ceratozamia robusta

  • Common names: none recorded, in English or in Spanish
  • Natural range: Mexico, northern Chiapas, Selva El Ocote Biosphere Reserve; a single known population, at 700 to 800 m
  • IUCN status: Not Evaluated (NE); Critically Endangered (CR) proposed by the authors of the description (one population, fewer than 50 adults on about 10 ha)
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I (whole genus, since 1985); Annex A in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: no data (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, a rock-dweller on karst limestone (holes and crevices), in tropical rainforest at 700 to 800 m; no cultivation data

  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; treat it as poisonous, like all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Ceratozamia alba

The most visible character is the colour of the emerging leaves: whitish green and glaucous, a shade unique in the genus according to the authors. They then become bluish green and bloomy, and finally light green to olive green, so that one plant often carries leaves of two colours. The original description (Pérez-Farrera and colleagues, 2024) adds circinate vernation (leaflets rolled like a crozier as they emerge), arching adult leaves, leaflet articulations that are light green to yellowish on the upper side, a short, slender trunk, upright bluish-green female cones and light green to yellowish-green male cones with reddish hairs.

The measurements given in the original description:

  • Trunk: unbranched, cylindrical, upright and then decumbent with age, covered with old leaf bases, 16 to 30 cm tall and 9 to 22 cm across; cataphylls persistent, triangular, brown and densely woolly when they emerge.
  • Leaves: 4 to 17 in an open crown, upright in young plants and drooping with age, 140 to 241 cm long and 52.5 to 76.4 cm wide; petiole cylindrical, ochre, 51.8 to 104.6 cm, armed with small, fine, scattered prickles; rachis green, 48 to 109.6 cm, with few prickles, fewer and fewer towards the tip.
  • Leaflets: 14 to 27 pairs, linear to long-lanceolate, leathery, with entire margins and an acute to acuminate tip; median leaflets 27.2 to 38.7 cm long and 2.2 to 3.5 cm wide, 1.8 to 5.9 cm apart, with 15 to 22 prominent parallel veins; seedlings have two eophylls (first leaves).
  • Male cone: solitary, conical, upright, light green to yellowish green when mature, 15.9 to 16.5 cm long and 3.4 to 3.5 cm across, on a light brown woolly peduncle 1.1 to 1.5 cm long; microsporophylls wedge-shaped, 10.6 to 11.5 mm by 6.2 to 8.1 mm, two-horned, reddish in the centre of the outer face.
  • Female cone: solitary, cylindrical, upright, bluish green, 21.6 to 24 cm long and 8 to 9 cm across, with a short-pointed tip and blackish to reddish hairs when mature; woolly peduncle 6 to 10 cm long; megasporophylls peltate, two-horned, 4.4 to 5.4 cm wide.
  • Seeds: ovoid, with a cream sarcotesta before maturity and a beige sclerotesta when mature, 14.5 to 28.8 mm long and 14.5 to 17 mm wide.

The sources do not quite agree on colours. The abstract of the article describes both mature male and female cones as bluish green, and the leaves as turning bluish green when mature; the detailed description keeps the bluish-green colour for the female cone, gives the male cone a light green to yellowish-green colour and has the leaves ending light green or olive green. This page follows the detailed description. The authors add that the species differs from its two nearest neighbours in the number of leaves and leaflets, the length of the petiole and rachis, and the width of the median leaflets.

Adult plants resemble Ceratozamia robusta in general appearance, whereas seedlings and young plants are closer to Ceratozamia santillanii.

Hybrids

No natural or garden hybrid has been documented.

Possible confusion

Two neighbouring species of northern Chiapas can cause confusion:

  • Ceratozamia robusta, to which the Selva El Ocote populations were first referred because of their location and the look of the adult plants; its young leaves are reddish brown or green depending on the individual;
  • Ceratozamia santillanii, a geographical neighbour whose seedlings and young plants look like those of Ceratozamia alba; the authors describe the two species as sister groups.

The whitish-green colour of the young leaves is the simplest way to tell them apart.

Taxonomy

Ceratozamia alba is accepted by The World List of Cycads and by the WCVP, which records its publication in Phytotaxa 666, page 265, in 2024. It was described in October 2024 by Miguel Angel Pérez-Farrera, Mauricio G. Martínez-Martínez, Gaspar Moreno-Méndez, Andrew Vovides and José Said Gutiérrez-Ortega. According to the abstract, the authors first thought these populations belonged to Ceratozamia robusta, then found that the young plants looked more like Ceratozamia santillanii, with which they describe a sister-group relationship. Morphometric comparisons with both species confirmed that the plants represented a new species. The name has never changed.

The species was described after the general phylogenetic studies of the genus and is therefore not included in them. The 2022 monograph of Ceratozamia does not deal with it either.

Etymology and history

The epithet comes from the Latin albus, “white”, and refers to the most distinctive feature of the species, the whitish-green colour of its young leaves. The type specimen, a female plant, was collected on 9 July 2023 by Miguel Angel Pérez-Farrera and Mauricio Martínez-Martínez (number 4396), in the mountains of northern Chiapas, within the Selva El Ocote Biosphere Reserve; the holotype is kept in the HEM herbarium, with isotypes at CHIP and XAL.

Photographic records are older than the description: The World List of Cycads holds one from 2019 and others from 2023. The herbarium specimens examined by the authors also go back to 2019 (Pérez-Farrera 3750, collected on 18 March 2019, HEM herbarium); the male specimen cited dates from 1 April 2023 (Pérez-Farrera and Martínez-Martínez 4354).

Common names

No vernacular name is known, in English or in Spanish. The species is too recently described, and too local, to have acquired one.

In the wild

Ceratozamia alba is known only from the Selva El Ocote Biosphere Reserve, in the mountains of northern Chiapas. A single population is known, made up of three groups of plants spread over about 0.1 km² (10 ha). The plant is a rock-dweller: it grows out of holes and cracks in karst limestone, in tropical rainforest, between 700 and 800 m.

In the tree layer, the authors list Platymiscium dimorphandrum, Dracaena americana, Neea psychotrioides and species of Oreopanax, Guarea, Trichilia, Eugenia, Ficus and Clusia; in the understorey, palms (Chamaedorea ernesti-augusti, Chamaedorea glaucifolia, Chamaedorea oblongata, Astrocaryum mexicanum and a Gaussia) and several Anthurium; among the epiphytes, Monstera acuminata. The precise locality has not been published, to discourage illegal collecting. The coning season is not described.

Conservation status and regulation

Ceratozamia alba has not been assessed by the IUCN (The World List of Cycads, version 2026.08.15). Its authors propose the category Critically Endangered (CR): only one population is known, with fewer than 50 adult plants in three groups over about 10 ha. The area is being turned into pasture and maize and coffee fields, and stone is quarried there for building. In their view, its presence in a biosphere reserve is therefore not enough to protect it, and they call for urgent conservation on site and for cultivation in botanic gardens through rescue and propagation programmes.

Like the whole genus, the species has been listed in CITES Appendix I since 1 August 1985, and in Annex A of Regulation (EC) No 338/97 in the European Union (annexes now set by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026). Species+ does not yet name it, but the listing covers the whole genus, seeds included (checked on 30 October 2026). International trade in wild-collected plants for commercial purposes is prohibited; only artificially propagated plants with the proper documents can be traded.

This general information does not replace the official texts: check the regulations in force before buying or exchanging plants, ask the seller for proof of origin, and prefer plants raised from cultivated seed.

Growing Ceratozamia alba

No cultivation is documented, in a botanic garden or by private growers, and no commercial offer has been recorded. The only guide is its natural habitat: karst rocks under tropical rainforest at about 700 to 800 m. No growing advice specific to the species has been published; the general advice on the genus page applies, with that reservation.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

No outdoor planting has been documented anywhere. In frost-free tropical or subtropical gardens, a shaded, sheltered spot in a very free-draining, stony soil would be the closest match to its habitat; this is a deduction from the habitat, not a tested result.

Container cultivation

No container-growing report has been published. A rock-dwelling forest plant of this size would need a deep pot, a coarse, free-draining compost, bright shade and warmth all year; again, this is inferred from the habitat.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

None documented. No botanic garden declares Ceratozamia alba to BGCI PlantSearch, and the online catalogue of Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida) has no entry for it (both checked on 30 October 2026). The authors call for it to be brought into botanic garden collections, which suggests that no institutional collection held it in 2024.

Propagation

No data. Like all Ceratozamia, the species has separate male and female plants, so propagation would be from fertilised seed. With a single wild population of fewer than 50 adults, collecting plants or seed from the wild would be unacceptable.

Pests and diseases

No pest or disease has been documented for this species.

Cold hardiness

There are no data on the tolerance of Ceratozamia alba to cold, heat or drought, and no minimum temperature has been published for its habitat.

Mediterranean and European experience

No cultivation has been reported in France or Italy, in the ground or in a pot. At the Jardin zoologique tropical in La Londe-les-Maures (Var, on the French Riviera), no observation of it has been published. For readers in Mediterranean-type climates such as California or southern Australia, it should be kept frost-free until data exist.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No cold record and no commercial hardiness rating have been published. In the UK it would need a heated glasshouse.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data exist for this species or for the genus. The risk is documented in other cycads: their tissues, and above all their seeds, contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a compound harmful to the liver and nervous system. Treat the plant as poisonous: keep seeds and leaves out of reach of children and animals, and wear gloves to handle seeds. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet straight away.

Traditional uses

No use has been documented.

Frequently asked questions

Why is it called alba?

From the Latin albus, “white”. Its young leaves emerge whitish green before turning bluish green and then light green or olive green.

Where does Ceratozamia alba grow?

Only in the Selva El Ocote Biosphere Reserve, in the mountains of northern Chiapas, Mexico, on karst rocks in tropical rainforest between 700 and 800 m.

How can I tell it from Ceratozamia robusta?

By the colour of the young leaves, whitish green, and by its bluish-green female cone. The adults look alike, but the two species differ in several leaf measurements.

Is it endangered?

Very probably. The IUCN has not yet assessed it, but its authors propose the category Critically Endangered: a single population of fewer than 50 adults is known.

Can it be grown?

Not as far as published records show. No botanic garden declares it, and any plant offered should come from artificial propagation and carry CITES documents.

Is it frost-hardy?

Nobody knows. There are no cultivation data, and the original description gives no climate figures for its habitat, a tropical rainforest at 700 to 800 m.
