Encephalartos brevifoliolatus is a South African cycad that now survives only in cultivation: in the wild it was known from just five to seven plants, all male, scattered along the Drakensberg escarpment in Limpopo province. This species of the genus Encephalartos forms trunks up to 2.5 m tall, sometimes in clumps, and stands out for its short, relatively broad, dark green leaflets with rolled-under margins. Described in 1996, it vanished from its habitat in less than ten years, taken by collectors: conservation officials removed the last damaged stems to safety in 2004. The IUCN lists it as Extinct in the Wild.

Key facts about Encephalartos brevifoliolatus

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos brevifoliolatus Vorster (1996), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15)
  • Former names and synonyms: no synonym; plants may have been collected before 1996 under the name Encephalartos laevifolius (SANBI)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Escarpment cycad (English, Species+ and SANBI)
  • Natural range: South Africa, Limpopo province, Drakensberg escarpment (locality withheld), about 1,300 to 1,500 m; Extinct in the Wild
  • IUCN status: Extinct in the Wild (EW), assessment dated May 2020 and published in 2022; listed as EW since 2006 according to SANBI
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I (whole genus); Annex A in the European Union; collecting wild cycads is prohibited in South Africa
  • Documented hardiness: no data, either from the wild or from cultivation (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, full sun, short grassland in very open Protea savanna, on quartzite-derived sandstone or cliffs
  • Habit and adult size: trunk up to 2.5 m tall and 25 to 30 cm thick, erect, leaning or hanging from cliffs, in clumps of up to six stems; leaves 80 to 90 cm long, possibly up to 1.2 m
  • In the UK, US and Australia: no botanic garden in these three countries has been found listing it
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; treat it as poisonous like all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos brevifoliolatus

Look first at the leaflets: short and relatively broad, 6 to 8 cm long and 10 to 12 mm wide, dark green, with smooth margins rolled under, and overlapping along the leaf. The original description by Piet Vorster (1996) gives these characters:

  • Habit: tree-like, unbranched but often forming clumps of up to six stems through basal suckers; stem erect, sometimes leaning or hanging over cliffs, up to 2.5 m tall and 25 to 30 cm thick, covered with fairly small old leaf bases, often blackened by grass fires
  • Crown: not woolly, but the scale leaves carry a thin whitish felt
  • Leaves: 80 to 90 cm long (one measurement of 1.2 m is given with a question mark), stiff, straight or slightly recurved at the tip; petiole without prickles, yellowish, 9 to 20 cm long, at first covered in whitish felt, later hairless; rachis yellowish
  • Leaflets: the basal ones hardly reduced and never turned into prickles; the middle ones overlapping towards the leaf tip, spaced 8 to 10 mm apart, inserted at about 45°, the two rows forming an angle of about 135°; dark green, very narrowly ovate, slightly sickle-shaped, with smooth, rolled margins and a sharp, pungent tip, finely ribbed beneath with 14 to 16 veins (sometimes 12); 60 to 80 mm long and 10 to 12 mm wide
  • Male cones: up to six per stem, stalkless, very narrowly ovoid, covered in a very short whitish felt, about 35 cm long and 6 to 7 cm across when dry
  • Female cones: unknown in the wild

Male cones seem to form and ripen within a few months in spring and early summer: by mid-May Vorster found only dry cones. In most related species, cones form in late summer and autumn.

Hybrids

No natural or garden hybrid has been reported. Vorster did consider whether the few known plants might be hybrids with Encephalartos laevifolius as one parent, because their leaflet width-to-length ratio lies outside anything known in this group of narrow-leaflet species, which includes Encephalartos laevifolius, Encephalartos lanatus, Encephalartos humilis, Encephalartos friderici-guilielmi and perhaps Encephalartos ghellinckii and Encephalartos cycadifolius. He rejected the idea for three reasons: no possible parent grows nearby; apart from its broader leaflets, the plant resembles no species outside this group; and its reproductive cycle is half a year out of step with the other members of the group, which makes natural hybridisation unlikely.

Possible confusion

The main confusion is with Encephalartos laevifolius. The two share almost identical male cones, thin, stiff, prickle-free petioles that are woolly when young, and narrow, smooth-edged leaflets finely ribbed beneath. According to Vorster, Encephalartos brevifoliolatus differs in its clearly broader and shorter leaflets with rolled margins, and in having more veins: (12–)14 to 16, against 10 to 12.

Vorster also mentions isolated populations in the north-eastern Drakensberg referred to Encephalartos laevifolius, with dark green rather than glaucous leaves and differently hairy cones, which he had known since 1969 but did not think worth separating. SANBI adds that plants of Encephalartos brevifoliolatus may have been removed from the wild before 1996 under the name Encephalartos laevifolius. A plant sold under either name without documented origin therefore cannot be identified with certainty.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos brevifoliolatus is an accepted name with no synonyms. Piet Vorster, of Stellenbosch University, described it in the South African Journal of Botany (62(1): 61–64), in a paper received in September 1995 and published in 1996 as “a new species from the Northern Province”, today Limpopo. The type, Vorster 2984, made up of leaves and male cone scales, was collected on 14 May 1990 at about 1,300 m; the holotype is at the National Herbarium in Pretoria (PRE) and an isotype at Kew (K). Two further leaf collections, Fourie 4002 and 4003, were examined. The locality has never been published.

The description is based on male plants alone, since no female cone was known. The World List of Cycads, the checklist of the IUCN Cycad Specialist Group, accepts the species in its version of 15 August 2026.

Etymology and history

The name comes from the Latin brevis, “short”, and foliola, “leaflets”, a reference to the shorter leaflets that distinguish it from Encephalartos laevifolius (Haynes, 2022, cited by The World List of Cycads).

The species was found during surveys by staff of the former Transvaal Chief Directorate of Nature and Environmental Conservation. Vorster gave full credit for the discovery to S.P. Fourie, who located the plants, collected specimens and took him to the site. The plants had been visited regularly since 1989. Despite a careful aerial search, only five widely scattered plants were found, with no sign of a female plant or of seedlings. As early as 1996, Vorster noted that the species was already in at least one private collection, but in no scientific collection, and he advised against removing the plants from the wild as long as they could be protected there.

What followed was swift. According to SANBI, collectors removed most of the plants in the following years, leaving only a few damaged stems, which conservation officials took out in 2004 and placed in safe collections. The species has been classed as Extinct in the Wild since 2006, and helicopter surveys over the last decade have found no other plants.

Common names

The English name Escarpment cycad is used by Species+ and by SANBI, after the Drakensberg escarpment where the plants grew. No Afrikaans or other South African name specific to the species is confirmed by a reference source.

In the wild

Encephalartos brevifoliolatus no longer exists in the wild. When it was discovered it grew on the Drakensberg escarpment in Limpopo province, over a few square kilometres of communal land according to SANBI, which puts the known area at about 50 km². The plants grew in short grassland in very open Protea savanna, on sandstone derived from quartzite or on cliffs, in full sun, between 1,300 and 1,500 m. Blackened leaf bases showed that grass fires passed through regularly.

Vorster stressed that this region holds several relict and endemic Encephalartos, and that several of them were discovered because of the illegal activities of amateur collectors. Nothing is known about the pollinators of the species.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos brevifoliolatus is listed as Extinct in the Wild (EW) on the IUCN Red List. The assessment, by J. D. Bösenberg, is dated May 2020 and was published in 2022, according to The World List of Cycads. SANBI states that the species has been EW since 2006. It is one of five Encephalartos in this category.

According to SANBI's 2023 document, updated in 2025, the known population numbered five to seven male plants and was collected to extinction in 2003–2004. The wild-collected plants survive in collections, with no reliable register; research to trace them is under way. A single plant is said to have produced a female cone in cultivation, and South African nurserymen claim to own females. SANBI considers it very likely that all large plants in collections are of illegal wild origin and that international trade contributed to the loss of the species. It also cites harvesting for traditional medicine (Rogers and Pillay, 2006). Reintroduction is possible in principle, but plants put back into the wild would be at risk of theft.

Legal trade exists: 88 live plants were exported from South Africa between 1997 and 2020, and a single South African nursery is registered with CITES for this species. SANBI recommends exports only of seedlings from registered nurseries, raised from legally held parent plants, with part of the seedlings set aside for the recovery of the species.

Like the whole genus, Encephalartos brevifoliolatus has been listed in CITES Appendix I since 4 February 1977, without annotation, so plants, seeds and pollen are all covered. Commercial international trade in wild-collected plants is prohibited; only artificially propagated plants can be exported, from registered nurseries and with the required permits. In the European Union the species is in Annex A of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383. Since 7 February 2013 the EU's Scientific Review Group has held a negative opinion on imports of artificially propagated plants of any size from South Africa. These details come from Species+, the official CITES and EU database, consulted in October 2026; check the current rules before any purchase or import, and ask for proof that the plant was propagated in cultivation.

Growing Encephalartos brevifoliolatus

Almost nothing is published on growing Encephalartos brevifoliolatus: the few existing plants are in private or conservation collections, and no published source describes how they are grown. Its native habitat, high-altitude grassland in full sun on poor, free-draining soil, gives only general pointers: a very bright position, a mineral, well-drained compost and measured watering.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

No in-ground planting outside South Africa is documented. A member of the French Fous de palmiers forum reported in 2011 that he had seen plants of the species in a South African garden, without further detail. Any outdoor trial would mean exposing a plant that is extinct in the wild to unknown conditions, which few owners are likely to risk.

Container cultivation

No container-growing data are published either. As with other rare Encephalartos of high grassland, a deep pot of gritty, free-draining compost, full light and a dry, frost-free winter are the cautious defaults; these are general practices for the genus, not observations made on this species.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

No botanic garden in Britain, the United States or Australia has been found listing the species: it is absent from the online catalogue of Montgomery Botanical Center in Florida, and no garden in these countries declares it in BGCI PlantSearch, the global database of botanic garden collections (October 2026). Plants may exist in private collections, but none is documented.

Propagation

Encephalartos brevifoliolatus can today be propagated only by suckers, since the species forms clumps of up to six stems, and in theory by seed, should a female plant ever produce fertile seed. SANBI mentions a single plant that has produced a female cone in cultivation. SANBI also considers it very unlikely that past exports met the CITES requirements on the legal origin of the parent plants. Ask for full documentation before buying.

Pests and diseases

No disease or pest specific to Encephalartos brevifoliolatus is documented. Plants taken from the wild often die: SANBI notes that the mortality of removed cycads is high, depending on their size, the care they receive and repeated transplanting.

Cold hardiness

There are no hardiness data for Encephalartos brevifoliolatus, either from the wild or from cultivation. The original description mentions neither frost nor snow in its habitat.

Mediterranean and European experience

No plant of the species is recorded in cultivation in France or elsewhere in the Mediterranean basin, and no observation exists for any climate zone. On the French Fous de palmiers forum the species is sometimes listed among the narrow-leaflet Encephalartos thought to be hardier, but without any observation to back it up.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No dated report exists from the English-speaking world either. The 1,300 to 1,500 m altitude of the habitat is not enough to infer a lowest temperature. Survival in cold conditions among Encephalartos often concerns young plants whose caudex is still underground, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil; an adult with a 2.5 m trunk above ground has no such protection. No USDA zone has been published for the species. Given how rare the plants are, no cold trial is advisable anyway.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data are published for Encephalartos brevifoliolatus itself, but the risk is documented in the genus: the toxicity of Encephalartos horridus was demonstrated as early as 1944 by Van der Walt, and Encephalartos, like other cycads, contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; the seeds are the most dangerous part. The ASPCA reports vomiting, diarrhoea, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses that have eaten cycads.

Treat the plant as poisonous: wear gloves to clean seeds, pick up any that fall, and keep plants and seeds away from children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

SANBI reports that Encephalartos brevifoliolatus was reportedly harvested for traditional medicine, citing Rogers and Pillay (2006), without saying how it was used. In South Africa cycads have cultural importance, and harvesters do not distinguish between species. No other use is documented.

Frequently asked questions

Does Encephalartos brevifoliolatus still exist?

Yes, but only in cultivation. The species is extinct in the wild: the last stems were removed from the wild in 2004. Wild-collected plants and their suckers survive in a few collections, and 88 plants have been exported legally since 1997.

Why are there only male plants?

Because no female plant was ever found in the wild. The five to seven known plants were all male, and no seedlings were seen. According to SANBI, a single cultivated plant is said to have produced a female cone.

How do I tell it from Encephalartos laevifolius?

By the leaflets. Those of Encephalartos brevifoliolatus are shorter and broader (60 to 80 mm by 10 to 12 mm), with rolled margins and 14 to 16 veins instead of 10 to 12. The male cones of the two species are almost identical.

Can I buy one?

In principle yes, if the plant was propagated in cultivation and is documented. The species is in CITES Appendix I and in Annex A in the European Union, which has held a negative opinion on imports from South Africa since 2013. Be wary of any large plant or seedling without proven origin.

Is it frost-hardy?

Nobody knows. No observation of frost has been published for this species, and claims of hardiness found online rest on no trial.

Why did it disappear so quickly?

Because of collectors. Described in 1996 from five to seven plants, it was removed down to the last plant between 2003 and 2004, even though its locality was kept secret. SANBI believes international trade contributed to its loss.
