Ceratozamia popolucana is a cycad of southern Veracruz, in Mexico, discovered in the Los Tuxtlas Biosphere Reserve, where it grows in tropical rainforest on limestone. Described in 2026, it is the most recently named species of the genus Ceratozamia, and its name honours the Popoluca, an Indigenous people of the region. Its populations were at first taken for Ceratozamia subroseophylla or Ceratozamia dominguezii. Its new leaves, cherry red when they emerge, set it apart from every other species of the genus. Its authors consider it Critically Endangered; the IUCN has not yet assessed it.

How to recognise Ceratozamia popolucana

The surest sign is the colour of the new leaves: in Ceratozamia popolucana they emerge cherry red, covered in a yellowish to whitish down, a trait its authors describe as unique in the genus. The 2026 description compares it with its two neighbours in Los Tuxtlas and Uxpanapa, Ceratozamia subroseophylla and Ceratozamia dominguezii:

  • Trunk: shorter and thinner than in both neighbours.
  • Leaves: shorter than in both neighbours; cherry red when they emerge.
  • Leaflets: olive green, as in Ceratozamia dominguezii, whereas those of Ceratozamia subroseophylla are glossy dark green.
  • Female cones: light green when they emerge, whereas they are yellow with a reddish down in Ceratozamia subroseophylla and yellow with a light orange down in Ceratozamia dominguezii.
  • Male cones: the authors also report clear differences in the male cones.

Detailed measurements of the trunk, leaves, cones and seeds are given in the original description. A plant without new leaves or cones is hard to tell from its neighbours.

Hybrids

No natural or garden hybrid has been documented.

Possible confusion

Its own authors first took these plants for one of two species that share its region and the general look of the adults:

  • Ceratozamia subroseophylla, described from Los Tuxtlas and also found at Uxpanapa and in Tabasco: a larger plant, with a trunk up to 2.5 m (exceptionally 5 m) according to the 2022 monograph, glossy dark green leaflets and young female cones yellow with a reddish down.
  • Ceratozamia dominguezii, from the forests on karst of Uxpanapa: a larger plant, with olive-green leaflets and young female cones with a light orange down.

Without cherry-red new leaves or cones, origin is the only reliable clue: a cultivated plant can be attributed to Ceratozamia popolucana only if its wild origin in Los Tuxtlas is documented.

Taxonomy

Ceratozamia popolucana is accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15), which attributes the name to Pérez-Farrera, Gutiérrez-Ortega and Vovides. It was described in June 2026 in the journal Phytotaxa (760(1): 77–92; name published on page 83, figures 4–9), in an article by Miguel Ángel Pérez-Farrera, Andrew Vovides, Pedro Díaz Jiménez, Mauricio G. Martínez-Martínez, María Ydelia Sánchez-Tinoco, Gaspar Moreno-Méndez and José Said Gutiérrez-Ortega.

The WCVP, the database behind POWO (read via GBIF on 30 October 2026), does not yet list the name: it accepts 43 species of Ceratozamia, against 45 for The World List of Cycads, which also keeps Ceratozamia dominguezii as a separate species. The difference reflects the date of the description rather than a taxonomic disagreement; no source has questioned the species so far.

Why a new species?

According to its authors, these populations were first thought to belong to Ceratozamia subroseophylla or Ceratozamia dominguezii, because of their location and the resemblance of the adult plants. Clear differences in the female and male cones, the shape of the trunk and the size of the leaves, observed in wild populations over several years, led them to describe a new species.

The description arrives in a disputed context: the 2022 monograph of the genus placed Ceratozamia dominguezii in synonymy under Ceratozamia subroseophylla, which The World List of Cycads does not follow. The 2026 description compares the new species with both, without taking sides.

Type

The holotype was collected on 4 October 2021 in the municipality of Hueyapan de Ocampo, Veracruz, by M.A. Pérez-Farrera and P. Díaz-Jiménez (no. 4069). It is held at the Eizi Matuda herbarium (HEM) in Tuxtla Gutiérrez, with an isotype at the XAL herbarium in Xalapa.

Etymology and history

The epithet honours the Popoluca Indigenous culture of the Los Tuxtlas region of Veracruz (The World List of Cycads, quoting the original description).

No source documents the introduction of the species into cultivation. The World List of Cycads shows photographs of the species taken in Veracruz in 2023 and 2025 by two of the authors of the description.

Common names

No common name is recorded for this species, in English or in any other language. The 2022 monograph records a Popoluca name, “hymniom pekmuk”, for Ceratozamia subroseophylla in Veracruz; no source says whether it is also applied to the plants now placed in Ceratozamia popolucana.

In the wild

Ceratozamia popolucana is known from the Los Tuxtlas Biosphere Reserve, in southern Veracruz; the type comes from the municipality of Hueyapan de Ocampo. The keywords of its description refer to tropical rainforest on karst, that is, on limestone. Its authors describe a very narrow range with a limited number of populations.

According to the 2022 monograph, Ceratozamia subroseophylla grows in the same region, on volcanic soils and karst rocks in evergreen tropical rainforest between 111 and 1,050 m. No study of the pollination or cone calendar of Ceratozamia popolucana has been published.

Conservation status and regulation

Ceratozamia popolucana has not been assessed on the IUCN Red List (The World List of Cycads, version 2026.08.15; no category in the Red List data distributed through GBIF, checked on 30 October 2026). Its authors propose a preliminary listing as Critically Endangered under criterion B2ab(iii): a very small area of occupancy, few locations and continuing habitat destruction. This proposal has no official standing until the IUCN publishes an assessment.

Like the whole genus, it has been covered by CITES Appendix I since 1 August 1985 (the genus had been in Appendix II since 1977), and by Annex A of Regulation (EC) No 338/97 in the European Union (annexes now set by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026), seeds included. Species+ does not list the species under its own name, since it was described long after the CITES checklist of cycads, but the listing covers the whole genus (“Ceratozamia spp.”) and therefore applies to it (checked on 30 October 2026). International trade in wild-collected plants for commercial purposes is banned; only artificially propagated plants with the required permits can be traded.

This general information does not replace the official texts: check the regulations in force before buying, importing or exchanging plants, and ask for proof of origin.

Growing Ceratozamia popolucana

No growing advice specific to Ceratozamia popolucana has been published, and no source records it in cultivation.

Its habitat, a tropical rainforest on limestone, points to warmth, partial shade and a free-draining, humus-rich mix. These pointers are drawn from the habitat and have not been confirmed by published trials.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

Only frost-free, warm and humid climates could suit it outdoors, such as southern Florida, Hawaii or tropical Queensland. No outdoor planting has been documented anywhere.

Container cultivation

If plants of documented origin become available, a deep pot with a free-draining mix rich in organic matter, warmth and bright shade would follow the general practice for tropical Ceratozamia; no trial specific to the species exists.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

No garden in the United Kingdom, the United States or Australia declares Ceratozamia popolucana: BGCI PlantSearch lists no garden under this name, and the online catalogue of Montgomery Botanical Center, in Florida, has no accession under it (both queried on 30 October 2026).

Propagation

No data exist for the species. Like all Ceratozamia, it has separate male and female plants; it is propagated from fresh seed after pollination, by hand in cultivation. Handle seeds wearing gloves.

Pests and diseases

No pest or disease specific to the species has been documented. The usual risks for Ceratozamia in cultivation are rot in cold, sodden soil and scale insects.

Cold hardiness

No cold-hardiness data exist for Ceratozamia popolucana, described in 2026. It is a plant of tropical rainforest, to be kept frost-free until documented trials exist.

Mediterranean and European experience

No cultivation result has been published in Europe, and the Jardin zoologique tropical in La Londe-les-Maures (Var, on the French Riviera) has published no observation of this species.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No dated cold record and no commercial hardiness rating have been published. In the UK it would be a plant for a heated glasshouse.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity study specific to Ceratozamia popolucana has been published. The risk is well documented in other cycads: their tissues, and above all their seeds, contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a compound harmful to the liver and nervous system. Dogs, livestock and people can be seriously poisoned. In the absence of evidence to the contrary, treat the plant as poisonous: keep seeds and leaves out of reach of children and animals, wear gloves to clean seeds, and if any part is swallowed, call a poison centre, a doctor or a vet straight away.

Traditional uses

No use is recorded for this species. The 2022 monograph reports, for Ceratozamia subroseophylla in Veracruz, a medicinal tea made by boiling the trunk, used against kidney stones; nothing indicates that this use also concerns Ceratozamia popolucana.

Frequently asked questions

Where does the name popolucana come from?

From the Popoluca. The epithet honours the Indigenous culture of the Los Tuxtlas region of Veracruz, where the species was discovered.

Where does it grow?

In the Los Tuxtlas Biosphere Reserve, in southern Veracruz, Mexico. It lives in tropical rainforest on limestone, in a very small area.

How can I recognise Ceratozamia popolucana?

By its new leaves, which emerge cherry red with a yellowish to whitish down. Its trunk and leaves are also smaller than those of its neighbours, and its young female cones are light green.

How does it differ from Ceratozamia subroseophylla?

By its size and colours. Ceratozamia subroseophylla is a larger plant, with glossy dark green leaflets and young female cones that are yellow with a reddish down; Ceratozamia popolucana has olive-green leaflets and light green young cones.

Why is it missing from POWO?

Because it is very recent. Described in June 2026, it is accepted by The World List of Cycads but was not yet listed in the WCVP, the database behind POWO, when checked on 30 October 2026.

Is it endangered?

Yes, according to its authors, who propose Critically Endangered. The IUCN has not yet assessed it; its range is very small and its habitat is being destroyed.

Can I buy one?

No source records it in cultivation. The whole genus is listed in CITES Appendix I: only artificially propagated plants with their documents can be traded.

Can it take frost?

Nobody knows, and it is unlikely. No trial exists; it is a plant of tropical rainforest, to be kept frost-free.
