Encephalartos manikensis is a green-leaved cycad with a medium-sized trunk, native to the border country of eastern Zimbabwe and western Mozambique, in the Manica region. This species of the genus Encephalartos was first described in 1938 as a variety of another species, under the name Encephalartos gratus var. manikensis, before being raised to species rank in 1939. It gave its name to the “manikensis complex”, a group of closely related cycads that two South African botanists divided into several species in 1969. It is listed as Vulnerable by the IUCN (2022 assessment), mainly because of collecting for gardens. Nurseries describe it as a fast-growing green cycad in frost-free climates, but the only cold report shows damage after a single light frost.

Key facts about Encephalartos manikensis

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos manikensis (Gilliland) Gilliland (1939), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and IPNI
  • Former names and synonyms: Encephalartos gratus var. manikensis Gilliland (1938), the original name
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Gorongowe cycad and Manica cycad (Flora of Zimbabwe); Gorongowe cycad and Gorongo cycad (Species+); muTete in Chimanyika (Gilliland, 1938)
  • Natural range: Mozambique (Manica province) and Zimbabwe (north and east of the country)
  • IUCN status: Vulnerable (VU A2acd), 2022 assessment (Bösenberg) cited by The World List of Cycads
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I since 4 February 1977, like the whole genus; Annex A in the European Union; EU opinion on imports of artificially propagated plants from South Africa positive up to a 7 cm caudex (2011), negative above (2013)
  • Documented hardiness: one incomplete report: foliage damaged after a single night at −1 °C (30.2 °F) in Apulia, Italy, in December 2009 (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: on Mount Gorongowe, open Uapaca and Brachystegia woodland on the lower slopes, then rocky slopes where the species dominates, at about 4,500 ft (about 1,370 m) (Gilliland, 1938); in southern California, sun on the coast and part sun inland, shade in desert areas, a free-draining soil (Jungle Music)
  • Habit and adult size: trunks up to 1.5 m tall and 30 cm across, branching at the base with age; leaves 1 to 1.9 m long with about sixty pairs of leaflets; male cones 25 to 60 cm, female cone 30 to 45 cm (Dyer and Verdoorn, 1969, followed by the Flora of Zimbabwe)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Montgomery Botanical Center (13 accessions, 1964 to 2006), Fairchild, the Huntington, Lotusland and a dozen other gardens in the United States; Brisbane, Melbourne, Adelaide, Cairns and Townsville in Australia (BGCI); no British garden declares it
  • Toxicity: not studied for the species; documented risk for the genus (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos manikensis

Look for large, straight green leaves carrying about sixty pairs of broad, toothed leaflets on a trunk that seldom exceeds 1.5 m. The description below is that of Dyer and Verdoorn (1969), which the Flora of Zimbabwe, an online flora based on Flora Zambesiaca and the regional literature, reproduces word for word:

  • Habit: the plant branches at the base with age; the trunks reach 1.5 m in height and about 30 cm in diameter, protected by alternating series of leaf bases and scale leaves
  • Leaves: narrowly ovate to oblong in outline, 100 to 190 cm long, with about 60 pairs of leaflets, the lowest pairs reduced to prickles; petiole and rachis woolly when young, then hairless
  • Leaflets: the largest 12 to 15 cm long and 2 to 2.5 cm wide, lanceolate, ending in a forward-pointing spine, with one or two spines on each margin
  • Male cones: one to four, 25 to 60 cm tall and 8 to 15 cm across, on a stalk of up to about 15 cm and 5 cm thick; scales almost rectangular, 4 to 6 cm long and 3.5 to 5.8 cm wide, slightly separating when ripe
  • Female cone: about 30 to 45 cm by 20 to 25 cm, on a loosely woolly stalk of about 6 cm; scales about 5.5 by 4 cm
  • Seeds: more or less ovoid, 2.5 to 3.5 cm long and 1.5 to 2 cm across

Dyer and Verdoorn stress an unusual feature: male and female cones look so alike from the outside that a male cone is easily taken for a female one; male plants carry one to four cones, females fairly often two. In their key to the male cones, the species is recognised by scales as broad as they are long, 4 to 5 cm across at the shoulders, spreading almost at right angles, with a blade truncate or heart-shaped at the base and a margin free of pollen sacs for 2 to 3 mm.

Gilliland's original description (1938) gave smaller dimensions, based only on the plants of Mount Gorongowe: plants 0.9 to 2.4 m tall, trunk 0.9 to 1.2 m in males and only 10 to 25 cm in females, 30 cm across; leaves 0.9 to 1.2 m; median leaflets 12.5 to 15 cm by 2 to 2.5 cm; male cones almost stalkless (stalk 2.5 to 4 cm), hairless, 23 to 30 cm by 7.5 to 10 cm; the female cone was then unknown. The 1969 figures also cover plants from Mozambique.

The online compilation LLIFLE adds that the plant suckers freely and forms clumps, that female cones sometimes approach a metre in length, and that male cones are so large they are hard to tell from female cones. The resemblance of the cones and the branching at the base are confirmed by Dyer and Verdoorn; female cones of nearly a metre are not (45 cm at most according to them). Jungle Music, a nursery in southern California, describes olive to dark green leaves about two metres long, fairly soft spines, stems either pale and finely hairy or dark with woody scales, and bluish green cones of both sexes.

Hybrids

No hybrid of Encephalartos manikensis has been described.

Possible confusion

The oldest confusion is with Encephalartos gratus, of southern Malawi and the Zambézia province of Mozambique, of which it was first treated as a variety. According to LLIFLE, the simplest character is the absence of fine hairs on the ripe cone scales of manikensis; Dyer and Verdoorn (1969) agree: the more robust Encephalartos gratus has longer and broader median leaflets (up to 26 cm by 2.3 to 3.5 cm), with two to seven spines on the upper margin, and relatively small cone scales with a characteristic dark down on the outer face. Gilliland (1938) had already separated his variety by its shorter leaflets, its almost stalkless male cone and the absence of flocculent down. A grower in Nîmes, in southern France, reports on the Fous de palmiers forum in July 2010 that he had two young seed-grown plants identified: the one with smaller leaves was a manikensis, the other a gratus, which shows how hard seedlings are to tell apart without comparison.

Encephalartos manikensis is also confused with the other members of its complex, in particular Encephalartos pterogonus and Encephalartos munchii, whose leaves are similar; these species are separated first by the scales of the male cones (see Taxonomy). On the same forum, a grower found in 2011 that the leaves of a young plant brought back from Réunion recalled both Encephalartos longifolius and manikensis: without cones, identification often remains uncertain.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos manikensis is accepted by The World List of Cycads and IPNI. H.B. Gilliland first published the plant in 1938 in the Journal of South African Botany (4: 153), as a variety of Encephalartos gratus. He raised it to species rank the following year in the Proceedings of the Rhodesia Scientific Association (37: 133–134), in a note entitled “A note on the Rhodesian Cycad”; IPNI specifies that this publication includes an amended description. The World List of Cycads cites no type specimen distinct from that of the variety.

The 1938 type is Gilliland's collection 2016, made in July 1937 on Mount Gorongowe, above the Nyamkwarara valley (“Numkwarara” in the text), where the plant dominated the vegetation of the rocks at about 4,500 ft. Dyer and Verdoorn (1969) add that this locality lies in the Stapleford Forest Reserve, in what was then Rhodesia, and that Gilliland, who at first had only a male cone, could describe the female cone in 1939 only from a damaged specimen. In practice, a label reading “Encephalartos gratus var. manikensis” refers to this species.

In 1969 R.A. Dyer and I.C. Verdoorn published in Kirkia (7: 147–158) a paper entitled “Encephalartos manikensis and its near allies”. They described several new species, until then included in a broadly defined manikensis: Encephalartos chimanimaniensis, Encephalartos concinnus, Encephalartos munchii and Encephalartos pterogonus, all accepted today by The World List of Cycads. According to LLIFLE, these species differ mainly in their male cones: thick scales in manikensis, smaller cones in concinnus, curved scales in chimanimaniensis, blue-green cones in munchii and scales drawn out into wings in pterogonus.

This division is still debated. Kew's fact sheet on the Mount Muruwere-Bossa Important Plant Area reports that some authors, including P. Capela (2006), regard Encephalartos pterogonus as a mere form of the manikensis complex; Kew nevertheless keeps the species, accepted by most authorities. Jungle Music also notes that some growers see up to five species or forms in the “manikensis alliance”. No genetic study of the complex has been published.

Etymology and history

The epithet manikensis refers to the Manica region. According to Haynes (2022), cited by The World List of Cycads, it refers to the province of Manica in Mozambique, where the species was discovered; the Flora of Zimbabwe speaks more broadly of the Manica districts of eastern Zimbabwe and western Mozambique, where it grows. The title of Gilliland's note, “the Rhodesian Cycad”, recalls that the species was described in former Rhodesia, now Zimbabwe.

The 1938 description tells the story of the discovery. Rumours of a cycad on the eastern border of Rhodesia reached Gilliland early in 1934; a year later, while he was directing locust control in the Nyamkwarara valley, his men's descriptions of the “muTete” convinced him that it grew on the slopes of Mount Gorongowe. In July 1937 he crossed the valley with the botanical expedition of the University of the Witwatersrand and sent two students, H. Janse van Rensburg and I.L. Johnston, up the mountain: they came back in the evening with leaves, male cones, young plants, seeds and photographs. An article devoted to the species was published in the journal of the Cycad Society of South Africa (Osborne, 1994, Encephalartos 38).

Common names

Gilliland (1938) gives the local name “muTete”, in Chimanyika, the language of the Manyika people, used by his men for the plant. The Flora of Zimbabwe and the Flora of Mozambique give two English names, Gorongowe cycad and Manica cycad; Species+ gives Gorongowe cycad and Gorongo cycad, and an archive of the Virtual Cycad Encyclopedia of the Palm and Cycad Societies of Florida (1998–2000) also uses Gorongowe cycad. The name “Rhodesian cycad” appears only in compilations.

In the wild

Encephalartos manikensis grows on both sides of the border between Zimbabwe and Mozambique. The World List of Cycads records it in Manica province, Mozambique, and in Zimbabwe; the Flora of Zimbabwe gives the northern and eastern divisions of the country, where it calls it rare, and the Flora of Mozambique the Manica-Sofala division, where it is described as local.

Gilliland (1938) describes the habitat of the type locality: on the lower slopes of Mount Gorongowe the plant grows in open Uapaca and Brachystegia woodland; higher up, it becomes dominant on the rocky slopes. According to local people, it bears seed in the rainy season (December and January); males seemed more numerous than females, and young plants were scattered. Dyer and Verdoorn (1969) also refer to the species plants from several localities in Mozambique (Bandula, Garuso, Vanduzi, Nhaungue, Chinyazange), noting that H.B. Christian saw at least two other species among them.

LLIFLE, which cites the 2010 IUCN assessment, gives altitudes of 600 to 1,400 m, grassy slopes of granite hills, often in full sun among large boulders, and wooded valleys, with a long list of localities from Mount Selinda to Mount Darwin in Zimbabwe and from Garuso to Mount Chinhazanza in Mozambique, most plants being in the Mapande range. These data could not be checked against the assessment itself. Jungle Music speaks of grassland, granite slopes and mountain forest with moderate to heavy rainfall.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos manikensis is Vulnerable (VU) on the IUCN Red List under criterion A2acd, according to the 2022 assessment by Bösenberg cited by The World List of Cycads: a past decline of the population, measured by direct observation, loss of habitat and collecting. GBIF, which relays the Red List, gives the same category. National statuses quoted by the online floras differ: the Flora of Zimbabwe gives “endangered” (Zimbabwe Red List, 2002) and the Flora of Mozambique “lower risk, near threatened”.

The Flora of Zimbabwe states that in 1995 there were an estimated 5,000 plants in the wild, that several local extinctions are known, and that the species is heavily threatened by collectors, but also by forestry plantations and agriculture. LLIFLE gives fewer than 10,000 mature individuals, with some still large subpopulations in Mozambique; this figure has not been verified.

Regulation, according to Species+ (consulted through its public API on 26 October 2026):

  • CITES: Appendix I since 4 February 1977, like the whole genus; international trade in wild-collected plants for commercial purposes is prohibited;
  • European Union: Annex A, current list in Regulation (EU) 2026/1383; for imports of artificially propagated plants from South Africa (source codes A and D), the EU Scientific Review Group gave a positive opinion for plants with a caudex of up to 7 cm in diameter (20 June 2011) and a negative opinion above that size (7 February 2013), both still current; past suspensions of trade with Mozambique, decided for the whole family, are no longer in force;
  • Zimbabwe and Mozambique: national statuses as above; no specific legal protection is documented.

These notes do not replace the official texts: check Species+ and EUR-Lex before any purchase or import, and ask for documents proving that a plant was propagated in cultivation.

Growing Encephalartos manikensis

Encephalartos manikensis is one of the more forgiving Encephalartos in frost-free climates, according to the commercial and compiled sources, though no scientific source describes its cultivation. LLIFLE presents it as one of the fastest-growing cycads: a seedling would make a plant with one-metre leaves in four to five years, and the first cones would appear at about eight to ten years, given a well-drained soil, no frost and regular watering in the dry season. It also says it is the cycad most commonly grown in Zimbabwe, even in the streets of Harare. These statements have not been confirmed by an independent source.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

In southern California, Jungle Music describes it as a fast-growing species suited to full sun on the coast and part sun inland, needing shade or filtered light in desert areas, and preferring a soil that drains well while being fairly tolerant of water, like many of the Central African species; the nursery adds that the plant needs room for its long leaves and stout trunk, and that it grows well in the ground. Planting out is only realistic where frost is absent or very rare: a single night at −1 °C (30.2 °F) was enough to damage its leaves in southern Italy (see Cold hardiness).

Container cultivation

In temperate climates, the species is grown in collections under glass. A grower in Agen (Lot-et-Garonne, south-west France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his manikensis produced new leaves in March and April 2010 in a conservatory kept at 20 °C (68 °F), and a grower in the Deux-Sèvres (western France) reports new leaves in October 2010 on a young plant grown indoors, after watering more generously. A grower in Naples reports on the Italian forum Tropicamente in June 2010 that seedlings with a 3 cm caudex, in 10 cm pots of peat and perlite, spent their first winter indoors without trouble before being put outside in full sun. On the Fous de palmiers forum, manikensis was cited in 2009 among the Encephalartos most often offered for sale in Europe, mainly as seed, together with Encephalartos ituriensis, Encephalartos whitelockii and gratus.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

BGCI PlantSearch lists 40 institutions holding the species. In the United States they include the Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida), whose online catalogue shows 13 accessions received between 1964 and 2006, Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden, Naples Botanical Garden, Marie Selby Botanical Gardens and the Edison and Ford Winter Estates in Florida; the Huntington, Ganna Walska Lotusland, Descanso Gardens, the San Diego Botanic Garden, the two gardens of San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance and the Fullerton Arboretum in California; the Honolulu Botanical Gardens, the National Tropical Botanical Garden and the University of Hawaii at Hilo; and the national plant germplasm system at Beltsville. Jungle Music, near San Diego, usually has “at least a hundred” plants of the species in stock. In Australia, BGCI lists the Brisbane Botanic Gardens Mount Coot-tha, the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria (Melbourne Gardens), the System Garden of the University of Melbourne, the Botanic Garden of Adelaide, the Cairns Botanic Gardens and James Cook University in Townsville. No British garden declares the species. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not a living plant on display today.

Propagation

Propagation is by seed; seed of the species circulates in the European trade, according to the Fous de palmiers forum (2009–2013), and Jungle Music sells plants from seedlings to coning size. Basal suckers, which mature plants produce sparingly according to Jungle Music, also allow vegetative propagation. No protocol specific to the species has been published; the general methods for sowing cycads apply.

Pests and diseases

No data specific to Encephalartos manikensis have been published. Like other cycads, it is mainly at risk of root and trunk rot in waterlogged soil.

Cold hardiness

Encephalartos manikensis is not frost-hardy, according to the only report available: a single night just below freezing damaged its foliage.

Mediterranean and European experience

A grower in Nardò (Apulia, southern Italy) reports on the Tropicamente forum that his plants of Encephalartos manikensis had their foliage damaged in December 2009 after a single night at −1 °C (30.2 °F), which came between two days close to 20 °C (68 °F); he attributes the damage to the sudden drop in temperature. The same episode affected Encephalartos pterogonus, Encephalartos concinnus, Encephalartos chimanimaniensis and Encephalartos munchii, while Encephalartos sclavoi, Encephalartos kisambo and Encephalartos hildebrandtii came through unharmed. Whether the plants were in pots or in the ground, and whether they were protected, is not stated. In France, the forum reports concern plants grown in conservatories, glasshouses or indoors, with no cold value. In Italy, the Naples botanic garden held three plants of Zimbabwean and Mozambican origin in 1993–1994 (Delpinoa, 1997), and the Messina botanic garden listed the species in 2009; whether they were grown outdoors is not stated. This experience is relevant to growers in comparable climates, such as coastal California or southern Australia: even a brief light frost should be avoided.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No measured value has been published for these countries. LLIFLE recommends frost-free conditions, and Jungle Music gives no temperature. The plants declared by gardens in Florida, Hawaii, southern California and tropical Queensland grow in frost-free or almost frost-free climates. No USDA zone is documented for the species in a reliable source. In Encephalartos, survivals in cold areas often concern young plants with an underground caudex, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil; an adult plant with an aerial trunk does not benefit from this protection.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data exist for Encephalartos manikensis itself, but the risk is documented for the genus. The toxicity of Encephalartos was demonstrated for Encephalartos horridus as early as 1944 (Van der Walt), and Encephalartos contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; these substances affect the liver, the digestive tract and the nervous system, and the seeds are the most dangerous part. For another cycad, the sago palm (Cycas revoluta), the ASPCA reports vomiting, black stools, jaundice, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses.

Wear gloves to handle seeds and their fleshy coat, keep plants and seeds out of reach of children and pets, and if any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use specific to Encephalartos manikensis is documented. Gilliland (1938) records only its local name, muTete, used by the people of the Nyamkwarara valley.

Frequently asked questions

Is Encephalartos manikensis a variety of Encephalartos gratus?

No, not since 1939. It was described in 1938 as Encephalartos gratus var. manikensis, then raised to species rank by the same author the following year.

What is the manikensis complex?

A group of closely related species from the border between Zimbabwe and Mozambique. Dyer and Verdoorn separated four species from it in 1969 (chimanimaniensis, concinnus, munchii and pterogonus), mainly on the male cones; the division is still debated.

Where does the name come from?

From the Manica region. It extends on both sides of the border between Mozambique and Zimbabwe, where the species grows.

Is Encephalartos manikensis endangered?

Yes, it is Vulnerable. The IUCN listed it as such in 2022; collecting for gardens is the main threat, together with forestry plantations and agriculture.

Is Encephalartos manikensis frost-hardy?

No, according to the only report available. A single night at −1 °C (30.2 °F) damaged its foliage in Apulia in 2009; in frost-prone climates it should be grown under glass.

How can it be told from Encephalartos gratus?

Mainly by the cones. The ripe cone scales of manikensis lack the fine hairs and dark down of gratus, which also has longer, broader median leaflets; seedlings are hard to separate.

Is Encephalartos manikensis fast-growing?

Yes, according to the nursery and compiled sources. Jungle Music and LLIFLE describe it as one of the faster-growing species in good conditions, but no measured growth rate has been published.

Is Encephalartos manikensis poisonous?

Very probably. The species has not been studied, but Encephalartos are toxic, especially their seeds.
