Encephalartos cycadifolius is a cycad endemic to South Africa, confined to the Winterberg mountains north of Bedford, in the Eastern Cape, where it grows in full sun among rocks on grassy slopes up to 1,800 m. This species of the genus Encephalartos, with fine, spineless leaflets, withstands fire, frost and drought in its habitat. First described in 1801 as Zamia cycadifolia, it is one of the longest-known species of the genus, and one of the few that the IUCN no longer considers threatened.

Key facts about Encephalartos cycadifolius

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos cycadifolius (Jacq.) Lehm. (1834), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15)
  • Former names and synonyms: Zamia cycadifolia Jacq. (1801), the original name; Encephalartos eximius I.Verd. (1954); Encephalartos cycadifolius var. glaber Regel (1874)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Winterberg cycad (English, Species+); Bedford cycad (nursery name)
  • Natural range: South Africa, Eastern Cape (Winterberg mountains, Bedford and Cradock areas)
  • IUCN status: Least Concern (LC), assessment published in 2022; formerly Vulnerable (VU)
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I (whole genus, since 1977); Annex A in the European Union; wild collection banned throughout South Africa since 2007
  • Documented hardiness: a young plant in the ground in Roussillon, southern France, came through −7 °C (19.4 °F) and snow in the winter of 2009–2010 undamaged, then declined after being overwatered in summer (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, full sun on rocky north- and east-facing slopes; in cultivation, very free-draining soil and sparing watering
  • Habit and adult size: one or several trunks 1 to 1.5 m tall and up to 25 cm in diameter, according to SANBI; leaves 0.6 to 1 m
  • In the UK, US and Australia: RHS Garden Wisley declares it to BGCI; five Californian gardens; an outdoor bed at the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria, Melbourne
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; treat it as poisonous like all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos cycadifolius

Look for fairly short, stiff leaves with narrow, entire leaflets, without teeth, set in a V on the rachis, which recall those of a Cycas and give the species its name. The description published by Henderson in 1945 could not be consulted; the PlantZAfrica account of SANBI (P. M. Xaba, Kirstenbosch National Botanical Garden, April 2009) gives these characters:

  • Habit: a slow-growing, medium-sized cycad with one or several trunks 1 to 1.5 m tall and up to 25 cm in diameter
  • Leaves: dark olive green, 0.6 to 1 m long, on a yellow-orange leaf stalk with a slight spiral twist
  • Leaflets: without spines, attached in a V on the rachis
  • Cones: usually one or two per plant, yellow and covered in grey hairs, turning brown with age
  • Male cones: 15 to 20 cm long and 5 to 8 cm wide
  • Female cones: 20 to 30 cm long and 16 to 18 cm wide

The San Diego nursery Jungle Music adds that the leaves range from olive to deep forest green, new ones sometimes almost whitish green, that the unarmed petioles show a visible twist, and that the plant can be semi-dormant, shedding its leaves before producing a new set. It describes both cones as pale yellow to pale yellowish green, more or less woolly with a white to yellowish felt. A grower in Roussillon, southern France, compared his young plant to a small Cycas revoluta with very fine, slightly glaucous leaflets, producing one leaf a year or even every other year.

Hybrids

No natural hybrid of Encephalartos cycadifolius has been described.

Possible confusion

The oldest confusion concerns Encephalartos friderici-guilielmi, another narrow-leaflet species of the Eastern Cape, which Regel treated in 1874 as a variety of Encephalartos cycadifolius. Robert Allen Dyer restored it to species rank in 1965 in his account of the cycads of southern Africa, and The World List of Cycads treats them as two distinct species. PlantZAfrica notes that Encephalartos cycadifolius is often confused with the other grassland cycads, Encephalartos friderici-guilielmi and Encephalartos ghellinckii; growers also compare it with other fine-leaved species such as Encephalartos lanatus or Encephalartos laevifolius. Spineless, olive-green leaflets held in a V, a twisted petiole and the very local range in the Winterberg point to it; only the cones and a documented origin settle the matter.

Taxonomy

Nikolaus Joseph von Jacquin first described the species as Zamia cycadifolia in his Fragmenta Botanica (p. 27, pl. 25–26), in 1801 according to The World List of Cycads. Johann Georg Christian Lehmann transferred it to Encephalartos in 1834, in the Allgemeine Gartenzeitung (2(11): 86, published on 15 March 1834), and again in his Novarum et Minus Cognitarum Stirpium Pugillus (6: 13, April–May 1834), according to The World List of Cycads and IPNI. The transfer dates from Lehmann's creation of the genus itself.

The World List of Cycads places two names in synonymy:

  • Encephalartos cycadifolius var. glaber Regel (1874), published in the Trudy Imperatorskago S.-Peterburgskago Botaniceskago Sada
  • Encephalartos eximius I.Verd. (1954), described by Inez Verdoorn in Bothalia (6(2): 426) from a plant collected on 28 January 1952 near Bedford, at about 4,000 ft (about 1,220 m), by R. Story (Story 4021, holotype at PRE)

Species+ lists further synonyms (Encephalartos acanthus, Encephalartos verschaffeltii, Encephalartos elongatus, Zamia villosa); The World List of Cycads assigns the first three to other species (Encephalartos friderici-guilielmi, Encephalartos afer and Encephalartos lehmannii). The discrepancy reflects the tangled history of Encephalartos names described in the nineteenth century from plants grown in Europe.

Etymology and history

The epithet combines cycadis, the Latinised form of Cycas, with the Latin folium, “leaf”: it means “with Cycas-like leaves” and refers to the foliage (Haynes, 2022).

The species was among the first South African cycads known in Europe: Jacquin described it in 1801 in the genus Zamia, together with Encephalartos horridus and Encephalartos longifolius, before Lehmann created the genus Encephalartos in 1834. In the twentieth century, Henderson (1945) revised the South African Encephalartos, and Dyer (1965) finally separated Encephalartos friderici-guilielmi. The plant described in 1954 as Encephalartos eximius proved to belong to the same species.

It also reached Mediterranean gardens early. The catalogue of plants growing in the open air in Thomas Hanbury's garden at La Mortola, on the Italian Riviera, published in 1897, lists Encephalartos cycadifolius alongside four other Encephalartos, and the Hortus Mortolensis of 1912 still includes it; neither says how long the plants lived. In Italy, a 1997 study published by the botanic garden of Naples analysed three plants of the species grown there in 1993–1994, and a member of the Tropicamente forum reported in 2011 a potted specimen with a trunk of about 1 m at the Florence botanic garden, which he believed to be very old; its age and history could not be checked.

Common names

The English name Winterberg cycad is used by Species+ and by the Virtual Cycad Encyclopedia of the Palm & Cycad Societies of Florida. Jungle Music sells it as the Bedford cycad, after the town near which it grows. An Afrikaans name built on “broodboom” (bread tree), as for other Encephalartos, circulates on compilation websites without a verifiable source for this species.

In the wild

Encephalartos cycadifolius is endemic to the Eastern Cape. According to PlantZAfrica, it has a restricted range in the Winterberg mountains, in the Bedford and Cradock areas, where it grows on mountainsides among rocks, in full sun, on north- and east-facing slopes up to 1,800 m. The type of Encephalartos eximius was collected at about 1,220 m near Bedford. Jungle Music describes a hot, dry habitat on rocky shale, on gentle to steep grassy hillsides swept by fairly regular fires.

PlantZAfrica describes the species as tolerant of frost and drought and well adapted to fire, which stimulates the production of cones and leaves; porcupines and baboons break off both male and female cones to eat them. Photographs in the Virtual Cycad Encyclopedia (1999) show a plant in the Eastern Cape regrowing after a fire and leaves torn off and left on the ground, attributed to baboons. The pollinators of the species are not documented.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos cycadifolius is listed as Least Concern (LC) on the IUCN Red List (assessment by J. D. Bösenberg, published in 2022), according to The World List of Cycads. Older sources, including PlantZAfrica and the Virtual Cycad Encyclopedia (1998–2000), gave it as Vulnerable (VU). The reasons for the change are not documented.

Like the whole genus, it has been listed in CITES Appendix I since 4 February 1977 and, in the European Union, in Annex A of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, according to Species+, the official CITES and EU database, consulted in October 2026. In South Africa, collecting wild cycads has been banned since February 2007. For artificially propagated plants imported into the EU from South Africa, Species+ records a positive opinion of the EU Scientific Review Group for plants with a caudex up to 7 cm in diameter (since 20 June 2011) and a negative opinion for larger plants (since 7 February 2013); a review covering the whole genus was under consultation in September 2026. Plants sold must come from cultivation and carry the required documents; check the current rules before any purchase or import.

Growing Encephalartos cycadifolius

The species is easy to germinate but very slow: according to PlantZAfrica, the seeds germinate readily, but the seedlings grow slowly and need special care, and adult plants are easily killed by transplanting or take a long time to recover. Jungle Music calls it one of the more cold-hardy Encephalartos, tolerant of heat, sun and drought but sensitive to overwatering, and a true collector's plant, rare and hard to obtain. On French forums, growers likewise describe it as one of the hardiest species of the genus, but sensitive to wet, hard to find in Europe and best not moved.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

Jungle Music recommends sun in coastal and inland southern California, part sun to filtered light in desert areas, and a well-drained soil; because the plant stays small, it advises giving it room towards the front of a planting. At Kirstenbosch, each plant receives in spring a 3:1:5 NPK fertiliser, bone meal and an organic fertiliser, with generous helpings of compost at intervals through the year (PlantZAfrica).

The only detailed record from Europe mainly shows the danger of water. A grower in Roussillon (Pyrénées-Orientales, France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his Encephalartos cycadifolius, a two-year-old seedling brought from California in 1994 and planted out in an acid, clayey soil, did not suffer from cold but lost all its leaves but one after being watered heavily for eight days, in his absence, in August 2009. The plant then stalled and declined; when he lifted it in 2012 its roots had rotted. Set to re-root in sand in a cold glasshouse, it still had no roots in January 2014, and he feared it was lost.

Container cultivation

Pot culture in a mineral, free-draining mix makes it easier to control water, both in summer and in winter. Keep the plant in full sun, water sparingly and avoid repotting or disturbing the roots more than necessary, given its reputation for recovering badly from transplanting.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In Britain, RHS Garden Wisley declares the species to BGCI PlantSearch, which shows that a garden has listed it at some point, not that the plant is alive today; no other British garden has been found listing it. In the United States, the Huntington, Lotusland, the San Diego Botanic Garden, the San Diego Zoo and the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley report it to BGCI; Montgomery Botanical Center lists no accession. In Australia, the census of the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria places it in an outdoor bed at Melbourne Gardens.

Propagation

The species is propagated from seed, which PlantZAfrica describes as easy, and by separating the suckers produced at the base. PlantZAfrica details the method used at Kirstenbosch. In cultivation, hand-pollination is needed for a good seed harvest: pollen is collected as soon as a light tap on the male cone releases it and is stored at −15 °C (5 °F); the scales of the female cone open for three days to two weeks depending on the plant, and the “wet” method, pollen mixed with distilled water and applied with a syringe, gives a good pollination rate. Seeds are left to mature for a year after the female cone is harvested, then sown at the beginning of summer in river sand on a heated bench at 24 to 28 °C (75.2 to 82.4 °F); germination starts after about three weeks, sometimes later. Seeds and young plants are rare in Europe: in 2014 a collector judged on the Fous de palmiers forum that they were almost impossible to find.

Pests and diseases

According to PlantZAfrica, the leaves and cones are prone to scale insects and mealybugs. In cultivation the best documented problem is root rot after overwatering, as in Roussillon. In the wild, baboons and porcupines break off the cones to feed on them.

Cold hardiness

Encephalartos cycadifolius is one of the Encephalartos best known for cold tolerance, but only one dated record exists, and it concerns a young plant. As with other Encephalartos, the survival in cold areas of a young plant whose caudex is still underground, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil and sometimes by snow, says nothing about how an adult with an exposed trunk would fare.

Mediterranean and European experience

The three reports below concern the same plant, in the ground in Roussillon, on the French Mediterranean coast, without any protection mentioned:

  • A grower in Roussillon (Pyrénées-Orientales, France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his young Encephalartos cycadifolius came through three to four weeks of night frosts in the winter of 2009–2010, including three nights at −5 °C (23 °F) and one at −7 °C (19.4 °F), followed by 40 cm of snow in early March 2010: its last leaf stayed intact and the trunk firm. The plant had lost almost all its leaves the previous summer through overwatering.
  • The same grower reports that the plant was still entirely undamaged after the winter of 2010–2011, with about ten days of frost and a minimum of −5 °C (23 °F).
  • In April 2009 he had reported the plant intact after −8 °C (17.6 °F) measured at ground level, without saying which winter; in 2012 he concluded that the species had given him “no problem with cold”, but that the plant had declined after the summer watering, down to rotten roots.

Other members of the same forum rank the species, with Encephalartos friderici-guilielmi and Encephalartos ghellinckii, among the three hardiest Encephalartos, but these are opinions rather than dated observations. The 1897 La Mortola catalogue shows that the species was grown in the open air on the Riviera, without giving temperatures or results. For gardeners in comparable Mediterranean climates, such as coastal California or southern Australia, these observations suggest that drainage and summer watering matter as much as winter cold.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No dated, quantified English-language growing report has been found, and the species appears on the Italian Tropicamente forum only in lists of species reputed to be hardy. In its habitat PlantZAfrica describes it as frost-tolerant, without giving a temperature. Jungle Music calls it very cold-tolerant without quoting a value, and no USDA zone is published for it by a botanical source. In Britain, outdoor cultivation is not documented; in Melbourne it grows outdoors at the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria, which publishes no cold data for it.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data are published for Encephalartos cycadifolius itself, but the risk is documented in the genus: the toxicity of Encephalartos horridus was demonstrated as early as 1944 by Van der Walt, and Encephalartos, like other cycads, contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; the seeds are the most dangerous part. PlantZAfrica lists the species as poisonous. The ASPCA reports vomiting, diarrhoea, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses that have eaten cycads.

Treat the plant as poisonous: wear gloves to clean seeds, pick up any that fall, and keep plants and seeds away from children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

PlantZAfrica states that no specific traditional or cultural use of Encephalartos cycadifolius is recorded. The name of the genus refers to the starchy pith that some Encephalartos provided as famine food; this species is not named in that context, and such preparations should not be tried.

Frequently asked questions

Is Encephalartos cycadifolius frost-hardy?

Partly, on the strength of a single record. In Roussillon, southern France, a young plant in the ground came through −7 °C (19.4 °F) and snow in the winter of 2009–2010 without damage. It then declined after being overwatered in summer: cold tolerance is not enough, the soil must drain very freely.

Why is it called cycadifolius?

Because its leaves recall those of a Cycas. The name combines Cycas with the Latin folium, “leaf”.

How can I tell it from Encephalartos friderici-guilielmi?

Not easily without cones. Both Eastern Cape species have narrow leaflets and were long confused. Those of Encephalartos cycadifolius are spineless, olive green and held in a V on a slightly twisted petiole; a plant of documented origin remains the best guarantee.

Does it grow fast?

No. It is reputed to be one of the slowest Encephalartos, and seedlings need special care. A young plant grown in Roussillon produced only one leaf a year, sometimes one every two years.

Is it endangered?

Not at present, according to the latest assessment. The IUCN has listed it as Least Concern since 2022, whereas it was previously considered Vulnerable. Like the whole genus, it remains in CITES Appendix I.

Can I buy one legally?

Yes, if the plant was propagated in cultivation and carries the CITES documents required for a species in CITES Appendix I and, in the European Union, Annex A. It is rare in the trade and seldom cheap.

Is it poisonous?

It must be assumed so. No data exist for the species, but Encephalartos seeds contain toxins dangerous to people and animals.
