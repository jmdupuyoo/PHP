Encephalartos msinganus is a tree-like cycad of South Africa, known only from around Msinga, near Tugela Ferry in KwaZulu-Natal, where it grows on steep, sunny slopes between 900 and 1,200 m. This species of the genus Encephalartos forms clumps of trunks up to about 3 m tall, crowned with stiff, glossy dark green leaves. Before it was described by the South African botanist Piet Vorster in 1996, it circulated in the trade as the “Msinga form” of Encephalartos natalensis. It is Critically Endangered: the colony found in the late 1980s was almost wiped out by collecting within a few years, while the plant became common in private collections. In cultivation it is a medium-sized green cycad for sun or light shade, described as frost-tolerant in South Africa but untested in cold, wet winters.

Key facts about Encephalartos msinganus

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos msinganus Vorster (1996), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and GBIF
  • Former names and synonyms: no published synonym; before 1996 the plants were sold and exported as the “Msinga form” of Encephalartos natalensis (SANBI)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Msinga cycad (Species+, PlantZAfrica); msinga-broodboom in Afrikaans (PlantZAfrica)
  • Natural range: South Africa, KwaZulu-Natal, Msinga district (around Tugela Ferry), between 900 and 1,200 m
  • IUCN status: Critically Endangered (CR B1ab(iii,v); C2a(ii)), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I since 1977, like the whole genus; Annex A in the European Union, with a negative EU opinion on artificially propagated plants from South Africa; “Specially Protected” in KwaZulu-Natal; wild collection banned throughout South Africa since 2007; national Biodiversity Management Plan (2017)
  • Documented hardiness: no measured or reliably reported value; described as “frost tolerant” by PlantZAfrica, with no temperature or plant age (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, short grassland on steep north-facing slopes among boulders and scrub, in almost full sun; in cultivation, light shade or full sun and a deep, well-drained soil (PlantZAfrica)
  • Habit and adult size: trunk upright or slightly leaning, up to about 3 m tall and 35 cm thick, suckering at the base; leaves 1.1 to 1.5 m (Vorster, 1996)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Montgomery Botanical Center (three accessions, 1978 and 2000), Fairchild, the Huntington, Lotusland, the Missouri Botanical Garden and three other gardens in the United States; Adelaide and the University of Melbourne in Australia (BGCI); no British garden declares it
  • Toxicity: not studied for the species; seeds toxic in other Encephalartos (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos msinganus

Look for a tree-like plant forming clumps, with fairly narrow, hard, glossy dark green leaflets, and female cones whose scales are warty on the outer face. Vorster's original description (1996) gives the following characters:

  • Habit: tree-like, often suckering from the base and forming clumps
  • Trunk: upright but often slightly leaning, up to about 3 m tall and 35 cm thick, covered with the remains of old leaf bases; crown filled with dense brown wool
  • Leaves: 1.1 to 1.5 m, stiff, straight to slightly arched; petiole short (2 to 10 cm), hairless and spineless; the lower leaflets become gradually smaller towards the base of the leaf, the last ones reduced to prickles; PlantZAfrica adds that the leaves are paler beneath, with prominent longitudinal veins
  • Leaflets: those of the middle of the leaf pointing towards the tip at about 30°, very narrowly ovate, hard, with a sharp, pungent tip, 14 to 17 cm by 1.6 to 2 cm, spaced 1.5 to 2.5 cm apart, glossy dark green; both margins usually, but not always, bear teeth
  • Male cones: two to four per stem, on stalks of up to 7 cm, very narrowly ovoid, hairless to the naked eye, pale yellow, 30 to 40 cm by 11 to 12 cm when ripe; the outer face of the scales is drawn out into a drooping beak
  • Female cones: stalkless, ovoid, up to two per stem, about 42 by 22 cm, greenish yellow becoming brighter yellow when ripe, the colour more or less hidden by brown felted hairs; the outer face of the scales is warty
  • Seeds: fleshy coat (sarcotesta) bright red

Without cones, identification is uncertain: SANBI's document on the trade in the species stresses that identifying Encephalartos msinganus is “particularly problematic”, including for enforcement officers. Many plants circulate as young plants; the firmest confirmation comes from the cones, especially the female cones. Jungle Music, a nursery in southern California, notes some variability in leaf type and spines, in the wild and in cultivation, and describes male and female cones as yellow to yellowish orange.

Hybrids

No natural hybrid of Encephalartos msinganus is documented. In collections the risk exists, since different species grow side by side and hand pollination is common: to obtain pure seed, female cones must be isolated and the origin of the pollen controlled. PlantZAfrica recommends hand pollination in the garden.

Possible confusion

Vorster (1996) places Encephalartos msinganus in the group formed by Encephalartos aemulans, Encephalartos altensteinii, Encephalartos lebomboensis, Encephalartos natalensis and Encephalartos senticosus. Two species resemble it closely:

  • Encephalartos senticosus has the same vegetative features and similar male cones, but the outer face of the scales of its female cones is smooth, not warty;
  • Encephalartos natalensis also has similar male cones and warty female cones, but much broader leaflets, 25 to 45 mm against 16 to 20 mm.

PlantZAfrica adds that, without cones, the species is hard to separate from Encephalartos aemulans, Encephalartos lebomboensis, some forms of Encephalartos natalensis and Encephalartos senticosus, and gives cone characters for each: permanently woolly cones of similar size in both sexes in Encephalartos aemulans; cones never woolly and smooth in Encephalartos lebomboensis; female cones first densely woolly in Encephalartos natalensis, whose leaflets are broader than 25 mm and boat-shaped; rough-surfaced cones in Encephalartos senticosus.

Confusion with Encephalartos natalensis was in fact the rule for years: according to SANBI, the Msinga plants were referred to that species before the late 1980s, and the first exports, from 1983, bore the name “Msinga form” of Encephalartos natalensis. In the wild the area is rich in Encephalartos: the type locality of Encephalartos cerinus is close by, Encephalartos natalensis and Encephalartos villosus grow almost within sight, and an isolated clump of Encephalartos laevifolius was once found there, some 300 km from the known populations in Eswatini (Vorster, 1996; PlantZAfrica).

Taxonomy

Encephalartos msinganus is an accepted name in The World List of Cycads and GBIF, with no synonym. Piet Vorster published it in 1996 in the South African Journal of Botany (volume 62, issue 2, pp. 67–70), in a paper entitled “Encephalartos msinganus (Zamiaceae): a new species from KwaZulu-Natal”, illustrated with drawings of the leaves and scales and photographs of the cones and plants in the wild. The type is Vorster's collection 2949a, made “near Msinga” on 18 April 1988 according to The World List of Cycads; the holotype is kept at the National Herbarium in Pretoria (PRE), with isotypes at Fort Lauderdale (FTG), Kew (K), St Petersburg (LE) and St Louis (MO). To protect the plants, Vorster did not publish the precise locality.

The species has not changed name since its description. The only “former name” under which it may be met is Encephalartos natalensis, to which the Msinga plants were referred in the trade before 1996: this is not a synonym in the botanical sense but an earlier identification. Jungle Music adds that the plants were first called “Encephalartos msinga” before the formal description; no published source confirms this name.

Etymology and history

The epithet msinganus refers to the Msinga district of KwaZulu-Natal, the only area where the species is known (etymology given by Haynes, 2022, cited by The World List of Cycads).

The history of the species is one of very rapid decline. According to the original description, the colony comprised several hundred rather scattered plants when it was discovered in the late 1980s. Within a few years collectors, and above all one or two dealers who became well-known suppliers of these plants, had almost wiped it out, without the KwaZulu Department of Nature Conservation being able to prevent it. In 1996 Vorster considered the species on the verge of extinction in the wild, although it had become common in private collections; it was not represented at the national botanic garden at Kirstenbosch.

SANBI's document adds several episodes: a seed-collecting permit was issued only once, in 2005, for research; 31 adult plants were removed by helicopter from the Msinga area to a nursery at Eshowe by the KwaZulu-Natal nature conservation service, at a time when this was thought to be the only way to protect them; in 2011 site visits showed that poaching was continuing, the head of the communal area confirming that all the cycads of a nearby site had been removed.

Common names

Species+ gives the English name Msinga cycad. The PlantZAfrica fact sheet (Lungisani Zondi, Walter Sisulu National Botanical Garden, July 2019) gives the same English name and the Afrikaans name msinga-broodboom. No Zulu name specific to the species is attested: according to the same fact sheet, most cycads are called isqgiki–somkhovu in Zulu culture, a name linked to beliefs about witchcraft, which therefore does not refer to this species in particular.

In the wild

Encephalartos msinganus is known only from around Msinga, near Tugela Ferry, in KwaZulu-Natal, where its range seems very small, between 900 and 1,200 m (Vorster, 1996). It grows in short grassland on steep north-facing slopes, which face the sun in the southern hemisphere, usually among boulders in clumps of scrub, more rarely on sandstone cliffs, in roughly full sun. PlantZAfrica (2019) describes the same habitat and the same neighbouring cycads. SANBI's trade document states that these mountains are “practically inaccessible”, which has not prevented poaching, and the 2017 management plan notes that the land belongs to the Msinga community.

Vorster recorded a few observations on reproduction. Cones seem to emerge in late February and early March, at the same time as those of Encephalartos natalensis and Encephalartos senticosus; by mid-April the female cones appeared fully grown, but the male cones had not yet shed their pollen. Pollination would take place in early May and the seeds would be ripe in October. No seedlings were found in the habitat, which is little disturbed by livestock; but seeds collected on site had germinated well in a nursery, proof that pollination was still working shortly before. PlantZAfrica gives a slightly different calendar: male cones produced in midsummer (December), with pollen shed in autumn (March and April).

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos msinganus is Critically Endangered (CR). The World List of Cycads cites the IUCN Red List assessment published in 2022 (J.D. Bösenberg), under criteria B1ab(iii,v); C2a(ii): a tiny range, a continuing decline in habitat and in the number of mature individuals, and almost all mature individuals in a single subpopulation. GBIF gives the same category. The 2017 management plan quotes the criteria B1ab(iii,v)+2ab(iii,v); C1+2a(ii).

The IUCN action plan for cycads (Donaldson, 2003) already listed the species as Critically Endangered, with a population estimated at 100 to 200 plants, a strong decline, overcollecting and reproductive failure as the main threats, and no population in a reserve; it counted it among the species for which a gene bank in a botanic garden did not yet exist or needed strengthening. SANBI's document, updated in May 2015, puts the number of wild adults at fewer than 200, in a few scattered subpopulations: an aerial survey in 2012 located fewer than 200, and other adults were found near houses and a nearby school. It cites poaching for horticulture and collections as the main cause, and possibly also for traditional medicine. PlantZAfrica, quoting the South African Red List, gives fewer than 250 plants, most of them in one subpopulation, and estimates that over 25% of the remaining plants could be removed within thirty years.

The national Biodiversity Management Plan for the Critically Endangered and Endangered Encephalartos (Government Gazette No. 40793, 21 April 2017) states that poaching has reduced a population of 100 to 200 plants to a small number of scattered individuals. Aerial surveys show coning plants, but no ground survey has been made to look for seedlings in this difficult terrain. Older members of the Msinga community disapprove of the removals, and there are unconfirmed reports that children take wild seedlings for trade. The species is held in a viable ex situ collection in at least one National Botanical Garden. The plan's objectives include agreements and a security plan with the community, a new population count, planting one Encephalartos msinganus at each homestead to meet local demand, hand pollination, sowing in the wild and a propagation programme at the local school; its target is 500 plants of more than five years at three sites by 2030, and in the long term 5,000 plants at five sites.

Regulation, according to Species+ (consulted through its public API on 26 October 2026):

  • CITES: Appendix I since 4 February 1977, like the whole genus;
  • European Union: Annex A, current list in Regulation (EU) 2026/1383; negative opinion of the EU Scientific Review Group on artificially propagated plants from South Africa (source codes A and D), current since 7 February 2013; a new consultation covering the whole genus has been open since 16 September 2026;
  • South Africa: wild collection of cycads banned since February 2007 (now under Government Notice 371 of May 2012); the species is “Specially Protected” under the KwaZulu-Natal Nature Conservation Ordinance (No. 15 of 1974).

SANBI judged the trade in the species detrimental (finding approved in February 2012): from 1983 to 2011, 523 specimens were exported from South Africa, with a peak in 1998 and 1999 (60 and 88 specimens), with no benefit to the species or its habitat, and the legal origin of parent plants cannot always be proved. Exports are now allowed only for seedlings produced by CITES-registered nurseries. These notes do not replace the official texts: check Species+ and EUR-Lex before any purchase or import, and ask for documents proving that a plant was propagated in cultivation.

Growing Encephalartos msinganus

Encephalartos msinganus is grown mainly in specialist collections, and the published advice is brief. PlantZAfrica recommends light shade or full sun, a deep, well-drained soil and frequent watering, and describes the species as frost-tolerant and fast-growing under these conditions. SANBI's trade document, on the other hand, describes slow-growing, long-lived adults, and considers the species uncommon in cultivation, even in South Africa, because cultivated plants rarely cone and the original wild population was small; it is found mainly in large collections and nurseries, usually at low prices. PlantZAfrica, like Vorster in 1996, says the opposite about private collections: the documents disagree on these points.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

Its habitat, steep slopes where water drains away quickly, in full sun, shows what matters in the garden: a very free-draining soil, no standing water, especially in cool weather, and plenty of light. In southern California, Jungle Music recommends sun on the coast and inland, partial sun or filtered light in desert areas, and a well-drained soil, and considers the plant suitable for both tropical-style and dry gardens, as it needs little space and keeps a full crown. In Europe the species is listed by the Barcelona botanic garden (list of 29 November 2012, “Parterre” sector), by the Messina botanic garden (cycad list of February 2009, without saying whether the plants are in the ground or in pots), and a plant is grown at the Jardin botanique de la Ville de Nice, as the photograph on this page shows; these sources record the presence of the species, not its growing conditions. The Naples botanic garden held four Encephalartos labelled “Msinga” in 1993–1994 (Delpinoa, 1997), before the species was described; their identity with Encephalartos msinganus is a deduction.

Container cultivation

A grower in Agen (Lot-et-Garonne, south-west France) shows on the Fous de palmiers forum, in June 2010, a new crown of leaves on an Encephalartos msinganus from a collection grown in pots, which he puts out in the sun in the warm season; he gives no temperature. A deep pot, a gritty, free-draining mix, generous watering in the growing season and a drier, frost-free winter are the logical translation of the advice above for cool climates.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

BGCI PlantSearch lists 13 institutions holding the species. In the United States they are the Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida), whose online catalogue shows three accessions, one of 1978 (before the species was described) and two of 2000, Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden and Naples Botanical Garden in Florida, the Huntington, Ganna Walska Lotusland, Descanso Gardens and the San Diego Botanic Garden in California, and the Missouri Botanical Garden in St Louis. In Australia, BGCI lists the Botanic Garden of Adelaide and the System Garden of the University of Melbourne. No British garden declares the species. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not a living plant on display today. Jungle Music describes the species as rare but reasonably affordable, and usually holds plants from seedlings to larger sizes.

Propagation

Encephalartos msinganus is propagated from seed and, less often, by separating the suckers that the plant produces naturally at the base. Vorster (1996) reports that seeds collected in the habitat germinated well in a nursery; in the wild they ripen in October. PlantZAfrica advises sowing in river sand on a heated bench at 24 to 28 °C (75 to 82 °F): germination should start about three weeks after sowing, more slowly without bottom heat, and seedlings at the one-leaf stage are susceptible to damping-off. SANBI recalls that in cycads suckers play a minor role in the regeneration of populations, which depends mainly on seed. Seeds, like plants, are covered by CITES Appendix I and must carry the required documents.

Pests and diseases

No disease or pest specific to Encephalartos msinganus has been described. As with other Encephalartos, the main risk in cultivation is rot of the thick roots and trunk in a substrate that stays wet, especially in cool weather; PlantZAfrica mentions damping-off of young seedlings. SANBI also notes that 30 to 50% of cycads dug up in the wild die within a few years, which illustrates the fragility of their roots.

Cold hardiness

The cold hardiness of Encephalartos msinganus is not documented by any figure. PlantZAfrica (2019) states only that the species is “frost tolerant”, with no temperature, frost duration or plant age, and the South African horticultural zones it recommends are two frost-free coastal zones and the Bushveld zone with light frosts. This is a general indication for South Africa, which cannot be translated into a temperature or extended to cold, wet winters. The Msinga area has a cooler, drier winter than summer, but no published source gives minimum temperatures for the habitat itself.

Mediterranean and European experience

No trial of planting out in the open has been published in Europe. The only French report concerns a potted plant in Agen, put outside in summer, and the plant at the Nice botanic garden comes with no information on the winters it has been through. On the Italian forum Tropicamente, the species is mentioned only in connection with plants of uncertain identity, including a claim of resistance to −12 °C (10.4 °F) attributed to unnamed “South African experts”: these reports are set aside. For growers in Mediterranean climates, or in comparable climates such as coastal California or southern Australia, outdoor planting can only be considered as a trial in the mildest sites, with perfect drainage and protection from winter rain and frost.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No measured value has been published for these countries, and the gardens listed above do not give any. No USDA zone is documented for the species in a reliable source. As with other Encephalartos, survivals in cold areas often concern young plants with an underground caudex, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil and sometimes by snow; adult plants with an aerial trunk, as in this species, do not benefit from this protection.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data have been published for Encephalartos msinganus. The risk is nevertheless documented in the genus: the toxicity of Encephalartos horridus was demonstrated as early as 1944 (Van der Walt), and Encephalartos contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; these substances affect the liver, the digestive tract and the nervous system, and the seeds are the most dangerous part. The ASPCA reports vomiting, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses that have eaten cycads. Treat this species as poisonous: wear gloves to handle seeds and keep plants and seeds out of reach of children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use specific to the species is documented. SANBI only mentions that plants were “possibly” also taken for traditional medicine. PlantZAfrica reports that in Zulu culture cycads in general are planted in front of the homestead gate to protect it from evil spirits, and the 2017 management plan speaks of a local demand for Encephalartos plants at homesteads, which it proposes to meet with propagated plants.

Frequently asked questions

Where does Encephalartos msinganus grow in the wild?

Only in KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa, in the Msinga district near Tugela Ferry, between 900 and 1,200 m. It grows in short grassland on steep north-facing slopes, among rocks and scrub.

Why is it Critically Endangered?

Because of poaching. The colony, which numbered several hundred plants in the late 1980s, had almost disappeared by 1996; SANBI estimates that fewer than 200 wild adults remain, and poaching was still going on in 2011.

How can it be told from Encephalartos senticosus?

By the female cones. The two species have similar leaves and male cones, but the female cone scales are warty in Encephalartos msinganus and smooth in Encephalartos senticosus. Without cones, a young plant is hard to identify.

What is the difference from Encephalartos natalensis?

Mainly the width of the leaflets: 16 to 20 mm in Encephalartos msinganus, 25 to 45 mm in Encephalartos natalensis. The cones are similar, and the Msinga plants were long sold as a form of Encephalartos natalensis.

Is Encephalartos msinganus frost-hardy?

Nobody knows for certain. No outdoor trial in a cold climate has been published, and PlantZAfrica, which calls it “frost tolerant”, gives no temperature. Like other Encephalartos with an aerial trunk, it is most at risk from prolonged cold and wet: in frost-prone climates, a pot kept under cover in winter is the safe choice.

How big does it get?

Up to about 3 m of trunk and 35 cm across, with leaves 1.1 to 1.5 m long, often in clumps of several trunks. Its growth rate is judged differently: SANBI speaks of slow-growing adults, PlantZAfrica of fast growth in a deep, well-drained, frequently watered soil.

Can it be bought legally?

Yes, but only plants propagated in cultivation and accompanied by CITES and, in Europe, EU documents. South Africa only allows exports of seedlings from registered nurseries, and the EU maintains a negative opinion on artificially propagated plants from South Africa.
