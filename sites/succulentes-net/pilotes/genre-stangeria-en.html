The genus Stangeria has a single species, a cycad from southern Africa that is easily mistaken for a fern: a carrot-shaped underground stem from which rise a few pinnate leaves with broad, veined leaflets. It lives on a narrow coastal strip of eastern South Africa, from the Eastern Cape to KwaZulu-Natal, and just reaches southern Mozambique. Its likeness to a fern is so strong that its first describers placed it among the ferns, as Lomaria eriopus, before a cone revealed its true nature. The species has also been called Stangeria paradoxa. Long placed in a family of its own, the Stangeriaceae, the genus is now placed by POWO in the Zamiaceae. It has been listed in CITES Appendix I since 1975 and is classed as Vulnerable by IUCN (2022 assessment), threatened above all by the harvesting of its stem for traditional medicine.

Key facts about Stangeria

  • Accepted name (POWO): Stangeria T.Moore (1853), a monotypic genus
  • Number of species: one, Stangeria eriopus (Kunze) Baill. (POWO, The World List of Cycads)
  • Former names: Lomaria eriopus Kunze (1839), described as a fern; Stangeria paradoxa T.Moore (1853); more rarely Stangeria schizodon W.Bull (1872) and Stangeria katzeri Regel (1874)
  • Family: Zamiaceae according to POWO and The World List of Cycads; Stangeriaceae in older classifications, a family still used in CITES nomenclature (Species+)
  • Common names: Natal grass cycad, Stanger's cycad (English), bobbejaankos (Afrikaans), imfingo (Zulu), umfingwani (Xhosa)
  • Natural range: coastal strip of eastern South Africa (Eastern Cape, KwaZulu-Natal), to the far south of Mozambique; Species+ also cites Eswatini
  • IUCN status: Vulnerable (VU A2acd+4acd), 2022 assessment cited by The World List of Cycads
  • CITES: Appendix I since 1 July 1975; Annex A in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: leaves destroyed at about −4 °C (24.8 °F); survival at −7 °C (19.4 °F) in the ground (February 2012, foliage destroyed) at the Jardin zoologique tropical in the south of France, where the plant, weak in the ground, is now grown in a pot and does much better; on forums, plants lost in western France during the cold spell of February 2012 (minimum not stated) and slight leaf burn at −5 °C (23 °F) in Florida in 2026 (see Cold hardiness)
  • Habit: stem entirely underground; leaves about 30 cm (1 ft) long in the grassland form to 2 m (6.5 ft) in the forest form
  • Toxicity: cycasin and macrozamin measured in the underground stem; poisonous like all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

Characteristics of the genus Stangeria

A Stangeria is recognised first by its leaflets: broad, flat, with a strong midrib from which run many almost parallel side veins that fork. No other cycad has this kind of venation, which recalls that of a fern.

  • Stem: entirely underground, bare, tuberous and carrot-shaped, sometimes branching by dichotomy; published descriptions give it a diameter of 10 to 25 cm.
  • Leaves: pinnate, few, rising straight from the ground. Their size varies greatly with habitat: up to 2 m in moist forest, sometimes only 30 cm in grassland burnt every year.
  • Cones: like all cycads, the plant is dioecious. The cones, on a short stalk, are covered with silvery hairs when young. The cylindrical male cone reaches about 25 cm long; the female cone, ovoid and green, about 18 cm long and 10 cm wide (descriptions from the Gymnosperm Database and Wikipedia).
  • Seeds: 2 to 3 cm long, released when the female cone breaks up at maturity.
  • Pollination: by insects; the cones give off a faint smell that attracts beetles. No detailed study of the pollinators was consulted.

Taxonomy and classification

Stangeria is a genus accepted by POWO, which places it in the family Zamiaceae with the eight other cycad genera with compact cones. Its only species is Stangeria eriopus (Kunze) Baill.

From Lomaria to Stangeria eriopus

The German botanist Gustav Kunze first referred plants collected in South Africa to a fern already known, Lomaria coriacea Schrad., in the journal Linnaea in 1836. In 1839, again in Linnaea, he made them a new fern species, Lomaria eriopus. In 1853 Thomas Moore described the genus Stangeria and the species Stangeria paradoxa in Hooker's Journal of Botany and Kew Garden Miscellany, the epithet paradoxa underlining the confusion with ferns. In 1892 the French botanist Henri Baillon transferred the older epithet to Moore's genus: the correct name is therefore Stangeria eriopus, and Stangeria paradoxa is only a synonym. The World List of Cycads also treats as synonyms two 19th-century horticultural names, Stangeria schizodon W.Bull (1872) and Stangeria katzeri Regel (1874).

A family of its own, then the Zamiaceae

Classifications based on morphology placed Stangeria in its own family, the Stangeriaceae, often with the Australian genus Bowenia, which also has fern-like leaves. Molecular studies have shown that this grouping does not reflect real relationships. The analysis of five nuclear genes published by Salas-Leiva and colleagues in 2013 (Annals of Botany) places Stangeria as the sister group of the pair formed by Microcycas and Zamia, two American genera, while Bowenia belongs to another branch. The authors note that this result agrees with a fossil, Eostangeria, which shares features of Stangeria and Zamia. The gymnosperm classification of Christenhusz and colleagues (2011), followed by POWO, therefore brought Stangeria into the Zamiaceae. This change of family alters neither the genus name nor the species name. CITES, however, still follows its 2013 standard reference list: its Species+ database still places Stangeria in the Stangeriaceae.

One species or several?

Forest plants and grassland plants are so different that whether to recognise several species was debated for more than a century, according to PlantZAfrica (SANBI). The two forms grade into each other without a clear boundary and are now interpreted as adaptations to habitat: POWO and The World List of Cycads accept only one species.

Etymology and history of the genus

The genus is named after William Stanger (1811–1854), an English doctor, geologist and botanist, first surveyor-general of the British colony of Natal from 1845; he had earlier taken part in the British Niger expedition of 1841. Some sources call him “Max”: this is a mistake, as the biographical databases (JSTOR Global Plants, S2A3) give William Stanger. The town of Stanger, now KwaDukuza, in KwaZulu-Natal, is also named after him. The epithet eriopus, chosen by Kunze, means “woolly foot”: it combines the Greek erion (“wool”) with pous (“foot”), in allusion to the woolly leaf bases, according to Haynes (2022), cited by The World List of Cycads.

According to PlantZAfrica and Wikipedia, it was Stanger who sent a living plant to England. That plant produced a cone, at the Chelsea Physic Garden in London according to Wikipedia, which revealed that it was a cycad and not a fern. Moore then based the new genus on it in 1853. The published sources do not agree on the exact date of the collection and of the appearance of the cone (around 1851).

Common names

In English the plant is known as Natal grass cycad or Stanger's cycad, according to PlantZAfrica (SANBI), which also gives the South African names:

  • bobbejaankos (“baboon food”), in Afrikaans;
  • imfingo, in Zulu, and umfingwani, in Xhosa.

“Hottentot's head” is also cited by some English-language sources. This name is ambiguous and offensive, and it is also applied to other plants.

Biogeography and ecology

Stangeria lives on a coastal strip about 800 km long beside the Indian Ocean, in the mosaics of coastal forest of the Eastern Cape, KwaZulu-Natal and Maputaland. The scattered populations extend from Bathurst, in the Eastern Cape, to Kosi Bay, in northern KwaZulu-Natal, and barely cross the Mozambique border. The plants are rarely found more than 50 km from the sea, and not within 2 or 3 km of it, because they do not tolerate salt spray.

The presence in Mozambique is given by the Gymnosperm Database and other sources, but POWO limits the range to the Eastern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal, and PlantZAfrica ends the range “just south of the Mozambique border”. At most, therefore, a few populations reach the far south of that country.

The climate of this coast is warm and humid in summer, the rainy season, and cooler and drier in winter, with rare frosts. The same species occupies very different habitats, which explains its two forms:

  • forest form: in dense, moist lowland forest, under heavy shade, in the wettest areas; large, broad leaves up to 2 m long;
  • grassland form: in full sun in short grassland, exposed every year to fire and drought; short leaves, sometimes 30 cm, with a thick cuticle, often a single upright leaf per crown, with short, leathery leaflets.

Between the two, plants also grow in partial shade under the trees of coastal parks. In grassland, the underground stem allows the plant to survive the yearly bushfires and regrow afterwards.

Traditional uses and harvesting

The underground stem of Stangeria is widely used in South African traditional medicine (muthi), mainly as an ingredient in magical preparations but also as an emetic; PlantZAfrica also mentions use against headaches. A study published in 1994 by Osborne and colleagues estimated that 3,410 stems, about 2,380 kg, were sold in July 1992 in the medicinal plant markets of Victoria and Isipingo, in Durban, alone. Its analyses found an unusually high content of sodium sulphate, which could explain the emetic effect, as well as cycasin. This information is given for documentary purposes only: the plant is poisonous and must not be eaten.

Regulation and conservation

Stangeria eriopus is classed as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List (category VU, criteria A2acd+4acd). The World List of Cycads cites the most recent assessment, published in 2022 by J.D. Bösenberg, which replaces that of 2010. The two main causes are harvesting for traditional medicine, described above, and the loss of habitat on a highly sought-after coast. The Red List itself could not be consulted directly: the category and year come from The World List of Cycads (version of 15 August 2026).

The species has been listed in CITES Appendix I since 1 July 1975, like all Encephalartos and a few other cycads. International commercial trade is possible only for artificially propagated plants, accompanied by the required documents. Unlike the seeds of Appendix II cycads, its seeds enjoy no exemption. The United Kingdom, the United States and Australia apply CITES through their own national legislation and permit systems. For readers in the European Union, Regulation (EC) No 338/97 places Stangeria eriopus in its Annex A, the list in force being set by Commission Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026. Before buying, importing or selling a Stangeria, check its status and the documents required on Species+ and with your national CITES authority. This general information does not replace the official texts. Species+ was consulted on 1 October 2026.

Growing Stangeria

Stangeria is one of the easiest South African cycads to grow, provided it is spared prolonged frost. PlantZAfrica describes it as a plant that does well in cultivation, luxuriant in a shaded, humid glasshouse, ideal for shade gardens but able to live in full sun without leaf scorch, and more tolerant of humidity than most South African cycads.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Light: light to partial shade preferably. PlantZAfrica adds that the plant can also grow in full sun without leaf scorch. Foster Botanical Garden in Honolulu, Hawaii, advises filtered sun and humid air.
  • Soil: free-draining, as the underground stem can rot in waterlogged soil, as in other cycads.
  • Transplanting: Foster Botanical Garden notes slow recovery when the roots are cut during transplanting. Repot without damaging the large fleshy root.
  • By the sea: the Dunedin Botanic Garden, in New Zealand, states that the species does not tolerate salt spray. In the wild it does not grow within 2 km of the sea. Avoid exposed seaside positions.
  • Propagation: mostly from seed, which is rarely available. PlantZAfrica reports a trial of rooting whole leaves, treated with hormones and set in sterilised sand: about 80 % formed new roots.

Container cultivation

At the Jardin zoologique tropical in La Londe-les-Maures (Var, south-eastern France), a plant grown in the ground for about ten years, in partial shade under trees and shrubs, was weak there; moved into a pot, it now spends the winter in an unheated glasshouse and stays outdoors from Easter to the first frosts, and it grows much better than it did in the ground. This Mediterranean experience may help readers in comparable climates, such as coastal California or southern Australia, without giving an exact equivalent for any particular place. A grower in Corsica, by contrast, finds that the species grows poorly in a pot (see below): the two experiences differ, and the pot remains the safer choice wherever frosts are regular. The Dunedin Botanic Garden, in a cool climate, also grows it under glass.

Cold hardiness

The leaves are destroyed at about −4 °C (24.8 °F), but the buried stem can regrow if the ground does not freeze deeply. At the Jardin zoologique tropical in La Londe-les-Maures, a plant grown in the ground, in partial shade under trees and shrubs, survived the cold spell of February 2012 (−7 °C, 19.4 °F, on the site) with all its foliage destroyed by frost, then regrew in the warm season. Elsewhere, only amateur reports exist: a failure in western France during the same cold spell and a successful planting in the ground in southern Corsica. No result is documented for inland or mountain areas. Outside the mildest Mediterranean coast and Corsica, pot culture brought in frost-free for the winter is therefore the safest method; for readers in mild, damp climates such as south-west England, no outdoor trial is documented.

Growers' reports

These are amateur observations of indicative value, not general rules. The posts of the Fous de palmiers forum were re-read in their threads in October 2026. No French report gives a place, a date and a temperature all together.

  • A grower in Saint-Sulpice-des-Landes (Loire-Atlantique, western France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that he lost his Stangeria eriopus during the cold spell of February 2012 (post of November 2015). He states neither the temperature in his garden, nor whether the plants were in the ground or in pots, nor the protection. The nearest Météo-France stations recorded −11.5 °C (11.3 °F) at Soudan (19 km away) and −10 °C (14 °F) at Ancenis (22 km away) on 12 February 2012.
  • A grower in Corse-du-Sud (Corsica, France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that he has long grown large male and female Stangeria eriopus outdoors in the ground, which cone every year. In his view the species looks best in shade, with leaves of at least 1.5 m, needs water in summer and humus-rich soil, and grows poorly in a pot (posts of April 2017). He gives no minimum temperature.
  • A grower in central Florida (United States) reports on the PalmTalk forum that his Stangeria eriopus, probably in the ground, suffered slight leaf burn in February 2026 at −5 °C (23 °F), whereas Cycas thouarsii and Encephalartos ferox were 100 % burnt in the same garden. Protection is not stated (known from a search snippet; the forum is protected by Cloudflare).

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In short: Stangeria is widely held in botanic gardens, under glass in Britain, both under glass and outdoors in the United States, and outdoors at Melbourne in Australia. A species listed by a garden in the BGCI PlantSearch database (consulted in October 2026) shows that it has been declared there at some point, not that a living plant is there today.

  • United Kingdom: the Royal Botanic Garden Edinburgh's Garden Explorer (updated 18 October 2026) and the Cambridge University Botanic Garden catalogue both list Stangeria eriopus; Kew, the Chelsea Physic Garden, where, according to Wikipedia, the plant sent by Stanger produced the cone that revealed its true nature, Glasgow, Bristol and Treborth declare it in BGCI PlantSearch. No Stangeria growing in the open ground in Britain is documented in a verifiable source.
  • United States: Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida) holds it in its online catalogue, and Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden's Garden Explorer (updated 16 October 2026) lists it. The Huntington, Lotusland, the San Diego Botanic Garden, the San Diego Zoo, the Missouri Botanical Garden and Naples Botanical Garden declare it in BGCI. At the University of California Botanical Garden in Berkeley, where most cycads grow outdoors, Stangeria needs a glasshouse (Plant Collections Network, 2015).
  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney lists it in its Garden Explorer (updated 1 October 2026), and the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria census records four plants in the African Tunnel Bed, an outdoor bed at Melbourne. Adelaide also declares it in BGCI.

Toxicity and precautions

Yes, Stangeria is poisonous. The study by Osborne and colleagues (1994) measured cycasin at 0.17 % in the fresh stem and 0.21 % in the dry stem, together with a related toxin, macrozamin. The body converts cycasin into methylazoxymethanol (MAM), which is toxic to the liver and nervous system, in people as in animals. No data specific to the genus are documented on poisoning of pets, but the risk is well documented in other cycads, particularly for dogs that chew the seeds. Wear gloves to handle the seeds, and keep the plant out of reach of children and animals. If any part has been eaten, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

The species of the genus Stangeria

The genus has a single species, accepted by POWO and by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15). It does not yet have an English page on the site.

  • Stangeria eriopus (Kunze) Baill. (1892): the only species in the genus; east coast of South Africa and far south of Mozambique; forest and grassland forms; Vulnerable (VU, 2022); CITES Appendix I since 1975.

Frequently asked questions

Is Stangeria a fern?

No. It is a cycad, a seed plant that produces cones. Its fern-like veined leaves fooled botanists: it was described in 1839 as a fern, Lomaria eriopus, before a cone revealed its nature.

How many species of Stangeria are there?

Only one, Stangeria eriopus. The forest and grassland forms, though very different in appearance, belong to the same species according to POWO and The World List of Cycads.

Are Stangeria paradoxa and Stangeria eriopus the same plant?

Yes. Stangeria paradoxa is the name given by Moore in 1853. In 1892 Baillon transferred Kunze's older epithet, eriopus, which has priority; Stangeria paradoxa is therefore a synonym.

Does Stangeria belong to the Stangeriaceae or the Zamiaceae?

To the Zamiaceae, according to POWO. Molecular studies have shown that Stangeria is close to the American genera Zamia and Microcycas, not to Bowenia. The name Stangeriaceae is still common in older books and in CITES documents.

Is Stangeria frost-hardy?

Only slightly. The leaves are destroyed at about −4 °C (24.8 °F), but the buried stem can regrow if the ground does not freeze deeply. At the Jardin zoologique tropical in the south of France, a plant in the ground survived February 2012 (−7 °C, 19.4 °F) with its foliage destroyed; weak in the ground, it is now grown in a pot, where it does much better: in an unheated glasshouse in winter, outdoors from Easter to the first frosts. In cool climates such as the UK, grow it in a pot and keep it frost-free in winter.

Can I buy a Stangeria legally?

Yes, if it has been propagated in cultivation and comes with the required documents. The species has been in CITES Appendix I since 1975: wild-collected plants cannot be traded internationally, and seeds are not exempt. Ask the seller for proof of origin.

Is Stangeria poisonous?

Yes. Its underground stem contains cycasin and macrozamin, like other cycads. Keep the plant and its seeds out of reach of children and animals, and contact a poison centre if any part is swallowed.
