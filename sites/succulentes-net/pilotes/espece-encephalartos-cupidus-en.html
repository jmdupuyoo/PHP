Encephalartos cupidus is a small cycad endemic to South Africa, where it grows on the Drakensberg escarpment of Mpumalanga, between the Blyde and Steelpoort rivers, on steep rocky slopes and grassy cliffs. This dwarf species of the genus Encephalartos, with glaucous blue-green leaves armed with sharp teeth and bright apple-green cones, was described in 1971 under a name meaning “desirous”. Little more than fifty plants were left in the wild in 2015, against over a thousand in 1984.

Key facts about Encephalartos cupidus

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos cupidus R.A.Dyer (1971), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15)
  • Former names and synonyms: no synonym
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Blyde River cycad (English, Species+ and SANBI)
  • Natural range: South Africa, Mpumalanga (escarpment between the Blyde and Steelpoort rivers); declared extinct in Limpopo in 2004 according to SANBI, although unconfirmed reports of a population there persist
  • IUCN status: Critically Endangered (CR), assessment published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I (whole genus, since 1977); Annex A in the European Union; Specially Protected in Mpumalanga; wild collection banned throughout South Africa since 2007
  • Documented hardiness: described as frost-hardy by SANBI, without any measured value; no dated cold report (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, steep rocky slopes, cliffs and open grassland; in cultivation, full sun, deep well-drained soil, drought-tolerant according to SANBI
  • Habit and adult size: dwarf, single-stemmed or branching from the base and suckering; trunk usually underground, about 15 cm according to SANBI, up to about 75 cm according to the original description; leaves 0.5 to 1 m
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Montgomery Botanical Center and Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden in Florida, several Californian gardens and the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney; no British record
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; treat it as poisonous like all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos cupidus

According to its author, three characters taken together set it apart from its South African neighbours: its small size, its more or less glaucous leaves with strongly spiny leaflets, and the large terminal face of the female cone scales. The original description by Robert Allen Dyer (1971) gives these characters:

  • Habit: trunk single or branching at the base, up to about 75 cm tall and 20 to 30 cm in diameter, covered in old leaf bases and scale leaves; scale leaves about 5 cm long, lance-shaped and covered in whitish wool
  • Leaves: about 1 m long, linear to narrowly oblong in outline; rachis straight or spreading and curved, the tip turned up rather than down; glaucous green
  • Leaflets: glaucous green, up to 3 cm apart towards the base of the leaf, closer and often slightly overlapping in the upper half, held in a V on either side of the rachis; reduced to 2 or 3 prickles at the base; middle leaflets linear-lanceolate, slightly upturned, 10 to 15 cm (rarely 19 cm) long and 10 to 13 mm wide, with 2 to 5 sharp, forward-pointing prickles 4 to 7 mm long on each margin and a pungent tip
  • Cones: apparently solitary at the tip of each trunk, hairless; male cone subcylindrical, about 18 cm long and 5 cm wide, on a stalk 5 to 10 cm long; female cone (immature) ovoid, 18 to 20 cm long and 10 cm wide, bright apple green
  • Female cone scales: terminal face 2.5 to 3 cm wide and 1.5 to 2 cm high, remarkably large

The PlantZAfrica account of SANBI (K. W. Sepheka, 2015) describes a much shorter plant: a dwarf with a usually underground trunk about 15 cm long and 15 cm across, often producing 10 to 15 suckers, rigid leaves 0.5 to 1 m long curving downwards near the tip, and leathery leaflets 10 to 16 mm wide with 3 or 4 sharp teeth on each margin. Each stem bears one cone per season; male cones, bright apple green turning yellowish when ripe, are 18 to 30 cm long and 5 to 8 cm wide and shed pollen from January to March; female cones are 18 to 20 cm long and 12 to 14 cm wide and break up from May to July, releasing about 120 seeds. The San Diego nursery Jungle Music describes leaves ranging from blue-green to powdery silver, bright green female cones that occasionally turn yellow-orange and blue-green male cones.

Hybrids

No natural hybrid of Encephalartos cupidus has been described. On the French Fous de palmiers forum, members discussed in 2013 a plant with twisted leaves offered as a possible Encephalartos cupidus or hybrid; an experienced collector thought it was more likely Encephalartos dyerianus. The exchange shows how hard these blue-leaved species are to tell apart without cones.

Possible confusion

Dyer considered Encephalartos munchii, from Mozambique, its closest relative on the characters he used; that species has much larger cones, the male ones tall, loose and often several to a plant. Within its region, confusion is most likely with the other blue-leaved Encephalartos of north-eastern South Africa, such as Encephalartos middelburgensis or Encephalartos dyerianus. The combination to look for is a dwarf plant, narrow leaflets (10 to 16 mm) with several prickles on both margins, and hairless, bright green cones.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos cupidus is an accepted name with no synonyms. Robert Allen Dyer, of the Botanical Research Institute in Pretoria, published it in 1971 in Bothalia (10(2): 379–383) under the title “A further new species of Cycad from the Transvaal”. The holotype is a plant cultivated at White River, in the Pilgrim's Rest division of the former Transvaal, in the garden of D. van Heerden (PRE 30546, National Herbarium, Pretoria). Plants from the Blyde River Nature Reserve (Steenveld farm), collected by the conservation officer G. Alberts, complete the material (PRE 30545).

Dyer placed the species near Encephalartos munchii, described two years earlier. No name change or revision has affected it since. The World List of Cycads, the checklist of the IUCN Cycad Specialist Group, accepts it in its version of 15 August 2026.

Etymology and history

Cupidus is a Latin adjective meaning “desirous” or “eager”. Dyer explained his choice: interest in Encephalartos as garden plants is such that the name could fit, in the good sense, any species of the genus; but he also meant to recall the tarnished sense of the word, desire turned into greed, that of collectors whom conservation laws were failing to deter. Haynes (2022) sums it up: the author thought the plant so striking that collectors would desire it.

The first trace of the species is a leaf sent to Pretoria by J. S. Oliver from a plant in David van Heerden's garden at White River. That plant came from Steenveld farm, on the boundary between the Pilgrim's Rest and Lydenburg districts, later incorporated into the Blyde River Nature Reserve. The colony studied by G. Alberts covered a few square miles and held about 120 plants on very broken ground (“Steenveld” means “stony ground”). Dyer stressed his surprise at finding a new species only five years after Encephalartos inopinus.

Common names

The English name Blyde River cycad is used by Species+ and by SANBI. No name in Afrikaans or in an African language specific to the species is documented.

In the wild

Encephalartos cupidus grows on the Mpumalanga Drakensberg escarpment, between the Blyde and Steelpoort rivers. According to SANBI, it occupies open, grassy positions on steep to precipitous rocky slopes and cliffs, sometimes along seepage lines at the edge of riverine forest and in dry forest. PlantZAfrica describes a climate that is hot and humid in summer and cool in winter, with an average annual rainfall of about 350 to 750 mm, falling mainly in summer. The World List of Cycads now records it only in Mpumalanga, and SANBI states that it was declared extinct in Limpopo in 2004. The biodiversity management plan published in 2017 nevertheless mentions unconfirmed reports of a relatively large population within its former range in Limpopo, in mountainous country that is very hard to survey, where such small plants are difficult to spot from the air.

Its decline is one of the best documented in the genus. The non-detriment finding (NDF) of SANBI, approved in 2012 and updated in 2015, reports about 1,110 plants in 1984 in a provincial nature reserve, 861 in 1999 and then only about fifty. At two sites that held about 200 and 61 plants in 1999, Mpumalanga Tourism and Parks Agency staff found only 19 plants (9 young and 10 adult) and 30 plants (21 young and 9 adult); in October 2010, five hours of searching yielded only two seedlings. The few plants that grew outside the reserve were removed long ago. Its pollinators have not been studied.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos cupidus is listed as Critically Endangered (CR) on the IUCN Red List (assessment by J. D. Bösenberg, published in 2022) under criteria A2acd+4acd; B1ab(ii,iii,v); C1+2a(i,ii): a steep past and projected population decline, a small range, continuing decline and very few adults. The category and criteria are those recorded by The World List of Cycads.

The threats are collecting for horticulture and for traditional medicine: SANBI estimates that hundreds of plants have been removed for medicinal use. A three-year undercover operation, completed in 2007, led to the seizure of 548 illegally collected plants, entrusted to the provincial conservation agency and to the Lowveld National Botanical Garden, which harvests about 1,000 seeds a year from them. Further seizures followed (35 plants in 2007, all of which later died; 11 in 2009).

The species is one of the fifteen covered by South Africa's Biodiversity Management Plan for critically endangered and endangered Encephalartos, published in the Government Gazette (No. 40793) on 21 April 2017. The plan sets a long-term target of 1,000 plants in the wild and, as a first five-year objective, the sowing of 500 seeds in secure sites within the former range, using the ex situ collections of the national botanical gardens and seed offered by members of the Cycad Society of South Africa; it also calls for a ground survey in Limpopo.

Like the whole genus, it has been listed in CITES Appendix I since 4 February 1977 and, in the European Union, in Annex A of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, according to Species+, the official CITES and EU database, consulted in October 2026. Species+ also records a negative opinion of the EU Scientific Review Group, in force since 7 February 2013, on imports into the EU of artificially propagated plants from South Africa, whatever their size; a review covering the whole genus was under consultation in September 2026. In South Africa, collecting wild cycads has been banned since February 2007, and the species is Specially Protected under the Mpumalanga Nature Conservation Act (No. 10 of 1998). SANBI notes that 1,180 plants were exported between 1986 and 2011, 92% of them after 1995, when the decline in the wild accelerated, and judges that this trade brought no benefit to the species. Plants sold must come from cultivation and carry the required documents; check the current rules before any purchase or import.

Growing Encephalartos cupidus

PlantZAfrica presents Encephalartos cupidus as a dwarf, multi-stemmed, frost-hardy cycad suited to temperate to tropical areas, which does very well in deep soil and full sun and tolerates drought. It recommends it as a container plant, a focal point or for group planting in low-maintenance gardens, since it needs little water and is undemanding about soil. In cultivation it cones readily: the 2017 management plan notes that this has produced hundreds of seedlings and that the species is now considered relatively common and inexpensive in the South African trade, whereas Jungle Music, in California, describes it as slow-growing, very sought after and one of the more expensive cycads.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

Its habitat, sunny rocky slopes where the trunk anchors in deep crevices, points to a mineral, free-draining soil and an open position. Jungle Music recommends full sun in coastal and inland southern California and part sun in desert areas, in a soil that drains well; because the plant is small, it advises placing it in the foreground of a planting, where it will not be lost among larger species.

Container cultivation

Its small size suits pot culture, in a deep container with a very free-draining mix. A grower in Belley (Ain) and Montpellier (Hérault), France, reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that a poorly rooted sucker bought around 2010–2011 and grown in a pot lost both its leaves after a year and a half, their bases having rotted; he kept the dry but firm sucker, which showed no sign of life for nearly two years, then produced new roots and two new leaves in the autumn and winter of 2013–2014. A weak sucker can therefore recover, but slowly. No published source gives precise watering or feeding needs.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

Montgomery Botanical Center, in Coral Gables, Florida, lists one accession in its online catalogue (20180273, from 2018 according to its number), and Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden lists the species in its Garden Explorer. The Huntington, Lotusland, the San Diego Zoo and the Los Angeles County Arboretum in California, the Marie Selby Botanical Gardens in Florida and the National Tropical Botanical Garden in Hawaii report it to BGCI PlantSearch, which shows that a garden has declared the species at some point, not that the plant is alive today. In Australia, the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney records it in its living collection. No British garden has been found listing it.

Propagation

The species is propagated from seed and from suckers. PlantZAfrica details both methods. Cleaned seeds are laid on their sides, half buried in washed sand or a potting mix, kept at about 28 °C (82.4 °F) and moist but not wet until they germinate; once the radicle is 10 to 20 mm long, each is potted singly, in tall, narrow, perforated bags of about 24 by 12 cm to accommodate the long taproot. Seedlings stay in shade for their first few years, watered daily with a fine spray at first and then about once a week after a month, and can be planted out at three to five years. A sucker is removed with a clean, sharp knife or spade after the soil has been cleared from its base; its leaves are cut off to limit water loss, the wound is treated with fungicide and left to dry for about a week before planting in a sterile medium, ideally in spring or early summer. In the garden, hand-pollination is advised for a good seed set.

Pests and diseases

PlantZAfrica names scale insects, beetles and chewing insects as the main pests. Scale insects suck the sap of the leaves and can be controlled with regular applications of a horticultural oil such as white oil; beetles attack the emerging leaves and are controlled with contact or systemic insecticides or a bacterial preparation. In pots, the main risk is rot of the leaf bases and roots in a mix that stays wet, as in the French report above.

Cold hardiness

No dated measurement of the cold tolerance of Encephalartos cupidus exists. SANBI describes it as frost-hardy, without giving any value.

Mediterranean and European experience

No in-ground trial is documented in France or elsewhere in Europe, and the only plant whose cultivation is described there was grown in a pot, without any temperature being reported. On the Italian Tropicamente forum, a collector stated in 2008 that the species should cause “no problem” with cold, without having grown it himself. In comparable Mediterranean climates, a pot kept dry and frost-free in winter remains the prudent choice until a dated trial is published.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No English-language growing report with a measured value has been found, and no USDA zone is published for the species by a botanical source. Survival in cold conditions among Encephalartos often concerns young plants whose caudex is still underground, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil; in this dwarf species the trunk usually stays underground, but the leaves and crown are not protected, and an older plant with an exposed trunk has no such shelter. In Britain it can only be grown under glass; it is held in collections in southern Florida, coastal California and Sydney, but none of these gardens publishes cold data for it.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data are published for Encephalartos cupidus itself, but the risk is documented in the genus: the toxicity of Encephalartos horridus was demonstrated as early as 1944 by Van der Walt, and Encephalartos, like other cycads, contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; the seeds are the most dangerous part. The ASPCA reports vomiting, diarrhoea, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses that have eaten cycads.

Treat the plant as poisonous: wear gloves to clean seeds, pick up any that fall, and keep plants and seeds away from children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

According to SANBI, hundreds of plants of Encephalartos cupidus have been removed for traditional medicine (muthi). Published sources do not say which parts are used or for what. This trade is one of the causes of its decline.

Frequently asked questions

What does the name cupidus mean?

“Desirous” or “eager” in Latin. Robert Allen Dyer chose it in 1971 with both senses in mind: gardeners' legitimate interest in Encephalartos, and the greed of collectors who dig them up in the wild.

How many are left in the wild?

About fifty plants, according to SANBI in 2015, all in a reserve in Mpumalanga. There were about 1,110 in 1984 and 861 in 1999. Unconfirmed reports mention another population in Limpopo.

How big does it get?

It stays small. Its trunk, usually underground, is about 15 cm long according to SANBI and up to about 75 cm according to the original description, with leaves 0.5 to 1 m long. It often forms several stems from the base.

Is it frost-hardy?

Possibly, but this is unproven. SANBI calls it frost-hardy without giving any figure, and no dated trial has been published. Outside frost-free climates, grow it in a pot and keep it frost-free in winter.

Can it be grown in a pot?

Yes. Its small size suits a deep pot with a very free-draining mix. Water moderately and do not let the leaf bases stay wet.

Can I buy one legally?

Yes, if the plant was propagated in cultivation and carries the CITES documents required for a species in CITES Appendix I and, in the European Union, Annex A. Seedlings from cultivated plants are now produced in numbers in South Africa, but imports into the EU from South Africa currently face a negative opinion of the EU Scientific Review Group; be wary of older plants without proven origin.

Is it poisonous?

It must be assumed so. No data exist for the species, but Encephalartos seeds contain toxins dangerous to people and animals.
