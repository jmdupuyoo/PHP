Encephalartos equatorialis is a cycad endemic to Uganda, known from a single site on the northern shore of Lake Victoria, near Thruston Bay in Mayuge District, where it grows on outcrops of granite and laterite. This species of the genus Encephalartos was described in 1995; in the same year another description named it Encephalartos imbricans, now a synonym. Listed as Critically Endangered by the IUCN, it numbers only a few dozen to a few hundred plants, most of them male, and the seeds collected in recent years have been sterile.

Key facts about Encephalartos equatorialis

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos equatorialis P.J.H.Hurter (1995), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15)
  • Former names and synonyms: Encephalartos imbricans Vorster (1995)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: no English or local name found in a reliable source
  • Natural range: Uganda (Eastern Region), Mayuge District, northern shore of Lake Victoria, at about 1,000 m; a single known site
  • IUCN status: Critically Endangered (CR), assessment dated 19 May 2021 and published in the IUCN Red List 2024-1; the previous assessment (2010) already listed it as CR under criteria B1ab(iii,v)+2ab(iii,v)
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I (whole genus, since 1977); Annex A in the European Union; negative EU opinion on imports of artificially propagated plants from South Africa; most of the population lies outside any protected area
  • Documented hardiness: none; its equatorial habitat is frost-free (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, thin soils over granite and laterite outcrops in grassland, wooded grassland or thicket; in 1995, plants in the open on the west slope of a granite hill; in cultivation, full to part sun and free-draining soil according to a Californian nursery
  • Habit and adult size: trunk up to 3.5 m (rarely 4.2 m) tall and 35 to 45 cm in diameter, suckering from the base; erect, stiff green leaves 3.1 to 3.4 m long (up to 4.1 m); dark green cones (original description)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden in Florida; the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney and Brisbane Botanic Gardens Mount Coot-tha; no British record
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; treat it as poisonous like all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos equatorialis

Look for stiff, spiny green leaves whose hard, toothed leaflets point towards the leaf tip and overlap more and more, like roof tiles, towards the end of the leaf. The original description by Hurter and Glen (1995) gives these characters:

  • Trunk: tree-like, up to 3.5 m tall (rarely 4.2 m) and 35 to 45 cm in diameter, keeping the bases of old leaves; the plant suckers from the base
  • Leaves: numerous, held erect in a dense crown, green and stiff, slightly recurved at the tip, 3.1 to 3.4 m long (up to 4.1 m) and 34.5 to 35 cm wide (up to 43 cm); petiole almost absent (13 mm at most), swollen at the base, first covered in fawn down and then hairless; rachis downy, becoming hairless with age
  • Leaflets: hard, rigid, ascending at about 30° to the rachis, with 3 to 6 teeth on each margin and a single terminal spine; the middle ones 20 to 26 cm long and 2 to 2.6 cm wide; they overlap mainly towards the base of the leaf (succubous arrangement) and then strongly in the opposite direction (incubous) towards the tip, and are gradually reduced to a series of prickles towards the base
  • Male cones: about five per trunk, erect, subconical, hairless, dark green, 30 to 40 cm long and 9 to 10 cm in diameter, on a stalk 20 to 30 cm long
  • Female cones: one to three per trunk, erect, ovoid, dark green, 36 to 40 cm long and 18 to 20 cm in diameter, on a stalk 25 to 31 cm long
  • Seeds: about 200 per cone, with an orange-red fleshy coat (sarcotesta); kernel ellipsoid and smooth, 35 to 38 mm long and 23 to 30 mm in diameter

The Latin epithet imbricans, chosen by Vorster, describes parts that overlap like tiles, which matches the overlapping leaflets described by Hurter and Glen; Vorster's description, published in Novon, could not be consulted. The San Diego nursery Jungle Music, which sells the species, describes cones that are blue with yellow undertones, unlike the dark green of the original description, and leaflets that are shorter, thicker, stiffer and more keeled than those of its relatives.

Hybrids

No hybrid of Encephalartos equatorialis is documented.

Possible confusion

In cultivation, an unlabelled young plant can be confused with other green-leaved Encephalartos of East and Central Africa, such as Encephalartos whitelockii, the other Ugandan species studied alongside it by the Darwin Initiative project (Mpanga gorge, in the west of the country), or Encephalartos ituriensis, from north-eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo. A plant sold under this name should come with documents of origin.

Hurter and Glen (1995) compared the species with Encephalartos hildebrandtii and Encephalartos ituriensis, which also have stiff, toothed, spiny green leaves. It can be told apart even without cones:

  • In those two species the leaflets are arched and supple, point towards the base of the leaf and overlap in the same direction along its whole length; in Encephalartos equatorialis they are straight and hard, point towards the tip and overlap strongly in the opposite direction near the tip
  • Encephalartos hildebrandtii often has two or three spines at the leaflet tip, the other two species a single one
  • The cones of Encephalartos equatorialis are dark green when ripe, against yellowish green (sometimes reddish in some populations of Encephalartos hildebrandtii) in the other two; its erect male cones are about a third of the size of theirs, and those of Encephalartos ituriensis usually hang down

Taxonomy

Encephalartos equatorialis is an accepted name. It is attributed to P. J. H. Hurter alone, in a paper co-signed with H. Glen: “Encephalartos equatorialis (Zamiaceae): a newly described species from tropical Africa” (South African Journal of Botany 61(4): 226–229, 1995). The holotype (P. J. H. Hurter 94U/1, a leaf and part of a male cone), kept at the National Herbarium in Pretoria, was collected on 22 October 1994 at Thruston Bay, at about 1,000 m.

The authors rejected the identification, published by Heenan in 1977, of cycads from around Jinja as Encephalartos hildebrandtii var. dentatus Melville: that Tanzanian variety, which Hurter had seen at several localities, has tooth-like protuberances on the scales of both male and female cones, and the Ugandan plants did not match its description.

In the same year, Vorster described Encephalartos imbricans in Novon (5(4): 388, figs 2–5). The World List of Cycads places that name in synonymy under Encephalartos equatorialis. The exact publication dates, which decide priority, have not been checked, and the position of the species in phylogenies of the genus is not documented.

Etymology and history

The name equatorialis refers to the habitat of the species near the equator, on the northern shore of Lake Victoria (Haynes, 2022). First collected as a type in 1994 and described in 1995, it is one of the more recent discoveries in the genus.

From April 2014 to March 2017, a project funded by the UK Darwin Initiative, “Protecting Ugandan endemic cycads from biodiversity loss and trafficking”, led by the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, with a Ugandan partner (JERA), SANBI and Nong Nooch Tropical Botanical Garden in Thailand, studied the species together with Encephalartos whitelockii and Encephalartos macrostrobilus. Nong Nooch contributed to a cultivation manual for the Ugandan cycads.

Common names

No common name is attested. The English name “Ugandan cycad”, seen online, does not appear in any reliable source, and no name in the local language (Lusoga) is documented.

In the wild

Encephalartos equatorialis grows only on the northern shore of Lake Victoria, about 120 km east of Kampala. According to the Buwerere account of Kew's Tropical Important Plant Areas programme (TIPAs), the only known site in the world straddles the Bukaleba Central Forest Reserve and neighbouring villages. The cycads grow mainly on granite and laterite outcrops with thin soils, in grassland, wooded grassland or thicket; a few isolated plants or groups survive in cultivated plots.

The original description gives a somewhat different picture, that of 1994–1995: the species was then known from a single granite hill on the east shore of Thruston Bay, where the plants grew in the open on the west slope, in heavily degraded moist forest dominated by Rubiaceae and Euphorbiaceae, the surroundings of the hill being entirely cultivated (Hurter and Glen, 1995).

The climate is equatorial: about 1,500 mm of rain a year, a mean annual temperature of 23.4 °C (74.1 °F) in the district, little seasonal variation, and two dry seasons (December to early February, then early July to August).

Population figures vary between studies:

  • in 1995, Hurter and Glen saw no young plants, all the seeds they found were sterile, and the species survived thanks to its abundant basal suckers
  • the TIPAs account cites a population of about 400 adults in two subpopulations a few kilometres apart, with 4 females to 21 males (Kalema and Mucunguzi, 2009); these authors saw no young plants, and a visit in 2023 found very few
  • the final report of the Darwin project (2017) gives 79 georeferenced individuals, with a female-to-male ratio of 0.2
  • the same report states that the seeds collected had no embryo and were therefore sterile, which the authors attribute probably to a genetic cause; pollen was collected and hand-pollination attempted in 2016–2017, too late to raise plants within the project

The pollinators of the species have not been identified; in 1995 Hurter and Glen thought it possible that its specific pollinator had disappeared, and the 2010 IUCN assessment, as quoted by Jungle Music, suggested that the lack of seedlings could also reflect overly frequent fires.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos equatorialis is listed as Critically Endangered (CR) on the IUCN Red List. The assessment in force, by J. Kalema and H. J. Beentje, is dated 19 May 2021 and was published in the Red List 2024-1, with no change of category (GBIF and Crossref, consulted on 26 October 2026; these sources do not give its criteria). The previous assessment, by J. S. Donaldson (2010), still cited by The World List of Cycads, used criteria B1ab(iii,v)+2ab(iii,v): a tiny range, a single location and a continuing decline in habitat quality and number of adults.

In 1995, Hurter and Glen cited mainly collectors (signs of recent removals; several trunks dug up and abandoned had to be replanted), human encroachment and the inability of the species to reproduce. According to the more recent TIPAs account, the threats are:

  • fires lit around the rocks for grazing, which spread to the cycad clumps at the edge
  • clearing for subsistence farming (sugar cane, rice), in one of the fastest-growing districts in the country
  • the planting of pines and eucalyptus in the reserve, licensed for 50 years to a forestry company created in 1996
  • invasive plants, notably Lantana camara, Broussonetia papyrifera and Chromolaena odorata
  • little awareness of the plant among local people; a few leaves are taken as firewood

Most of the population lies outside the reserve and has no formal protection. In 2016–2017 the Darwin project bought a microdot marking system for all the female plants, pending authorisation from the Ugandan authorities.

Like the whole genus, it has been listed in CITES Appendix I since 4 February 1977 and, in the European Union, in Annex A of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383. According to Species+, consulted in October 2026, the EU Scientific Review Group has held a negative opinion since 7 February 2013 on imports of artificially propagated plants of this species from South Africa. Check the current rules before any purchase or import.

Growing Encephalartos equatorialis

Published growing data are almost limited to one nursery. Jungle Music, in southern California, recommends full to part sun in coastal and inland areas and filtered light or shade in desert areas, in a well-drained soil, ideally on a mound; it finds the species slower than Encephalartos whitelockii and Encephalartos ituriensis but eventually more robust, and not as cold-hardy as the South African green-leaved cycads. On the French Fous de palmiers forum, a member reported in November 2008 having a few seeds sold under this name, with no result published afterwards.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

Its equatorial origin, with warm, wet weather all year, means that outdoor cultivation is realistic only in frost-free tropical or warm subtropical climates. Jungle Music advises placing it towards the back of a planting, as it becomes large, with a full crown of long leaves.

Container cultivation

In cooler climates it can only be grown under glass, in a large, deep pot with a free-draining mix, kept warm in winter; this is a deduction from its habitat, not a documented result. Its eventual size, with leaves over 3 m long, makes long-term pot culture difficult.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden, in Coral Gables, Florida, lists the species in its Garden Explorer; Montgomery Botanical Center lists no accession. In Australia, the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney records it in its living collection and declares it to BGCI PlantSearch, as does Brisbane Botanic Gardens Mount Coot-tha; a BGCI listing shows that a garden has declared the species at some point, not that the plant is alive today. No British garden has been found listing it.

Propagation

Seed is the only route documented in the wild, but the production of fertile seed is now the central problem of the species (see In the wild). The plant suckers freely from the base, and it was through these suckers that the population maintained itself in 1995 (Hurter and Glen); no data are published on separating suckers in cultivation or on germination.

Pests and diseases

No disease or pest specific to the species is documented.

Cold hardiness

No hardiness data exist for Encephalartos equatorialis, and its habitat never sees frost.

Mediterranean and European experience

No growing report is documented in France or Italy. Jungle Music's remark that it is less cold-hardy than the South African green cycads points in the same direction: in Mediterranean climates, keep it frost-free under glass in winter.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No outdoor trial outside the tropics has been published. The natural site has a mean annual temperature of about 23 °C (73.4 °F), with little variation through the year (TIPAs). No USDA zone is published for the species by a botanical source; zones attributed to it by some websites are not based on any measurement. In Britain it needs a heated glasshouse; it is held in collections in southern Florida and, in Australia, at Sydney and Brisbane, but none of these gardens says whether its plants grow outdoors or publishes cold data for them.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data are published for Encephalartos equatorialis itself, but the risk is documented in the genus: the toxicity of Encephalartos horridus was demonstrated as early as 1944 by Van der Walt, and Encephalartos, like other cycads, contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; the seeds are the most dangerous part. The ASPCA reports vomiting, diarrhoea, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses that have eaten cycads.

Treat the plant as poisonous: wear gloves to clean seeds, pick up any that fall, and keep plants and seeds away from children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use is documented. The TIPAs account mentions only the occasional taking of leaves as firewood.

Frequently asked questions

Where does Encephalartos equatorialis grow?

Only in Uganda, on the northern shore of Lake Victoria. Its single known site lies near Thruston Bay, in Mayuge District, at about 1,000 m. Its name recalls the nearness of the equator.

How many are left in the wild?

Few, but the figures differ. A 2009 study gives about 400 adults; the Darwin project georeferenced 79 between 2014 and 2017. In both cases males clearly predominate and young plants are almost absent.

Why does it no longer reproduce?

Nobody knows for sure. Females are very few, and the seeds collected by the Darwin project had no embryo. As early as 1995 the authors of the description had seen no fertile seed and suspected that the pollinator had disappeared; the project's report points to a probable genetic cause.

What is Encephalartos imbricans?

A synonym. Vorster described the same plant under this name in 1995, the same year as Hurter. The World List of Cycads accepts Encephalartos equatorialis.

Can it be grown outside the tropics?

Only under glass. No outdoor trial has been published, the species comes from an equatorial climate and a Californian nursery finds it less cold-hardy than the South African green cycads. Keep it warm and frost-free in winter.

Can I buy one legally?

Only with full documents, and it is extremely rare. The species is in CITES Appendix I and, in the European Union, Annex A; plants must come from cultivation and carry the required permits.

Is it poisonous?

It must be assumed so. No study concerns this species, but Encephalartos seeds contain toxins dangerous to people and animals.
