Encephalartos turneri, Turner's cycad, is a tree-like cycad from northern Mozambique, endemic to Nampula province, where it grows on granite hills. This species of the genus Encephalartos, described in the 1980s, is named after the Zimbabwean collector Ian S. Turner. It is recognised by its overlapping, boat-shaped leaflets with a hooked tip and by its yellowish cones with a pink bloom. Assessed as Least Concern by the IUCN, it remains rare in cultivation, and nothing reliable is known about its cold tolerance.

Key facts about Encephalartos turneri

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos turneri Lavranos & D.L.Goode (1988), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15), IPNI, Species+ and GBIF; the article itself bears the volume date 1985 (see Taxonomy)
  • Synonyms: none
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Turner's cycad (Species+)
  • Natural range: Mozambique, Nampula province
  • IUCN status: Least Concern (LC), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated 7 April 2020 and published in 2022; listed as Endangered in the 1997 IUCN Red List of plants
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I since 1977, like the whole genus; Annex A in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: no reliable value (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, low granite hills, in sun or partial shade, mostly in rock crevices; in cultivation, sun or partial shade and a well-drained soil (nursery advice)
  • Habit and adult size: trunks up to 3 m tall and 80 cm in diameter, the tallest often reclining, suckering from the base; leaves up to 1.5 m; cones 28 to 30 cm long (original description)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: no British garden declares it; the Montgomery Botanical Center (three accessions), the Huntington, the San Diego Botanic Garden, the San Diego Zoo, UC Berkeley and the Honolulu Botanical Gardens in the United States; Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria in Australia (BGCI)
  • Toxicity: not documented for the species; seeds toxic, as in all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos turneri

Encephalartos turneri is recognised by its broad, overlapping leaflets with a hooked tip and by its yellowish cones covered with a pink bloom. For its authors, these two characters, together with the boat shape of the leaflets, set it apart from all other species of the genus. The original description, published in the Portuguese journal Garcia de Orta by Lavranos and Goode, gives the following details:

  • Trunks: the plant usually suckers from the base; trunks cylindrical, up to 3 m tall and 80 cm in diameter, the tallest often reclining, covered with irregularly spaced leaf bases; scale leaves (cataphylls) narrowly ovate, acuminate, with whitish wool;
  • Leaves: up to 150 cm long and 37 cm wide near the middle, fairly straight and upright, the older ones less so; rachis rounded, 20 mm thick near the base, slightly woolly on its lower third, green turning yellowish with age, with a few sharp prickles on its first 10 cm;
  • Leaflets: glossy dark green, hairless, about 25 mm apart towards the base of the leaf, closer and slightly overlapping higher up; up to 200 mm long and 30 mm wide, boat-shaped, ending in a single sharp point curved into a hook; in the lower half of the leaf, none or one spine on the lower margin and none to three on the upper margin, near the base; in the upper half, margins entire; very thick texture;
  • Cones: one to three at a time in both sexes, hairless, yellowish with a clearly visible pink bloom;
  • Male cones: subcylindrical, slightly swollen, clearly narrowed at both ends, about 30 cm long and 8.5 cm in diameter, on a stalk 12 cm long and 22 mm thick; median scales 30 by 25 mm, almost perpendicular to the axis;
  • Female cones: more or less ovoid, about 28 cm long and 14 cm in diameter, on a stalk about 5 cm long; scales 60 mm long, with an outer face 70 mm wide and 35 mm thick (fresh measurements), rough and hairless;
  • Seeds: scarlet, 60 mm long and 25 mm in diameter.

Vorster and Heibloem (1995) summarise the species by comparing it with another East African cycad:

  • overlapping leaflets, in the arrangement called succubous, up to 3 cm wide, with a hooked tip rather than a fine point;
  • female cone narrowly ovoid, entirely sessile (without a stalk), with projecting scales (the original description nevertheless gives a stalk about 5 cm long);
  • up to three male cones, on stalks no more than 12 cm long;
  • cones of both sexes yellowish, with a clearly visible pink bloom.

The Californian nursery Jungle Music describes trunks reaching about three metres on very old plants, glossy green leaves 1.5 to 2 m long, and concave, elliptical, slightly hooked leaflets, armed with spines like the petiole; it also notes a buff down on the top of the trunk and on the petioles when the plant flushes, and cones that turn yellow or golden yellow as they age. The original description gives leaves no more than 1.5 m long and leaflets with entire margins in the upper half of the leaf.

Hybrids

No hybrid of Encephalartos turneri is documented.

Possible confusion

According to Jungle Music, Encephalartos turneri recalls at first glance Encephalartos sclavoi, from Tanzania, though there is no doubt that the two are separate species. Vorster and Heibloem (1995) compare it with Encephalartos imbricans, from Uganda, now included in Encephalartos equatorialis: both have overlapping leaflets, but those of Encephalartos turneri overlap the other way (succubous rather than incubous), are broader and end in a hook, and its cones are yellowish rather than green.

Lavranos and Goode considered the affinities of the species obscure. In Mozambique, the species of the Encephalartos manikensis complex, in the west-centre, have green cones; Encephalartos ferox, in the south, and Encephalartos gratus, in the north-west, have orange-red cones, and the former very different leaves. Heenan had told them of plants in southern Tanzania with equally thick foliage, but with green cones and no hook at the tip of the leaflets. Finally, they mentioned a then undescribed species from the Marungu Hills, south-east of Voi in Kenya, with yellow to orange-yellow cones, but without boat-shaped or hooked leaflets.

On the French Fous de palmiers forum, growers hesitated in 2009 and 2013 between Encephalartos turneri and other species, including Encephalartos natalensis, for plants bought without precise provenance. Without cones, the identification of a young plant remains uncertain.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos turneri is an accepted name with no synonyms. J.J. Lavranos and D.L. Goode described it in Garcia de Orta, Série de Botânica 7(1–2): 11–14. The World List of Cycads and IPNI date the publication to 1988; Vorster and Heibloem (1995) and the IUCN action plan (2003) cite the same reference with the year 1985. The article itself bears the footer “7 (1-2), 1985” and states that the manuscript was received on 22 February 1985: 1985 is therefore the date of the volume, the actual date of publication not being given. The holotype (I. Turner sub J.J. Lavranos 22553) comes from granite hills about 22 km south-east of Nampula; the plant collected by Ian Turner had been cultivated at “Springs Farm”, Arcturus, Zimbabwe (spelling of the original description; The World List of Cycads writes “Spring Farms”). The holotype is kept at the LISC herbarium (Lisbon), with isotypes at LISC, LMU and SRGH.

There has been no change of name. The position of the species within the genus has not been studied.

Etymology and history

The epithet honours Ian S. Turner of Zimbabwe, a well-known student and collector of cycads, who provided the specimens and field notes used for the description (Haynes, 2022, cited by The World List of Cycads). Turner himself published a note on the species in 1998 in the journal Encephalartos.

In 2007 the newsletter of the American Cycad Society devoted three articles to the species: a general presentation, a field account and a report on its cultivation in central Florida. According to Jungle Music, a large seed collection took place in the 1990s, with none since. Photographs of plants in habitat, taken in 2010, 2013 and 2014 in Nampula province, appear in the gallery of The World List of Cycads.

Common names

In English, Species+ gives “Turner's cycad”, a name also used in the title of a 2007 article and by Jungle Music. No name in a local language is documented.

In the wild

Encephalartos turneri is endemic to Nampula province, in northern Mozambique (The World List of Cycads). According to the original description, the plants grow about 22 km south-east of the town of Nampula, on low granite hills, in partial shade or full sun, mostly wedged in crevices of the rock. Old trunks often recline, with their tips turned up. The authors did not know whether the plants are sometimes exposed to fire; they noted that the leaflets fall soon after the leaf has died and dropped. On the Italian Tropicamente forum, a photograph posted in 2014 places plants in the Jaiane hills, in Rapale district, some 30 km from Nampula, between 700 and 1,000 m above sea level.

Jungle Music describes hills covered with grassland and fragmented woodland, with rocky, dry soil, where the plants grow mostly in sun, among rocks and boulders, in a hot, dry climate. It also mentions Niassa province, which The World List of Cycads does not include, and considers the species fairly plentiful, thanks partly to the remoteness of its sites. Pollination biology and population dynamics are not documented.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos turneri is assessed as Least Concern (LC) on the IUCN Red List, according to J.D. Bösenberg's assessment, dated 7 April 2020 and published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, GBIF, Crossref). In 2003 the IUCN action plan estimated its population at about 10,000 plants, with a slight decline and no known protected area, and named traditional use as a threat; the species was listed as Endangered in the 1997 IUCN Red List of plants.

Regulation, according to Species+ (consulted through its public API on 26 October 2026):

  • CITES: Appendix I since 4 February 1977, like the whole genus;
  • European Union: Annex A, current list in Regulation (EU) 2026/1383 (in force since 29 June 2026); for artificially propagated plants imported from South Africa (source codes A and D), the EU Scientific Review Group gave a positive opinion for caudices up to 7 cm in diameter (20 June 2011) and a negative opinion above that size (7 February 2013), both still current;
  • past measures: CITES suspended all exports from Mozambique between 2012 and 2018, and the European Union suspended imports of wild-collected specimens of the species from that country from 2008 to 2017 (following a negative opinion of 2006); these measures are no longer in force.

Seeds are covered in the same way as plants. Any plant offered for sale must come from cultivation and carry the CITES documents required by the countries concerned (and, in the European Union, the EU documents). These notes do not replace the official texts: check Species+ and the national authorities before any purchase or import.

Growing Encephalartos turneri

Few independent sources document the cultivation of Encephalartos turneri. At the Nong Nooch Tropical Botanical Garden in Thailand, which grows almost all Encephalartos, the species is grown and hand-pollinated (see Propagation).

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

Jungle Music reports that it is grown successfully by a few enthusiasts in southern California, in full or partial sun in coastal and inland areas and in filtered light in desert areas, and that it requires a well-drained soil. Its crowns are full, symmetrical and fairly upright, and the nursery suggests giving it room in the middle layer of a garden; very old plants can become large, which it does not expect in cultivation. This commercial advice matches what holds for the genus as a whole: a mineral, free-draining medium, watering in the warm season and an almost dry winter.

Container cultivation

No container-specific advice has been published. The only documented failure is a young seedling lost in May 2011 by an Italian grower on the Tropicamente forum, together with two other Encephalartos, after monthly watering (location and conditions not given). In Salento (Apulia, southern Italy), a collector showed in October 2009 the new leaves produced during the summer by his plant, without saying whether it was in a pot or in the ground; in May 2010 another grower, location not given, reported that a plant bought online had arrived with dry roots and was in very good condition after repotting (Tropicamente).

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

The species is rare in collections. BGCI PlantSearch lists 12 institutions holding it in its main record. In the United States, the Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida) lists three accessions in its online catalogue, whose numbers point to 1997 and 2006; BGCI also lists the Huntington, the San Diego Botanic Garden, the San Diego Zoo, UC Berkeley and the Honolulu Botanical Gardens. In Australia, Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria declares it. No British garden declares it. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not a living plant on display today. In Europe, the Palmengarten in Frankfurt declares it to BGCI; the Naples botanic garden did not hold it in 1993–1994 (Gaudio et al., 1997). Jungle Music describes the species as extremely rare in cultivation and occasionally offers older plants, with no seed available recently.

Propagation

Sowing is the only method described. The curator of cycads at Nong Nooch explained in 2022 that, to obtain seed, Encephalartos turneri must be pollinated dry, by placing the pollen as it is into the female cone: the “wet” method, which consists of injecting pollen mixed with water with a syringe and succeeds with other Encephalartos, does not work for this species (Lindstrom, CYCADS, 2022). Jungle Music notes that the species produces basal suckers without forming large clumps. For the general rules, see the article on growing cycads from seed.

Pests and diseases

No disease or pest specific to Encephalartos turneri is documented. The only cultivation failure reported is linked to watering (see Container cultivation).

Cold hardiness

No cold-tolerance value, measured and dated, is documented for Encephalartos turneri. As with all Encephalartos, survivals in cold areas often concern young plants whose caudex is still underground, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil and sometimes by snow; a species with an aerial trunk loses this protection once adult. In the absence of data, a pot overwintered frost-free and dry is the prudent solution.

Mediterranean and European experience

No cold result is documented in France or in Italy; the messages on the Fous de palmiers and Tropicamente forums concern identification, purchase or new growth, not overwintering.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

  • United States: no dated and located temperature record has been published; an article of 2007 is devoted to its cultivation in central Florida (Broome, 2007), and Jungle Music grows it in southern California without giving temperatures.
  • Australia: Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria declares the species, without published winter data.
  • United Kingdom: no data; a heated glasshouse is the only safe choice.

No USDA hardiness zone is documented for Encephalartos turneri in a reliable source.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data have been published for Encephalartos turneri. Like other cycads, Encephalartos contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a toxin affecting the liver, the digestive tract and the nervous system; the seeds are the most dangerous part. Treat the plant as toxic, wear gloves to clean the seeds, and keep plants and seeds out of reach of children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

The IUCN action plan (2003) cites traditional use as a threat to Encephalartos turneri, without specifying what the use is. No published source describes it.

Frequently asked questions

How can Encephalartos turneri be recognised?

By its leaflets and cones. The boat-shaped leaflets overlap towards the top of the leaf and end in a hook; the yellowish cones carry a pink bloom, which the authors of the description had seen in no other species of the genus.

Where does it grow?

In Nampula province, northern Mozambique, on granite hills, notably south-east of the town of Nampula.

Is it endangered?

Not much, according to the IUCN, which assesses it as Least Concern. In 2003 its population was estimated at about 10,000 plants, with no known protected area.

Who was Turner?

Ian S. Turner, a collector and amateur specialist of cycads from Zimbabwe. He provided the specimens and field notes that made the description of the species possible.

Is it frost-hardy?

Nobody really knows. No reliable measurement has been published. Overwinter it frost-free.

How can seeds of Encephalartos turneri be obtained?

By dry hand-pollination. A male and a female plant are needed; at Nong Nooch, pollen mixed with water gives no result with this species, unlike other Encephalartos.

Is it poisonous?

It should be treated as such. No data exist for the species, but Encephalartos contain toxins dangerous to people and animals.
