Encephalartos aemulans is a South African cycad known from a single hill in the Vryheid district of northern KwaZulu-Natal, where it grows against sandstone cliffs at about 1,000 to 1,100 m. This species of the genus Encephalartos forms erect trunks up to 1.5 m (rarely 3 m) tall, crowned with stiff, glossy dark green leaves. Its name, “rivalling” or “equalling”, points to its most unusual feature: male and female cones that look almost identical, both wrapped in thick brown wool. Long known to collectors as a woolly form of Encephalartos natalensis, it was described as a species only in 1990 by the South African botanist Piet Vorster. The IUCN now lists it as Critically Endangered, mainly because of plant theft for collections.

Key facts about Encephalartos aemulans

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos aemulans Vorster (1990), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15)
  • Former names and synonyms: no synonym; informally called “woolly natalensis” before its description (PlantZAfrica)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Ngotshe cycad (English); ngotshe-broodboom (Afrikaans)
  • Natural range: South Africa, northern KwaZulu-Natal (Vryheid district), one known population at about 1,000 to 1,100 m, plus two old male plants some 10 km away
  • IUCN status: Critically Endangered (CR), criteria B1ab(v)+2ab(v), assessment dated May 2020 and published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I (whole genus); Annex A in the European Union; “specially protected” in KwaZulu-Natal; collecting wild cycads is prohibited throughout South Africa
  • Documented hardiness: light winter frosts in its habitat (original description); “tolerates light frost” according to PlantZAfrica; no measured value from cultivation (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, south-facing sandstone cliffs and humus-rich scree; adults in full sun, young plants in the light shade of shrubs
  • Habit and adult size: erect trunk up to 1.5 m (rarely 3 m) tall and 35 cm thick, usually with basal suckers; leaves 1.2 to 1.45 m long, sometimes 2 m
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Montgomery Botanical Center, Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden, Lotusland, San Diego Botanic Garden and San Diego Zoo, among others; an outdoor bed at the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria, Melbourne; no British record
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; treat it as poisonous like all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos aemulans

In cone, the species is easy to recognise: its cones sit without a visible stalk in the crown and are wrapped in thick, light brown wool, and the male cones look much like the female ones. Out of cone, look at the narrow leaflets, 16 to 18 mm wide, which shrink to a series of spines towards the base of the leaf. The original description by Vorster (1990) gives these characters:

  • Habit: tree-like, unbranched but usually producing suckers at the base; erect trunk up to 1.5 m, rarely 3 m, and 35 cm thick; crown densely woolly
  • Leaves: 1.20 to 1.45 m long, sometimes 2 m, glossy dark green (slightly duller and paler beneath, according to PlantZAfrica), straight and rigid; petiole short, 7 to 11 cm
  • Leaflets: pointing towards the leaf tip at 15 to 45°, the two rows forming an angle of about 135°; middle leaflets 12.5 to 15 cm long and 16 to 18 mm wide, very narrowly elliptic, slightly sickle-shaped, with an oblique, pungent tip, one to three teeth on the upper margin and one or two on the lower; the lowest leaflets, spaced 25 to 30 mm apart, are gradually reduced to spines that almost reach the base of the leaf
  • Male cones: two to four per stem, sessile (their 5 to 6 cm stalk stays hidden in the crown), 29 to 33 cm long (up to 38 cm) and 14 to 18 cm wide, lemon yellow beneath a thick brown wool
  • Female cones: sessile, 35 to 41 cm long and 20 to 23 cm wide, green beneath the same brown wool; scales tuberculate
  • Seeds: covered in bright red flesh; kernel 25 to 29 mm long and 15 to 19 mm wide

Until they are nearly ripe, cones of both sexes share the same ellipsoid shape and the same woolly coat, hence the name. At maturity the female cones remain somewhat larger and broader. Vorster also stressed the male cone scales, very broad and rhomboid, without the drooping beak seen in related species. PlantZAfrica gives a wider size range for the male cones (22 to 38 cm long) and notes that the leaflets overlap slightly towards the tip and in the middle of the leaf, but not at the base.

Hybrids

No natural or garden hybrid of Encephalartos aemulans has been reported. Vorster noted that the area around the type locality is unusually rich in Encephalartos, with five other species growing almost within sight, but he did not mention hybridisation.

Possible confusion

Without cones, Encephalartos aemulans is hard to tell from its neighbours. PlantZAfrica lists four possible confusions and the cone characters that separate them:

  • Encephalartos lebomboensis: similar narrow leaflets reduced to spines towards the base, but cones of both sexes never woolly, with a smooth surface
  • Encephalartos msinganus: female cones with only a thin, felt-like brown layer, greenish yellow turning brighter yellow; male cones hairless
  • Encephalartos natalensis: female cones densely woolly at first, but male cones hairless and leaflets broader than 25 mm, toothed and boat-shaped, with thick margins
  • Encephalartos senticosus: cones with a rough surface; the wool of the female cones almost disappears at maturity, and the male cones are hairless

Vorster added that the tuberculate female cone scales recall Encephalartos altensteinii as well as Encephalartos natalensis, but that the dense wool, the sessile male cones without beaked scales and the likeness between the sexes set Encephalartos aemulans apart. SANBI, South Africa's scientific authority for CITES, describes its identification by enforcement officers as “particularly problematic”: a plant sold under this name without documented origin is difficult to verify.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos aemulans is an accepted name with no synonyms. Piet Vorster, of Stellenbosch University, described it in 1990 in the South African Journal of Botany (56(2): 239–243), after a critical study of Encephalartos natalensis led him to treat the plants of northern Natal as a separate species. The type, Vorster 2951a (a leaf and a male cone), was collected on 20 April 1988 in the Ngotshe district at about 1,000 m. The holotype is kept at the National Herbarium in Pretoria (PRE), with isotypes at Kew (K), the Missouri Botanical Garden (MO), Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden in Florida (FTG) and St Petersburg (LE). To protect the plants, the exact locality was not published.

The World List of Cycads, the checklist of the IUCN Cycad Specialist Group, accepts the species in its version of 15 August 2026. No name change concerns it; only its informal garden name, “woolly natalensis”, predates the description.

Etymology and history

The epithet is the Latin aemulans, “equalling” or “rivalling”, a reference to the similar male and female cones (Haynes, 2022, cited by The World List of Cycads). Vorster explained it himself: the cones of the two sexes are “almost alike”. PlantZAfrica translates it as “resembling”.

The plant was in collections long before it had a name. According to PlantZAfrica, it was popularly regarded as a form of Encephalartos natalensis and known as the “woolly natalensis” until 1990. In his description, Vorster noted that plants probably taken from the type locality were already in many private collections, and he suggested that the site be made a nature reserve. According to SANBI, international trade in the species began in 1995: by 2011, 869 specimens, mostly seedlings, had been exported from South Africa, with a peak of 177 seedlings in 2008.

Common names

The English name Ngotshe cycad, after the district of the type locality, is used by SANBI (PlantZAfrica and its CITES assessment) and by Species+. PlantZAfrica also gives the Afrikaans ngotshe-broodboom, built on “broodboom”, “bread tree”, which recurs in the Afrikaans names of several South African Encephalartos on the same website (natalbroodboom, drakensbergbroodboom). No Zulu name specific to the species is documented.

In the wild

Encephalartos aemulans is known from a single hill in the Vryheid district of northern KwaZulu-Natal, between 1,000 and 1,100 m. In 1990 Vorster described a population of several hundred plants there and recorded two old, multi-stemmed male plants about 10 km away, at about 600 m. Despite searching, he found no other population. PlantZAfrica adds that more than 90% of the mature plants grow in one subpopulation on a hillside on a privately owned farm.

The plants favour south-facing sandstone cliffs, in short grassland where they stand in full sun. Others grow in the humus-rich scree below the cliffs, where the smaller plants shelter in the shade of shrubs. All the coning plants Vorster saw were fully exposed; on the north- and north-east-facing cliffs he found only a few, mostly very old, plants. The climate brings summer rain, 600 to 800 mm a year, and light frosts in winter.

Cones mature from April to early July, as in Encephalartos altensteinii, Encephalartos lebomboensis and Encephalartos natalensis; PlantZAfrica places the emergence of male cones in midsummer (January) and pollen shedding in autumn (March to May). On a cultivated plant, male cones still held pollen in August, two and a half months after opening: the dense wool kept the wind from dispersing it, which suggested to Vorster that insects are essential for pollination. Natural seedlings were abundant at the time, and in 2015 SANBI still regarded the many seedlings in the wild population as a sign of good recruitment.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos aemulans is listed as Critically Endangered (CR) on the IUCN Red List under criteria B1ab(v)+2ab(v): a very small range and a continuing decline in the number of mature individuals. The assessment, by J. D. Bösenberg, is dated May 2020 and was published in 2022, according to The World List of Cycads.

Population figures vary. Vorster spoke of several hundred plants in 1990; an aerial survey in 2012 estimated about 600 adults, according to SANBI, without a ground count. PlantZAfrica (2019) cites lower estimates, 150 to 250 remaining plants, from the Red List of South African Plants website, and notes a substantial decline in the 1980s that is hard to quantify. SANBI reports that the landowner supports conservation but that collecting continues: 50 plants were removed in a single incident, plants have been found for sale at the roadside, and only about five plants remained on the small part of the population inside a private nature reserve. The bark of wild plants shows no sign of stripping for traditional medicine.

SANBI also points to laundering: since 2002 the KwaZulu-Natal authorities have issued possession permits for about 150 adult plants of doubtful origin, although no collecting has ever been authorised, apart from a seed harvest for research in 2005. The species is “specially protected” under the KwaZulu-Natal Nature Conservation Ordinance (No. 15 of 1974), and collecting wild cycads has been prohibited throughout South Africa since 2007, and since 2012 under Government Notice No. 371.

Like the whole genus, Encephalartos aemulans has been listed in CITES Appendix I since 4 February 1977, without annotation, so plants, seeds and pollen are all covered. Commercial international trade in wild-collected plants is prohibited; only artificially propagated plants can be exported, from registered nurseries and with the required permits. In the European Union the species is in Annex A of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383. Since 7 February 2013 the EU's Scientific Review Group has held a negative opinion on imports of artificially propagated plants of any size from South Africa. These details come from Species+, the official CITES and EU database, consulted in October 2026; check the current rules before any purchase or import, and ask for proof that the plant was propagated in cultivation.

Growing Encephalartos aemulans

Encephalartos aemulans is grown like the other green-leaved Encephalartos of South Africa: a plant for sun or part shade in very free-draining soil. Published growing data remain scarce. In the wild, adults live in full sun on cliffs while young plants grow in the light shade of shrubs, so young plants are best moved into full sun gradually.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

PlantZAfrica recommends semi-shade or full sun and describes the species as a fast grower when planted in sandy soil. The San Diego nursery Jungle Music, in southern California, advises full sun on the coast and inland, part shade in desert areas, and well-drained soil; it describes growth as moderate but steady, with a new flush of leaves each year. The two sources disagree on the growth rate, and neither gives measurements. Outdoor planting is realistic only where frosts are light and short (see Cold hardiness).

Container cultivation

With no published hardiness data, pot culture sheltered from frost and winter rain is the prudent choice in cooler climates: a large, deep pot of free-draining compost, moved under glass before the first frost. A grower in Belley (Ain) and Montpellier (Hérault), France, reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that he keeps it in a heated conservatory: an offset received without roots in 2011 produced its first leaf in December 2012 but none in 2013, which he put down to the rootless start, and a second plant had still not restarted in August 2013 although its caudex remained firm.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

The species is grown in several American botanic gardens. Montgomery Botanical Center, in Coral Gables, Florida, lists it in its online catalogue (accession 9165, year not shown in the number), and Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden lists it in its Garden Explorer. Lotusland and San Diego Botanic Garden in California, San Diego Zoo and four other American gardens report it to BGCI PlantSearch, which shows that a garden has declared the species at some point, not that the plant is alive today. In Australia, the census of the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria places it in an outdoor bed at Melbourne Gardens, and Cairns Botanic Gardens declares it to BGCI. No British garden has been found listing it.

Propagation

Encephalartos aemulans is propagated from seed and from the suckers it usually produces at the base. PlantZAfrica advises sowing fresh seed in river sand on a heated bench at 24 to 28 °C (75 to 82 °F): germination starts after about three weeks, but some seeds take longer, especially without bottom heat. At the one-leaf stage, seedlings are prone to damping-off. In cultivation, without the insects that pollinate the cones in the wild, hand-pollination is usually needed to obtain fertile seed.

Suckers are best removed during the dormant season, in early spring (July to September in South Africa), once they reach about 8 cm (3 in) in diameter: expose the base, cut cleanly at the point of attachment and treat both wounds with a fungicide or sulphur. PlantZAfrica recommends planting large, well-rooted suckers (at least 25 cm, 10 in) directly in the ground, and letting smaller or rootless ones callus in a cool, dry place for a few weeks before potting. The French grower quoted above found that a rootless offset took more than a year to produce its first leaf.

Any plant or seed offered for sale must come from cultivation and carry CITES documents. Given how hard the species is to identify without cones, buy from a supplier who documents the origin of its seedlings.

Pests and diseases

No disease or pest specific to Encephalartos aemulans is documented. As with other Encephalartos, the main risks in cultivation are root rot in wet compost, especially after transplanting, scale insects, and damping-off in young seedlings.

Cold hardiness

The hardiness of Encephalartos aemulans is poorly known: its habitat has only light winter frosts, and no measured cold value from cultivation has been published. PlantZAfrica says it tolerates light frost. No figure should be read into this.

Mediterranean and European experience

No in-ground trial in France or elsewhere in Europe is documented; the only plants followed on French forums are grown in a heated conservatory. Gardeners in mild Mediterranean climates, coastal California or southern Australia have no published reference point either: treat it as a plant for frost-free or almost frost-free sites, and protect it from long cold, wet spells.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

In the wild the original description records only light winter frosts on the hill of the type locality, between 1,000 and 1,100 m. Survival in cold conditions among Encephalartos often concerns young plants whose caudex is still underground, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil; an adult with a 1.5 m trunk above ground has no such protection. No USDA zone is published for the species by a botanical source. In Britain it can only be grown under glass; it is held by gardens in southern Florida and coastal southern California and grown outdoors at Melbourne, in Australia, but none of these gardens publishes cold data for it.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data are published for Encephalartos aemulans itself, but the risk is documented in the genus: the toxicity of Encephalartos horridus was demonstrated as early as 1944 by Van der Walt, and Encephalartos, like other cycads, contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; the seeds are the most dangerous part. The ASPCA reports vomiting, diarrhoea, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses that have eaten cycads.

Treat the plant as poisonous: wear gloves to clean seeds, pick up any that fall, and keep plants and seeds away from children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use specific to Encephalartos aemulans is documented. SANBI notes that wild plants show no sign of bark stripping for traditional medicine, unlike some other South African cycads. PlantZAfrica records that in Zulu culture most cycads are called isigqiki-somkhovu and are planted at the gate of the homestead to ward off evil spirits; this belief concerns cycads in general, not this species in particular. Its only known use is ornamental.

Frequently asked questions

Why is it called Encephalartos aemulans?

Because its male and female cones look alike. Aemulans is Latin for “equalling” or “rivalling”. The cones of both sexes are stalkless, ellipsoid and covered in the same brown wool until they are almost ripe, whereas in most Encephalartos the male cones are much narrower than the female ones.

How do I tell it from Encephalartos natalensis?

Mainly by the cones. In Encephalartos aemulans both sexes are sessile and densely woolly, and the male cone scales have no beak, whereas the male cones of Encephalartos natalensis are hairless. Its leaflets are also narrow, 16 to 18 mm, and shrink to spines towards the base of the leaf; those of Encephalartos natalensis are broader than 25 mm. Without cones, identification remains difficult.

Is it the “woolly natalensis”?

Yes. According to PlantZAfrica, collectors knew the plant by that name, as a woolly form of Encephalartos natalensis, until Vorster described it as a separate species in 1990.

Is it frost-hardy?

Only to light frost, as far as is known. Its habitat has light winter frosts, and PlantZAfrica says it tolerates them, but no measured value from cultivation has been published. Grow it outdoors only in frost-free or almost frost-free gardens, otherwise in a pot moved under glass in winter.

How many are left in the wild?

A few hundred at most. An aerial survey in 2012 estimated about 600 adults on a single hill in KwaZulu-Natal, while PlantZAfrica cites estimates of 150 to 250 plants. Theft continues, and the IUCN lists the species as Critically Endangered.

Can I buy one legally?

Yes, but only plants propagated in cultivation and sold with CITES documents. The species is in CITES Appendix I and in Annex A in the European Union, which has held a negative opinion on imports from South Africa since 2013. Be wary of large plants with no proven origin.

Is it poisonous?

It must be assumed so. No study concerns this species, but Encephalartos seeds contain toxins dangerous to people and animals. Keep seeds and plants out of reach of children and pets.
