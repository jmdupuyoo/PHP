Macrozamia spiralis is a small cycad from New South Wales, in eastern Australia, that grows as scattered plants in dry eucalypt forest from the Goulburn River valley to the lower Blue Mountains and the outskirts of Sydney. A species of the genus Macrozamia, it has an underground stem and only a few grey-green leaves, more or less twisted into a spiral, hence its name. First described as Zamia spiralis, it was long known as Macrozamia corallipes, while the name “spiralis” was wrongly applied to the much larger Macrozamia communis. Miquel founded the genus on this name in 1842, and the species is now listed as Endangered on the IUCN Red List.

Key facts about Macrozamia spiralis

  • Accepted name: Macrozamia spiralis (Salisb.) Miq. (1842), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and the Australian Plant Census
  • Former names and synonyms: Zamia spiralis Salisb. (1796), the basionym; Encephalartos spiralis (Salisb.) Lehm. (1834); Macrozamia corallipes Hook.f. (1872); Macrozamia tridentata var. corallipes (Hook.f.) J.Schust. (1932); the Australian Plant Census also lists Macrozamia spiralis var. corallipes and Macrozamia tridentata f. dielsii
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales; section Parazamia
  • Common names: “burrawang” (Flora of Australia), a name shared with Macrozamia communis; the Atlas of Living Australia also gives “native pineapple”
  • Natural range: endemic to New South Wales, from Dunedoo and the Goulburn River valley to the lower Blue Mountains, Sydney and Waterfall
  • IUCN status: Endangered (EN A2c), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated September 2020 and published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union; no listing under Australian or New South Wales threatened-species law (Atlas of Living Australia)
  • Documented hardiness: no measured value in cultivation; in its habitat near Richmond, about 43 frosty days a year (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, dry eucalypt forest on poor sandy or gravelly soils
  • Habit and adult size: underground stem 8 to 20 cm across; 2 to 12 leaves 35 to 100 cm long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: grown at the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney, the Australian Botanic Garden Mount Annan and Montgomery Botanical Center; declared to BGCI by 32 institutions, including four in Britain
  • Toxicity: not documented for the species; seeds and leaves toxic throughout the genus (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Macrozamia spiralis

It is recognised by its small size, its few grey-green to bluish leaves, slightly or moderately twisted, and the orange or red callus at the base of its leaflets, whose tips are not sharp. The Flora of Australia (Hill, 1998), online through the ABRS, describes it as follows.

  • Stem: underground, 8 to 20 cm across.
  • Leaves: 2 to 12 per crown, 35 to 100 cm long, moderately keeled, grey-green, semi-glossy; petiole long, 15 to 40 cm; axis not to moderately twisted into a spiral.
  • Leaflets: 45 to 120 per leaf, simple, 12 to 20 cm long and 5 to 9 mm wide, much paler beneath, with a blunt, non-pungent tip; the lowest ones are not reduced to spines.
  • Male cones: spindle-shaped, 15 to 20 cm long and 5 to 6 cm across.
  • Female cones: ovoid, 12 to 20 cm long and 6 to 9 cm across.
  • Seeds: ovoid, 25 to 30 mm long and 20 to 25 mm wide, with red flesh (orange to scarlet according to the flora of New South Wales).

The twist that gave the species its name changes with age. According to L.A.S. Johnson (1959), the semi-juvenile leaves, common above all after fires, may turn through 180° or more and carry widely spaced leaflets ending in a few small teeth; leaves of fully adult form are little twisted, with more crowded leaflets. The flora of New South Wales (PlantNET) gives a twist of up to 180°, rarely 360°, and 5 to 10 prominent veins under the leaflets.

Observers for the Palm and Cycad Societies of Australia (PACSOA) describe, near Richmond, new leaves of a distinctive blue-grey with vivid pinkish-red calluses where the leaflets join the axis; the leaves later turn green and the calluses yellowish or creamy white. Very rarely, new leaves of an albino-like golden-bronze colour appear. They count 6 to 8 leaves per plant on average, rarely up to 17, standing about 60 cm above the ground, at most 90 cm. The leaflets point slightly forward and rise at about 30° to the axis, giving the leaf a broad V-shaped profile. The population of the lower Blue Mountains, from Wheeney Creek to the Mellong Range, has larger plants, with more or less glaucous leaves and broader leaflets (PlantNET).

Hybrids

No natural hybrid is confirmed. Johnson (1959) notes that Macrozamia spiralis sometimes grows close to stands of Macrozamia communis, but that no hybrids are known. To the west it is replaced by the closely allied Macrozamia secunda; one or two inadequate herbarium specimens suggested some interbreeding, but Johnson saw no living intermediate plants. In the Goulburn River valley its range overlaps that of the plants now called Macrozamia plurinervia, without any contact between populations being found.

Possible confusion

The commonest confusion is with Macrozamia communis, for historical reasons (see Taxonomy). In the field, size is enough: according to the PACSOA, Macrozamia spiralis has on average only 6 to 8 leaves about 60 cm tall, whereas Macrozamia communis often has more than 100, up to 2 m tall; Macrozamia communis also has its lowest leaflets reduced to spines. Plants sold under the name have not always been genuine. On the French Fous de palmiers forum, a grower from Cagnes-sur-Mer (Alpes-Maritimes, France) reported in 2010 that his “Macrozamia spiralis” proved to be a Macrozamia communis. Cycadales.eu, a European specialist nursery, states on its product page that the plants and seeds offered as Macrozamia spiralis in previous years turned out to be Macrozamia riedlei, a species from Western Australia; this is the nursery's own statement.

Macrozamia elegans, long known in Sydney as “robust spiralis” and described in 1998, has the same twisted leaves and the same reddish leaflet bases, but it is larger in every part and its leaflets have a sharp tip (Flora of Australia; PACSOA). Macrozamia flexuosa has leaves twisted several times; a plant of Macrozamia spiralis occasionally bears one or two such leaves, which the PACSOA regards as aberrant. Macrozamia secunda, further west, has a concave petiole and stiff, narrow leaflets held erect on one side of the axis.

Taxonomy

Macrozamia spiralis is accepted by The World List of Cycads and the Australian Plant Census, but its history is one of the most tangled in the genus. Richard Anthony Salisbury described Zamia spiralis in 1796 from a plant collected by David Burton “near Port Jackson, in shady places”; no specimen survives. His description is two sentences long: leaflets in 30 to 40 pairs, sickle-shaped, ending in 3 to 5 sharp teeth, and petioles “a little spiral”.

The name was then applied to other plants. Robert Brown (1810) used it for a mixture of Macrozamia communis and Macrozamia riedlei; Miquel, when he made the combination Macrozamia spiralis in 1842, in fact described Macrozamia riedlei from drawings by Ferdinand Bauer; botanists in New South Wales later used it for Macrozamia communis, and those in Queensland, following F.M. Bailey, for Macrozamia lucida. The small species with twisted leaves was known meanwhile as Macrozamia corallipes, a name published by Joseph Dalton Hooker in 1872 in the Botanical Magazine (plate 5943).

In 1959, L.A.S. Johnson re-examined Salisbury's description. Only two species grow close enough to Port Jackson to have been collected by Burton before 1796. Johnson showed that the description cannot fit Macrozamia communis, whose plants old enough to bear 60 to 80 leaflets never have toothed, sickle-shaped leaflets or a twisted petiole, but that it “applies perfectly” to many half-grown plants of Macrozamia corallipes. He therefore restored the name Macrozamia spiralis to the small species, sank Macrozamia corallipes into synonymy and described Macrozamia communis for the large one. In practice, any text earlier than 1959 that speaks of “Macrozamia spiralis” in New South Wales usually concerns Macrozamia communis: Johnson stresses that the morphological studies and chromosome counts published under that name, such as those of Brough and Taylor (1940), apply in fact to Macrozamia communis. Queensland records under the name usually refer to Macrozamia lucida.

Macrozamia spiralis belongs to section Parazamia, the small species with an underground stem whose leaflets lack mucilage canals and whose lowest leaflets are not reduced to spines. Its closest relatives are Macrozamia elegans, described in 1998 by Ken Hill and David Jones, and Macrozamia secunda, which Johnson regarded as its western counterpart. Its chromosome number is 2n = 18 (L.A.S. Johnson, 1963; C.J. Marchant, 1968; as given by the Flora of Australia).

Etymology and history

The name comes from the Latin spiralis, “spiral”, after the twisted leaf axis of young plants (Haynes, 2022, as given by The World List of Cycads). No published source explains the name corallipes given by Hooker.

Since no specimen from Burton's collection survives, the application of the name rests on Salisbury's words alone. The importance of Macrozamia spiralis goes well beyond its size: Miquel founded the genus Macrozamia on this name in 1842, which led to the debate on the type species of the genus set out by Johnson (1959), who considered that Miquel's description was in fact based on Macrozamia riedlei.

Common names

The Flora of Australia gives the English name “burrawang”, but this name refers above all to Macrozamia communis, which keeps the two species confused. The Atlas of Living Australia also lists “native pineapple”, without stating its source. No Aboriginal name specific to the species is attested, and outside Australia the scientific name is used.

In the wild

Macrozamia spiralis is endemic to New South Wales. According to Johnson (1959) and the state flora, it ranges from Dunedoo and the Goulburn River valley to the lower Blue Mountains, Sydney and Waterfall; the PACSOA records it in the outer southern, south-western and western suburbs of Greater Sydney. It grows scattered in dry, rarely semi-moist, eucalypt forest on poor, sandy or gravelly soils.

The plants usually grow singly, but the PACSOA also describes small clusters of 10 to 12 plants within a radius of 2 m and, once, about forty plants over an area of 2.5 by 5 m, probably from seeds that fell at the foot of the mother plant; the isolated plants are thought to result partly from seed dispersal by marsupials. In very rare cases a plant has several heads, probably after damage to the top of the stem; one had seven. New leaves appear irregularly, except after a bushfire, when most plants produce them. Cones seem to appear every three to four years, often with a large part of the population coning in the same year; females usually bear a single cone, sometimes two, and males one to four.

Near Richmond, at 20 m above sea level, the average rainfall is 799 mm a year, on 112 days, a third of it in summer. The extreme temperatures reached at least once a week in July and January are −1.7 °C (28.9 °F) and 34.4 °C (93.9 °F), and frost occurs on 43 days a year on average (PACSOA).

Conservation status and regulation

Macrozamia spiralis is listed as Endangered (EN) on the IUCN Red List under criterion A2c, which refers to a population reduction caused by a decline in the area or quality of its habitat. The assessment, by J.D. Bösenberg, is dated 10 September 2020 and was published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, GBIF and Crossref, checked on 26 October 2026); an earlier assessment, by K.D. Hill, was published in 2010. Part of its range lies within Greater Sydney and its western fringe. The Atlas of Living Australia shows no listing for it under Australian or New South Wales threatened-species law.

Like other Macrozamia, it has been listed in CITES Appendix II since 4 February 1977, with annotation #4: seeds and pollen are exempt, but plants are not. In the European Union it falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026 (Species+, checked on 26 October 2026). Check the regulations in force before any purchase or import, and prefer seed-raised plants of known origin.

Growing Macrozamia spiralis

Macrozamia spiralis is a collector's plant, rare in cultivation, whose main difficulty is its extreme slowness: the PACSOA describes it as an extremely slow-growing cycad, both for seedlings raised in cultivation and for the production of new leaves by adult plants in the wild. No reliable source gives cultivation advice specific to the species. Its natural habitat, open forest on poor, free-draining soil with hot summers and frosty winters, points to a very free-draining substrate and a bright position; these suggestions are deduced from the habitat.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Light: bright; in the wild, under an open eucalypt canopy.
  • Soil: poor and very free-draining, like the sands and gravels of its habitat.
  • Water: the species is grown at the Jardin zoologique tropical in La Londe-les-Maures, in the Var (southern France). Experience with Macrozamia in this garden gives two guidelines: in summer, when there is no rain, watering once a week is a minimum and twice a week ideal; in winter, a dry frost is less dangerous than the same frost following a spell of rain. This Mediterranean experience may help growers in comparable climates, such as coastal California or southern Australia; no cold figure has been published for the plants grown there.

Container cultivation

Given its slowness, its small size and the lack of cold data, a pot overwintered under cover is the prudent choice outside mild climates. Cycadales.eu advises keeping the underground stem buried to avoid dehydration and says the plant likes heat and water in summer; this is a nursery's advice, not a published trial.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney's Garden Explorer lists six plants of a single accession of 1997, of garden origin, in several beds (updated 1 October 2026), and the Australian Botanic Garden Mount Annan four plants of a wild-collected accession of 1987 (updated 15 October 2026). The Australian National Botanic Gardens, Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria (Melbourne Gardens), the University of Melbourne's System Garden and Fruit Spirit Botanical Garden (Dorroughby) also declare it to BGCI.
  • United States: Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida) holds one accession numbered 2024 in its online catalogue. The Huntington (San Marino), San Diego Botanic Garden (Encinitas), the Los Angeles County Arboretum and Botanic Garden (Arcadia), the Gardens of Golden Gate Park (San Francisco) and Brooklyn Botanic Garden also declare it to BGCI.
  • United Kingdom: Glasgow Botanic Gardens, RHS Garden Wisley, the University of Bristol Botanic Garden and Thwaite Gardens (University of Hull) declare it to BGCI; in Britain cycads are essentially glasshouse plants.

In all, 32 institutions declare the species to BGCI (checked on 26 October 2026), the others in Europe, China and New Zealand. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not necessarily a living plant today, and given the history of the name, an old plant labelled Macrozamia spiralis may belong to another species (a caution, not a verified fact). No English-language grower's report has been found.

Propagation

Macrozamia spiralis is propagated by seed. Fresh, fertilised seed is needed, which means a male and a female plant and, outside Australia, hand pollination. As in the other small species of section Parazamia, seedlings grow very slowly. A plant may exceptionally form several heads after damage to the stem, but division is not a documented means of propagation. Wear gloves to clean off the flesh of the seeds, which is toxic.

Pests and diseases

No disease specific to the species is documented. In the wild, the PACSOA notes damage to older leaves by leaf-rolling insects. In cultivation, as for other cycads, too much water in winter and scale insects are the risks most often mentioned for the genus.

Cold hardiness

The cold hardiness of Macrozamia spiralis has not been documented by cultivation trials: no cold figure has been published, although the species is grown in the south of France, at the Jardin zoologique tropical in La Londe-les-Maures. No published source gives a USDA zone based on a trial.

Mediterranean and European experience

At the Jardin zoologique tropical (Var), the species is grown but no cold data have been published; experience with the genus there shows that a dry frost is less dangerous than the same frost after rain. Elsewhere in Europe, no in-ground result is documented, and plants grown under the name in France have often proved to be Macrozamia communis, whose results cannot be transferred. In 2014, a member of the Fous de palmiers forum relayed the ranking of an Australian cycad specialist, who placed Macrozamia spiralis among the “relatively hardy” species of the New South Wales coast, without giving a temperature.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

In its habitat near Richmond, the species experiences frequent frosts, about 43 days a year, with winter minima of around −1.7 °C (28.9 °F) reached every week in July (PACSOA). These are climate data, not measurements of cold tolerance. The only rating found is commercial: Cycadales.eu calls the species “probably hardy to zone 9b, maybe zone 9a, to be tested”, zone 9b corresponding to an average annual minimum of −3.9 to −1.1 °C (25 to 30 °F) and zone 9a to −6.7 to −3.9 °C (20 to 25 °F). Until trial results are available, a pot sheltered in winter is the safe choice in Britain and in the colder parts of the United States.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data have been published for Macrozamia spiralis under its current name, but the risk is well documented for the genus. Macrozamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a poison of the liver; their leaves can cause a locomotor disorder of the hindquarters in cattle, linked to damage to the spinal cord (Healy, 1967).

Beware of old references. Healy (1967) cites a poisoning published in 1931 by Seddon, Belschner and King, in which 30% of a flock of 6,000 Merino ewes in New South Wales died after eating ripe seeds of “Macrozamia spiralis”, with no precise date or place. Much early chemistry of Macrozamia was likewise published under that name in New South Wales, such as J.M. Petrie's chemical examination (1920) and Joyce M. Cooper's isolation of a toxic principle from the seeds (1941). At that time the name usually meant Macrozamia communis in that state (see Taxonomy), so the species actually involved cannot be established; we deduce that it was probably Macrozamia communis, without having checked the plant material of these studies.

Treat the plant as toxic: wear gloves to handle the seeds, pick up those that fall and keep them out of reach of children, dogs and cats. In case of ingestion, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

Macrozamia seeds were eaten by Aboriginal people in New South Wales after long leaching, from three days to two weeks in running or still water, and then roasting. Asmussen (2011) lists Macrozamia spiralis among the five species whose consumption is reported, but points out that the accounts using that name (Backhouse, Jackson, Moore) probably concern Macrozamia communis, the commonest species in the state, separated only in 1959. The Flora of Australia adds that the plant has been used to produce alcohol, adhesive pastes and laundry starch; here too, nothing guarantees that these uses concern the present species rather than Macrozamia communis. These are not recipes: never eat cycad seeds.

Frequently asked questions

Are Macrozamia spiralis and Macrozamia communis the same plant?

No. Macrozamia spiralis is a small plant with an underground stem and 2 to 12 leaves less than a metre long; Macrozamia communis is a large cycad that can carry more than 100 leaves. The confusion comes from the old use of the name “spiralis” for Macrozamia communis, corrected by Johnson in 1959.

Why are the leaves twisted?

It is a feature of the species, most visible on young plants. According to Johnson (1959), semi-juvenile leaves may turn through 180° or more, whereas adult leaves are only slightly twisted. The Latin name spiralis recalls this twist.

How can I tell whether my “Macrozamia spiralis” is genuine?

Count the leaves and look at their base. A plant with dozens of leaves and lowest leaflets reduced to spines is very probably a Macrozamia communis; and according to a European nursery, plants and seeds sold under the name in recent years were Macrozamia riedlei, a Western Australian species. Macrozamia spiralis stays small, with a few grey-green leaves, blunt leaflet tips and orange or red leaflet bases.

Is Macrozamia spiralis endangered?

Yes. The IUCN has listed it as Endangered since the assessment published in 2022, because of the decline of its habitat, part of which lies around Sydney. Buy only seed-raised plants.

Can it be grown outdoors?

Nobody knows for certain. It is grown at the Jardin zoologique tropical in the south of France, but no cold figures have been published, and its habitat has only moderate frosts. Grow it in a pot, in a very free-draining mix, and shelter it in winter.

Why does it grow so slowly?

It is in its nature. The PACSOA calls it extremely slow, from seed as well as at adult age; in the wild it produces new leaves only irregularly, mostly after bushfires.
