Macrozamia concinna is a small cycad endemic to New South Wales, in eastern Australia, growing on wooded slopes in the upper Hunter Valley and the ranges bordering the Liverpool Plains, often between 800 and 1,200 m above sea level. This species of the genus Macrozamia has an underground trunk from which rise one to five stiff leaves with a spirally twisted axis. Described only in 1998, it was previously included in Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi subsp. plurinervia, a name still found in older books. In its habitat, frosts are frequent and snow falls from time to time.

Key facts about Macrozamia concinna

  • Accepted name: Macrozamia concinna D.L.Jones (1998), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and the Australian Plant Census
  • Former names and synonyms: no formal synonym; plants formerly identified as Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi subsp. plurinervia (now Macrozamia plurinervia)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Nundle cycad (Species+)
  • Natural range: Australia, New South Wales: upper Hunter Valley to the hills around the Liverpool Plains and the Nundle and Hanging Rock area
  • IUCN status: Least Concern (LC), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated September 2020 and published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II, like the whole genus; Annex B in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: no dated cultivation report; in the wild, frequent frosts and occasional snow (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, slopes under eucalypt cover in dry sclerophyll forest; in cultivation, not documented
  • Habit and adult size: underground trunk 8 to 15 cm across; 1 to 5 leaves 50 to 90 cm long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: declared to BGCI by the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley and the National Tropical Botanical Garden (Hawaii); no British or Australian garden lists it
  • Toxicity: not studied for the species; seeds and young leaves are toxic throughout the genus (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Macrozamia concinna

It is recognised by its few stiff leaves, whose axis makes one or more complete turns, and by its dark green leaflets with upturned margins. The Flora of Australia (Hill, 1998) sums up its distinguishing features as stiff leaves, strongly spirally twisted, on a long, almost cylindrical petiole, with stiff, dark green leaflets with incurved margins. The measurements below come from the Flora of Australia and its New South Wales counterpart, PlantNET:

  • Trunk: underground, 8 to 15 cm across;
  • Leaves: 1 to 5 in the crown, 50 to 90 cm long, dark green, somewhat glossy; petiole 9 to 24 cm; axis strongly spirally twisted;
  • Leaflets: 80 to 120 per leaf, undivided, 14 to 21 cm long and 4 to 6 mm wide, clearly paler beneath, with flat or slightly incurved margins and a tip that is not sharp; the lowest leaflets are not reduced to spines;
  • Male cones: spindle-shaped, 14 to 22 cm long and 4 to 4.5 cm wide, the scales ending in a spine 0 to 13 mm long;
  • Female cones: ovoid, 13 to 15 cm long and 7 to 8 cm wide, with spines 2 to 30 mm long;
  • Seeds: ovoid, 21 to 26 mm long and 18 to 24 mm wide, with red flesh.

Paul Kennedy and Craig Thompson, authors of the account published by the Palm and Cycad Societies of Australia (PACSOA), add some features visible in the field. The axis usually turns several times on itself, so that the leaflets seem to radiate in all directions, “like the branches of a tree”. The leaflets at the tip stand at a more acute angle than the others, and their point of attachment forms a small reddish or creamy-yellow callus. Plants with only one or two leaves are not unusual, and some leaves are not twisted at all.

Hybrids

No hybrid is known. According to the PACSOA account, Macrozamia concinna is geographically isolated from other cycads, which rules out natural crosses. No garden hybrid has been reported.

Possible confusion

It can be confused with the other small New South Wales Macrozamia whose leaves are spirally twisted. The PACSOA relates it to three species: Macrozamia plurinervia, larger, from which it was separated; Macrozamia flexuosa, closer in size and appearance; and Macrozamia fawcettii. Compared with Macrozamia flexuosa, the Flora of Australia gives Macrozamia concinna a slightly narrower trunk (8 to 15 cm against 8 to 20 cm), a shorter petiole (9 to 24 cm against 20 to 35 cm) and stiff, dark green leaflets, whereas those of Macrozamia flexuosa are soft and mid-green. Their ranges do not overlap: Macrozamia flexuosa grows near the coast between Bulahdelah and Lake Macquarie, while Macrozamia concinna occupies the inland hills and ranges.

Taxonomy

Macrozamia concinna D.L.Jones was published in 1998 in volume 48 of the Flora of Australia (page 718), which covers the cycads of the country. It is accepted by The World List of Cycads, has no synonym and belongs to the family Zamiaceae.

Before 1998, these plants were included in Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi subsp. plurinervia, one of the three subspecies that Lawrence Johnson recognised within Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi in 1959. Johnson gave that subspecies a range covering the far south of the Darling Downs in Queensland and the lower parts of the Northern Tablelands, the North-western Slopes and the Upper Hunter Valley in New South Wales, in dry sclerophyll forest or woodland on stony slopes, and noted that the Hunter Valley populations showed a gradual approach to subsp. flexuosa. In 1991, David Jones raised subsp. plurinervia to species rank as Macrozamia plurinervia, observing that the populations of the group are isolated and each has its own characters (Jones, 1991). The plants of the upper Hunter Valley and the Nundle area were then recognised as a distinct species and described as Macrozamia concinna. For the reader, this means that older photographs or seed labelled “Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi subsp. plurinervia” may belong to this species.

The PACSOA account places Macrozamia concinna in section Parazamia, which groups the small species with an underground trunk and few leaves. The World List of Cycads does not use sections.

Etymology and history

The epithet comes from the Latin concinnus, “neat, elegant”, in reference to the compact, tidy habit of the plant (Haynes, 2022, as given by The World List of Cycads; the PACSOA also speaks of its “neat and trim appearance”). The genus name joins the Greek makros, “large”, to the genus name Zamia.

The type specimen was collected by Paul Forster and Peter Machin on 7 December 1994 at Hanging Rock, New South Wales; the holotype is kept at the Queensland Herbarium (BRI) with an isotype in Canberra (CANB). Among the reference specimens cited by the Flora of Australia is a collection by L.A.S. Johnson at Mount Terrible, near Currabubula, dated October 1951: the plant was known to botanists long before it was described.

The PACSOA authors recall that in 1990 they guided Piet Vorster, past president of the Cycad Society of South Africa, on a tour of the cycads of New South Wales; he photographed several plants of Macrozamia concinna (then known as subsp. plurinervia) partly or almost completely covered in snow. The journal of the Cycad Society of South Africa devoted an article to the species in 2007 (Osborne and Randall).

Common names

Species+ gives “Nundle cycad” as its English name, after the town of Nundle, near which the species grows. No Aboriginal name specific to the species is documented.

In the wild

Macrozamia concinna is widespread but scattered. According to the Flora of Australia and PlantNET, it most often grows on sloping ground in dry sclerophyll forest, from the upper Hunter Valley to the hills and ranges surrounding the Liverpool Plains. The PACSOA adds that it grows on steep slopes between 800 and 1,200 m, under a eucalypt canopy with a medium to dense understorey, notably near Hanging Rock at about 900 m. Herbarium collections come from around Lake Glenbawn, east of Scone, from Warrung reserve, Hanging Rock, Blackville and Mount Terrible.

There is no weather station near Nundle and Hanging Rock, so the PACSOA gives figures for Murrurundi, at 466 m and about 40 km south-west of Nundle: 830 mm of rain a year over 87 days; above 35 °C (95 °F) on four days on average in January; below 0 °C (32 °F) on thirteen days on average in July; and, in 2006, a maximum of 40.7 °C (105.3 °F) and a minimum of −6.5 °C (20.3 °F). At Hanging Rock, higher up, frosts are frequent in winter and heavy snowfalls occur occasionally. A member of the French Fous de palmiers forum pointed out in 2014 that the Murrurundi station lies far below the species' habitat, which must therefore have lower minimum temperatures.

Coning is irregular (Flora of Australia). Female plants usually carry a single cone, male plants one or two (PACSOA). No pollination study specific to the species has been published; in Macrozamia, pollination is carried out by specialist insects, thrips of the genus Cycadothrips or weevils of the genus Tranes (see the genus page).

Conservation status and regulation

Macrozamia concinna is not threatened: the IUCN Red List classes it as Least Concern (LC), in the assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated September 2020 and published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, GBIF and Crossref, checked on 26 October 2026). An older assessment by K.D. Hill appeared in the 2010 Red List. The Atlas of Living Australia shows no listing for the species under national or New South Wales threatened-species legislation.

Like all species of the genus, it is listed in CITES Appendix II and, in the European Union, in Annex B of the wildlife trade regulations (Species+, checked on 26 October 2026). International trade in plants and seeds therefore requires permits. In 2014, an Australian grower wrote on the Fous de palmiers forum that collecting seed in the wild was no longer permitted; this point has not been checked against Australian regulations. Buy only cultivated plants with their documents, and check the regulations in force before any purchase or import.

Growing Macrozamia concinna

No published source describes the cultivation of Macrozamia concinna, which appears to be very rare in collections. The pointers below are inferred from its natural habitat and from experience with other Macrozamia; they are guidelines, not tested rules.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Soil: in the wild, well-drained slopes in dry forest; a very free-draining, sandy or gritty soil appears essential.
  • Light: the plant grows under eucalypt cover; a bright position with a little shade in the hottest hours seems closest to its habitat.
  • Water: Murrurundi receives about 830 mm of rain a year, so the plant is not adapted to prolonged drought; but, as with other Macrozamia, cold combined with waterlogged soil should be avoided in winter.
  • Growth: not documented for the species; the small Macrozamia with an underground trunk are reputed to be very slow. In 2014, a grower on the Fous de palmiers forum reckoned that these dwarf species need at least 15 to 20 years to reach adult size.

The observations of the Jardin zoologique tropical in southern France, given on the genus page and in the Macrozamia communis profile, concern other species; none is published for this one.

Container cultivation

With one to five leaves and a trunk less than 15 cm across, this is one of the smallest Macrozamia and can in principle be grown in a pot. No container-growing report has been published; a deep pot suited to the taproot of cycads and a very free-draining mix are the usual precautions.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

  • United States: the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley and the National Tropical Botanical Garden, on Kauai in Hawaii, declare the species to BGCI.
  • United Kingdom: no garden declares it to BGCI, and no other source mentions it there.
  • Australia: no garden declares it to BGCI; it does not appear in the Garden Explorer of the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney (updated on 1 October 2026) or in the census of Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria downloaded on 19 October 2026.

Six institutions in all declare it to BGCI, the others in China, the Czech Republic, Germany and France. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not necessarily a living plant on display today. No English-language grower's report has been found.

Propagation

Seed is the only documented method for this species, and no source mentions offsets. The plant being male or female, both sexes are needed to obtain seed in cultivation, and hand pollination is then necessary in the absence of the pollinating insects. No germination data specific to the species has been published.

Pests and diseases

No problem specific to the species is documented. In Macrozamia grown in Europe, growers' forums mainly mention scale insects and rot in cold, wet soil (see the genus page).

Cold hardiness

No dated cultivation report exists for Macrozamia concinna. The only indications come from its natural habitat and from an expert ranking reported on a forum.

Mediterranean and European experience

No result in the ground is documented in France or elsewhere in Europe, and no recorded cold spell concerns this species. In March 2014, a grower from the Ain and Hérault departments of France reported on the Fous de palmiers forum the opinion of an Australian nurseryman specialising in cycads, who ranked Macrozamia in groups of decreasing hardiness without giving temperatures. Macrozamia concinna was in the first group, the species judged hardiest, together with Macrozamia diplomera among others. This ranking is an opinion, not a measurement. Other members pointed out that the published minimum (−6.5 °C, 20.3 °F, at Murrurundi) comes from a station lower than the plants.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

In its habitat, the species experiences frequent frosts and occasional snow, and photographs taken in 1990 show plants partly covered by snow (PACSOA). No cultivation result has been published from Britain, North America or elsewhere in Australia, and no source gives a USDA zone for the species. Any figure for its hardiness would be a guess, so none is given here.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data specific to Macrozamia concinna has been published, but the risk is documented throughout the genus. Macrozamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a toxin of the liver; the seeds are the most dangerous part, and grazing the leaves, especially the young shoots, causes an irreversible paralysis of the hindquarters in cattle (McKenzie, 2006; see the genus page). Keep seeds out of reach of children, dogs and livestock, wear gloves to clean seeds, and contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay in case of ingestion.

Traditional uses

No use specific to this species is documented. Other Macrozamia provided Aboriginal peoples with edible seeds after lengthy treatment (see the genus page).

Frequently asked questions

Is Macrozamia concinna frost-hardy?

Probably, but no measurement in cultivation proves it. In its habitat, between 800 and 1,200 m, it endures frequent frosts and sometimes snow, and the nearest weather station, lower down, recorded −6.5 °C (20.3 °F) in 2006. No grower has published a dated result.

Is it a subspecies of Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi?

No, not since 1998. These plants were placed in Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi subsp. plurinervia; that subspecies became Macrozamia plurinervia in 1991, and Macrozamia concinna was then described separately in 1998.

How can it be told from Macrozamia flexuosa?

By its leaflets and petiole. Macrozamia concinna has stiff, dark green leaflets and a petiole 9 to 24 cm long, whereas Macrozamia flexuosa has soft, mid-green leaflets and a petiole 20 to 35 cm long. Their ranges also differ: inland hills and ranges for the first, the coast for the second.

What does concinna mean?

“Neat” or “elegant” in Latin. The name refers to the compact, tidy habit of the plant.

Is it endangered?

No. The IUCN classes it as Least Concern in its 2022 assessment. It remains protected by CITES (Appendix II), like all cycads.

Can it be grown in a pot?

Yes, in principle: with an underground trunk 8 to 15 cm across and one to five leaves, it is one of the smallest Macrozamia. No container-growing experience has been published, however; a very free-draining mix and a deep pot are the usual precautions with cycads.

Is it poisonous?

Very probably. No analysis concerns this species, but the seeds and young leaves of Macrozamia are toxic to people and animals. Keep seeds out of reach of children and pets.
