Zamia brasiliensis is a small cycad with an underground stem from west-central Brazil, where it grows in the transition zone between the Cerrado savanna and the Amazon forest, in the states of Mato Grosso and Rondônia. Described only in 2019, this species of the genus Zamia is recognised by its petiole without prickles and by leaflets wider than those of its neighbours. Its herbarium specimens were previously often filed under Zamia ulei. It has not yet been assessed by the IUCN, but its authors propose listing it as Endangered, because it lives in the “arc of deforestation” of southern Amazonia. It is practically absent from cultivation.

Key facts about Zamia brasiliensis

  • Accepted name: Zamia brasiliensis Calonje & Segalla (2019), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and by GBIF; POWO could not be consulted directly
  • Former names and synonyms: no synonym; herbarium specimens formerly misidentified as Zamia ulei
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: none attested, in English or in Portuguese
  • Natural range: Brazil, northern Mato Grosso and Rondônia, Cerrado–Amazon transition zone; at least ten localities known in 2019
  • IUCN status: Not Evaluated (NE); its authors propose Endangered (EN B2ab(i–v))
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (the whole genus; seeds exempt under annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: no data; frost-free tropical savanna climate in its range (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, semi-deciduous forest and wooded savanna, on slightly acid to acid sandy clay loam; no cultivation data
  • Habit and adult size: underground stem 6.4 to 25 cm long and 6.2 to 8.4 cm across; 1 to 6 leaves 10 to 112 cm long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: no garden record found
  • Toxicity: no analysis specific to the species; treat it as poisonous, like other Zamia (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Zamia brasiliensis

The decisive character is the leaflet: 2.4 to 3.5 cm wide in the middle of the leaf and strongly toothed towards the tip, on a leaf whose petiole is smooth, without prickles. The original description (Segalla and Calonje, 2019) gives the following measurements:

  • Stem: underground, single or clustering, globose to cylindrical, 6.4 to 25 cm long and 6.2 to 8.4 cm across. Cataphylls deciduous, triangular, 6.5 to 8.5 cm by 1.2 to 1.8 cm, with orange margins and creamy-orange down.
  • Leaves: 1 to 6 per crown, spreading, 10 to 112 cm long; petiole 10.5 to 61.2 cm, unarmed, abruptly swollen at the base (up to 13.5 mm); rachis smooth. Young leaves are light green; mature leaves dull greyish green to bright green.
  • Leaflets: 2 to 24 per leaf (at least 10 on adults), lanceolate to obovate, strongly toothed in the upper quarter or third; median leaflets 16.7 to 21 cm by 2.4 to 3.5 cm, basal ones 16 to 22 cm by 1.9 to 3.5 cm, apical ones 12.7 to 17 cm by 2.2 to 3.2 cm. The first leaves of seedlings, no more than 10.5 cm long, carry 2 to 4 leaflets of about 4.8 by 1.7 cm.
  • Male cones: 1 to 3 per plant, cylindrical-conical, about 12.8 cm by 1.9 cm when shedding pollen, covered in creamy-yellow down, with a sterile tip covered in orange-brown down.
  • Female cones: usually one (up to five), upright, cylindrical, 8 to 11.5 cm by 3.5 to 4.8 cm, with a marked sterile tip; greenish beige on emergence, uniformly orange-brown when receptive, then with cream-tan scale edges at maturity; peduncle 10 to 26 cm.
  • Seeds: ovoid, 11.9 to 16.6 mm by 7.1 to 9.3 mm, with a very thin orange-red fleshy coat (sarcotesta).

The original description is not entirely consistent from one place to another: it gives 1 to 6 leaves per crown in the description and 1 to 4 in the comparative table; 9 to 10 vertical rows of scales on the female cone in the description, 8 to 14 in the table and text; and a leaflet width of 0.8 to 2 cm for Zamia boliviana in the text and key, against 1.1 to 2 cm in the table.

Hybrids

No natural or horticultural hybrid has been documented.

Possible confusion

According to its authors, Zamia brasiliensis is best compared with two species with underground stems whose ranges are adjacent but do not overlap:

  • Zamia boliviana, from the Cerrado of Brazil and Bolivia, also has an unarmed petiole, but much narrower leaflets (0.8 to 2 cm). Its male cone is uniformly creamy yellow, without an orange-brown tip, and its female cone has fewer vertical rows of scales (6 to 7, against 8 to 14).
  • Zamia ulei, an Amazonian species, has leaflets of similar shape on young plants, hence the misidentifications in herbaria. But its petiole bears stout prickles from an early age, and adults have much larger leaves, up to 4 m according to the authors’ comparative table.

The key published by the authors for the Zamia of southern Amazonia and the Cerrado comes down to two questions: is the petiole prickly (Zamia ulei) or smooth; if smooth, are the leaflets 2.4 to 3.5 cm wide (Zamia brasiliensis) or 0.8 to 2 cm (Zamia boliviana)?

Taxonomy

Zamia brasiliensis was published on 16 May 2019 in the journal Phytotaxa (volume 404, issue 1, pages 1 to 11), in a paper by Rosane Segalla and Michael Calonje. The species name itself is attributed to Calonje & Segalla, in that order, as the paper, IPNI and The World List of Cycads all show; both forms are therefore met, depending on whether the paper or the name is being cited. The type was collected on 18 September 2018 at Itaúba (Mato Grosso), at 295 m (Segalla and Jesus ITA01); the holotype is kept in the herbarium of the Federal University of Mato Grosso (UFMT), with an isotype at INPA in Manaus. The name has no synonym, and IPNI records no homonym.

Before this description, several specimens collected since the 1980s in Mato Grosso and Rondônia remained unidentified in herbaria or were wrongly named Zamia ulei, whose young plants have similar leaflets. The authors recognised these specimens in Brazilian herbaria and then found two populations in 2018. Unarmed petioles are very rare among mainland Zamia: in South America they are known only in Zamia encephalartoides, Zamia boliviana and Zamia brasiliensis. This character, the geographical proximity and the similar cones make Zamia boliviana, according to the authors, its closest relative. No molecular phylogeny consulted specifies its position. In 2023, Segalla, Pimenta and Calonje listed it among the ten accepted Zamia of the Amazon basin, together with Zamia amazonum, and described this group as “the least understood assemblage of species” in the genus.

Etymology and history

The epithet brasiliensis refers to Brazil: according to its authors, it was in 2019 the only Zamia endemic to the country, its range being confined to Brazil (Segalla and Calonje, 2019; Haynes, 2022, as quoted by The World List of Cycads). That statement dates from 2019. Of the species described since, Zamia multidentata (2023), from the Brazilian state of Acre, also occurs in Peru according to The World List of Cycads; the other recent descriptions have not been checked one by one here.

The two populations studied for the description, more than 650 km apart, were visited in September, October and November 2018; the fieldwork was funded by the exploration fund of Montgomery Botanical Center (Florida), where Michael Calonje works, and formed part of Rosane Segalla’s doctoral work at the Federal Institute of Mato Grosso.

Common names

No common name is attested, in English or in Portuguese: neither the original description nor The World List of Cycads gives one.

In the wild

Zamia brasiliensis grows in northern Mato Grosso and in Rondônia, on the uplands of the transition zone between the Cerrado and the Amazon forest. It occupies varied habitats there: dense or open submontane rainforest, semi-deciduous and deciduous seasonal forest, and wooded savanna (cerradão). It has been collected at no fewer than ten localities.

The two populations studied in 2018 show this diversity:

  • Itaúba (Mato Grosso), the type locality, at about 295 m: submontane semi-deciduous forest grading into open rainforest along the rio Teles Pires; during the long dry season 20 to 50 % of the trees lose their leaves. The plants are commoner in the drier, sandier parts.
  • Cacoal (Rondônia), at about 185 m: hill wooded savanna, mainly at the edge of the more open “campo cerrado”, among grasses, shrubs and trees.

At both sites the soil is a sandy clay loam over arkosic sandstone, with a pH in water of 6.6 at Itaúba and 4.6 at Cacoal. The climate is tropical savanna with a dry winter (Aw): according to modelled data cited by the authors, the mean annual temperature is 24.7 to 25.2 °C (76.5 to 77.4 °F), low temperatures 18.9 to 20.6 °C (66.0 to 69.1 °F) and high temperatures 30.4 to 32.2 °C (86.7 to 90.0 °F); annual rainfall is 1,400 to 2,500 mm, with a dry season from May to September (less than 30 mm a month from June to August) and more than 300 mm a month from December to March.

Phenology follows the seasons: cones emerge from September to December, pollination takes place from October to December, pollinated female cones develop from November to August, seeds fall from June to September and new leaves appear from August to November. At Itaúba the authors observed colonies of an unidentified beetle of the genus Pharaxonotha, a possible pollinator, in open male cones and in the soil around the plants; at Cacoal, caterpillars of a butterfly of the genus Eumaeus were eating the young leaves.

Conservation status and regulation

Zamia brasiliensis has not yet been assessed by the IUCN (Not Evaluated, according to GBIF and The World List of Cycads). Its authors recommend the category Endangered (EN B2ab(i–v)): the extent of occurrence covers 139,453 km², but the area of occupancy is estimated at only 53.3 km², the populations are severely fragmented, and the species lives in the “arc of deforestation”, the agricultural frontier of southern and south-eastern Amazonia. From 1987 to 2017, forest cover there fell by 32.45 %, only about 60 % remains, and 34 % of the extent of occurrence has become pasture. Most of the old collecting sites have been heavily cleared and only a few isolated populations survive. A single protected area holds the species, Cristalino State Park (Mato Grosso).

Like every Zamia except Zamia restrepoi, the species is listed in CITES Appendix II, where the genus has appeared since 4 February 1977; Species+ has no separate entry for it, the listing of Zamia spp. covering it. Annotation #4 (the version adopted at CoP20, in force since 5 March 2026) excludes from CITES seeds and pollen, as well as seedlings and tissue cultures obtained in vitro and transported in sterile containers; international trade in the plants requires an export permit. In the European Union the species falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, whose current annexes are set by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026.

Databases consulted on 19 and 24 October 2026 (Species+, GBIF, The World List of Cycads, IPNI). This summary does not replace the official texts: check the regulations in force before any purchase or exchange, and ask the seller for proof of origin.

Growing Zamia brasiliensis

Zamia brasiliensis is practically absent from cultivation, and no grower’s experience has been published. The specialist French nursery Cycadales.eu (2023) places it among the Zamia of lowland forests of Central and South America, which it considers the most difficult to grow outside the tropics: in a European climate these species lack warmth, even in summer, and air humidity. For them it recommends a glasshouse, a conservatory, a bright heated room or even a growth chamber, and notes that these plants generally grow on fairly neutral or acid soils.

That classification is advice for a group of species, and the habitat of Zamia brasiliensis qualifies the picture: unlike rainforest Zamia, it experiences a five-month dry season in the wild, partly in wooded savanna. No source says how this rhythm should be reproduced in cultivation.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

No planting outside its range has been documented. Only frost-free tropical climates could be considered, and even there no trial is known.

Container cultivation

In the UK and other temperate regions, it is a plant for a pot in a heated glasshouse or a warm, bright room, following the nursery’s advice for lowland Zamia; nothing has been published on watering through the dry season.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In short: no record.

  • United States: Montgomery Botanical Center’s online catalogue returns no accession, although the garden funded the fieldwork for the description, and no American garden declares the species to BGCI (both consulted on 24 October 2026).
  • Australia and United Kingdom: no garden declares it to BGCI, and it is absent from the British and Australian catalogues checked in October 2026; the British nursery Trebrown has no page for it.

No report from a private grower was found.

Propagation

No cultivation data exist. The species is dioecious: a male and a female plant are needed and, in cultivation, hand pollination; in the wild this role probably falls to Pharaxonotha beetles, which the authors present as a hypothesis. Seeds are exempt from CITES under annotation #4.

Pests and diseases

No pest is documented in cultivation. In the wild, caterpillars of Eumaeus butterflies, which live on cycads, eat the young leaves (Segalla and Calonje, 2019). In cultivation, the general risks for the genus are scale insects and rot of the underground stem in a cold, waterlogged compost.

Cold hardiness

There are no data on the cold tolerance of Zamia brasiliensis. In its range, the modelled low temperatures stay between 18.9 and 20.6 °C (66.0 and 69.1 °F): nothing indicates exposure to cold, and no threshold can be proposed.

Mediterranean and European experience

No cultivation is reported from southern France or Italy: the French Fous de palmiers forum has no message on the species (search of 19 October 2026), the Italian Tropicamente forum none either, and the Jardin zoologique tropical at La Londe-les-Maures, in the Var, has published no observation of it.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No outdoor cultivation report was found.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity study specific to Zamia brasiliensis has been found, but the risk is documented in the genus: pharmacological studies cited by The Gymnosperm Database found that Zamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived. The ASPCA lists Zamia furfuracea as poisonous to dogs, cats and horses (cycasin and BMAA, a neurotoxic amino acid; vomiting, bloody diarrhoea, liver damage), the seeds being the most dangerous part. In the absence of evidence to the contrary, treat the plant as poisonous: keep seeds and leaves out of reach of children and animals, and wear gloves to clean seeds. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet straight away.

Traditional uses

No traditional use is documented for this species, distinguished only in 2019. Uses reported for other Zamia cannot be attributed to it without a source.

Frequently asked questions

Is it really the only Zamia endemic to Brazil?

That is what its authors wrote in 2019: it grows only in Mato Grosso and Rondônia. Other Zamia grow in Brazil, but their ranges extend into neighbouring countries, and species have been described since; the statement should be read as dated.

How can I tell it from Zamia boliviana?

By its leaflets, much wider (2.4 to 3.5 cm against 0.8 to 2 cm), and by its cones: orange-brown tip on the male cone, more rows of scales on the female cone.

How can I tell it from Zamia ulei?

By the petiole: smooth in Zamia brasiliensis, armed with stout prickles in Zamia ulei, even when young. Adult Zamia ulei is also much larger.

Why was it described only in 2019?

Its herbarium specimens, collected since the 1980s, remained unidentified or were taken for Zamia ulei. Populations had to be found again in the field, in 2018, and the cones compared to establish that it was a new species.

Is it endangered?

Probably. It has not yet been assessed by the IUCN, but its authors propose Endangered: a very small area of occupancy, fragmented populations and rapid deforestation of its range.

Is it hardy in the UK?

No data exist, but in its range the low temperatures stay above 18 °C (64.4 °F): in Britain it is a plant for a heated glasshouse.

Is it poisonous?

It should be assumed so, as for all Zamia: no analysis concerns it, but the seeds of the species studied are highly toxic.
