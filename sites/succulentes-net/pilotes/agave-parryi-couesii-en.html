Agave parryi var. couesii, known as Coues agave or Coues' century plant, is a small, cold-hardy rosette agave native to central Arizona, where it grows on gravelly slopes in grassland, chaparral, pinyon-juniper and oak woodland between roughly 1,100 and 2,100 m. It is one of the smallest members of the Agave parryi complex and differs from the typical form by its narrower, greener leaves, its more open rosette and its smaller flowers. First described as a species, Agave couesii, it was reduced to a variety of Agave parryi in 1939, and that is the rank Plants of the World Online (POWO) accepts today. In the nursery trade it is also sold as "Agave parryi ssp. couesii", a label that does not match the accepted name. Freely offsetting and naturally exposed to snow and frozen ground, it is a good candidate for cold gardens, provided its roots and crown stay dry in winter.

Key facts about Agave parryi var. couesii

  • Accepted name (POWO): Agave parryi var. couesii (Engelm. ex Trel.) Kearney & Peebles (1939)
  • Former names and synonyms: Agave couesii Engelm. ex Trel. (basionym, 1911 [1912]); sold as "Agave parryi ssp. couesii" in the trade (not an accepted name)
  • Family: Asparagaceae (subfamily Agavoideae)
  • Common names: Coues agave, Coues' century plant
  • Native range: central Arizona, United States (POWO; Flora of North America)
  • IUCN status: not evaluated at variety level; the species Agave parryi is reported as Least Concern by secondary sources (not checked on the Red List itself; year not verified)
  • CITES: not listed according to the sources consulted (see Conservation status)
  • Documented hardiness: nursery ratings of USDA zone 6a to 6b (about −23 to −18 °C) for dry, well-drained plants in the ground; wet cold is the main risk (see Cold hardiness)
  • Exposure and soil: full sun; very free-draining gravelly or rocky soil
  • Habit and mature size: rosettes 35–55 cm tall and 40–65 cm across, offsetting freely into clumps
  • Toxicity: sap irritating to skin, sharp spines; risk documented at genus level (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to identify Agave parryi var. couesii

The short answer: think of a scaled-down Agave parryi with narrower, greener leaves, a looser rosette and a strong tendency to form clumps. The Flora of North America gives the following measurements for the variety:

  • Rosettes: 35–55 cm tall and 40–65 cm wide, less tightly imbricate (overlapping) than in the typical variety;
  • Leaves: 25–42 cm long, occasionally up to 47 cm, and 6.5–11 cm wide, with an ovate blade; margins toothed and a dark brown to grey terminal spine;
  • Flowers: 4.3–5.5 cm long, rarely to 6 cm, with a perianth tube of 6–9 mm and an ovary of 2–3.4 cm;
  • Flowering: late spring to early summer.

Horticultural descriptions (llifle, Plant Delights Nursery) add that the leaves are blue-green, noticeably greener than the steel grey-blue of typical Agave parryi, that the tips of the tepals are more densely covered in minute papillae, and that the flower buds are reddish before opening yellow. The plant suckers freely and eventually builds wide colonies of flat-topped rosettes.

Like most agaves, each rosette is monocarpic: it dies after producing its branched flowering stalk, while the offsets around it carry on. No reliable figure for the age at first flowering was found for this variety.

Hybrids

Yes, natural hybrids exist. The Flora of North America states that Agave parryi var. couesii hybridises with Agave chrysantha in Arizona, where the two grow in the same foothill landscapes. A US nursery offers seed-raised plants from a hybrid swarm between Agave parryi and Agave chrysantha found in Yavapai County, under the informal name Agave ×parryantha; that name does not appear as an accepted taxon in the sources consulted. No named garden hybrid involving Agave parryi var. couesii could be confirmed.

Possible confusions

The most frequent confusion is with the other varieties of Agave parryi, whose boundaries are not always sharp in cultivated plants. Agave parryi var. couesii is recognised mainly by size and colour:

  • Agave parryi var. parryi: larger, tighter rosettes of broader, greyer leaves and flowers 5.5–7.5 cm long;
  • Agave parryi var. huachucensis: clearly larger (rosettes 45–75 cm tall and 75–85 cm wide according to the Flora of North America), flowers 6.2–8.1 cm, native to south-eastern Arizona and north-western Mexico;
  • Agave parryi var. truncata: short, very broad, powder-blue leaves with blunt tips, from Durango and Zacatecas in Mexico;
  • Agave parryi subsp. neomexicana: comparable in size, but native further east, in New Mexico and western Texas.

In the wild, hybrids with Agave chrysantha can blur identification: that species is distinguished by its golden-yellow flowers. Because nurseries use "couesii" loosely, a plant's provenance (wild-collected seed locality, reputable supplier) is often a better guide than the label.

Taxonomy

POWO accepts Agave parryi var. couesii as a variety, not as a separate species or a subspecies. It is one of the infraspecific taxa POWO recognises within Agave parryi, alongside var. huachucensis, var. truncata, subsp. neomexicana and subsp. parryi. The species belongs to the genus Agave, family Asparagaceae, and to group Parryanae in Howard Scott Gentry's 1982 classification.

The name history is short and documented by IPNI and POWO. The plant was first published as a species, Agave couesii, by William Trelease in the Annual Report of the Missouri Botanical Garden (volume 22, page 94, dated 1911 but issued in 1912), using a name attributed to George Engelmann, hence the author citation "Engelm. ex Trel.". Thomas Kearney and Robert Peebles then transferred it to Agave parryi as a variety in the Journal of the Washington Academy of Sciences (volume 29, page 474, 1939). Agave couesii is therefore a homotypic synonym: same type, different rank.

The sources consulted do not explain the reasoning behind the 1939 transfer. The morphological differences from typical Agave parryi are ones of degree (size, leaf width, flower length), which fits the choice of a variety rather than a species, but no study justifying the change could be read directly. The later treatment in the Flora of North America (2002) keeps the variety.

What this means for the reader: search for older literature and labels under Agave couesii. The trade label "ssp. couesii" is not a combination found in POWO, and a plant sold under it is simply Agave parryi var. couesii if correctly identified.

Etymology and history

The epithet couesii honours Elliott Coues (1842–1899), an American army surgeon, ornithologist and naturalist who worked in the South-West. The etymology of the genus and species names is covered on the Agave parryi page.

Coues was commissioned as an assistant surgeon in 1864 and posted to the newly established Fort Whipple, near Prescott in Arizona, reportedly because the area was scientifically unexplored; he stayed there some sixteen months and sent large collections to the Smithsonian Institution. Fort Whipple and Prescott are among the localities cited for Agave parryi var. couesii in horticultural references, but no source consulted confirms that Coues collected the type specimen himself. The type and its collector remain unverified here.

No reliable information was found on when the variety entered cultivation outside Arizona. Today it is propagated from seed and offsets by specialist nurseries in North America, some of which state the county of origin of their seed.

Common names

In English the plant is called Coues agave or Coues' agave, and Coues' century plant. "Coues agave" is used by World of Succulents and by native plant nurseries; "Coues' century plant" appears in the catalogue of Plant Delights Nursery (North Carolina). These are book and trade names derived from the scientific epithet, not traditional vernacular names. The broader names "Parry's agave" and "mescal agave" apply to Agave parryi as a whole and are not specific to this variety. No traditional Indigenous name documented for Agave parryi var. couesii alone was found.

In the wild

Agave parryi var. couesii is endemic to central Arizona. The Flora of North America places it in gravelly sites in grassland, chaparral, pinyon-juniper and oak woodland at 1,100–2,100 m.

Horticultural references (llifle, drawing on Gentry) locate it at the north-western edge of the range of Agave parryi, in Yavapai and Gila counties, on the upland slopes of the Agua Fria and Verde river watersheds, with localities such as Date Creek, Congress Junction, Prescott and Fort Whipple, and westward from Wickenburg towards Kingman. The same source mentions isolated populations around 2,000–2,100 m along the Mogollon Rim, the escarpment that marks the southern edge of the Colorado Plateau. Nurseries give wider altitude ranges (Plant Delights: about 1,370–2,440 m) and some mention Coconino County; these figures go beyond the Flora of North America range and are not confirmed by it.

Winters in this upland habitat bring frost, and World of Succulents describes the plant as native to snow-covered, frozen ground. Soils are gravelly and drain fast, which is consistent with the plant's low tolerance of waterlogging in gardens.

Conservation status and regulation

The variety has no separate IUCN assessment that could be found; secondary sources report that the species Agave parryi is listed as Least Concern on the IUCN Red List, on the grounds of a large range, numerous stable subpopulations and presence in protected areas. The Red List page itself could not be consulted, so the year of that assessment is not given here.

Agave parryi does not appear among the agaves listed by CITES according to the sources consulted; within the genus, Agave victoriae-reginae is cited as the species listed in Appendix II. Species+ (the CITES database) could not be consulted directly, so this should be checked there. Collecting wild plants is also subject to local law and to the rules of the landowner or land manager. The plants sold in the trade are nursery-raised from seed or offsets, which is the only responsible source. Outdoor / In-ground cultivation

Grown in the ground, Agave parryi var. couesii is easy wherever winters are cold but dry, and demanding wherever they are cold and wet. All nursery sources agree on the key point: Plant Delights warns that, because of its high-elevation origin, it is very sensitive to excessive moisture in summer as well as in winter.

Site and soil

  • Light: full sun; light afternoon shade is tolerated in hot, low-altitude climates.
  • Soil: very free-draining, gravelly or rocky; heavy soils need raised beds or mounds of gravel, pumice or crushed rock.
  • Position: a south-facing slope, rock garden or the top of a dry-stone wall. Llifle recommends tilting the rosette slightly at planting so that water does not collect in its centre in winter.

By climate type

  • Arid and semi-arid continental climates (interior western United States, high plains): the closest match to its habitat, although no dated, verifiable garden report from these regions was found.
  • Mediterranean climates (Mediterranean Basin, California, parts of Chile, South Africa and Australia): cold is rarely limiting; the risk comes from winter rain on heavy soil. No dated cultivation report for Agave parryi var. couesii in these regions was found.
  • Oceanic and humid temperate climates (Atlantic Europe, British Isles, Pacific Northwest): the hardest case. Phoenix Perennials (British Columbia) advises protecting it from winter rain with extremely sharp drainage or overhead cover.
  • Humid subtropical climates (south-eastern United States, eastern Australia): hot, wet summers add to the risk of rot; a raised, mineral bed is essential.

Care

Water only during establishment and in long summer droughts; once rooted, it needs very little. Feeding is rarely needed. In autumn, remove fallen leaves that lodge between the rosettes, as they hold moisture. The offsets can be left to form a colony or removed to keep a single rosette.

Container cultivation

Yes, it does well in a pot, and its small size makes it one of the easier Agave parryi forms to keep in a container. Use a wide, stable pot, since offsets soon fill the surface, and a very mineral mix (pumice, grit, crushed rock with a little organic matter). Water freely in the warm season when the mix has dried out, and keep it dry from autumn to spring.

A pot gives less cold protection than the ground because the root ball freezes faster. No verified figure was found for the hardiness of potted plants of this variety, so a dry, bright, frost-protected place in winter is the prudent choice in cold regions. In wet-winter regions, a pot under a roof overhang or in an unheated greenhouse is the simplest option.

Propagation

The easiest method is to separate offsets, which this variety produces freely. Detach rooted offsets in spring or early summer, let any wounds dry for a few days, then pot them in a dry mineral mix and water sparingly until new roots form.

Seed is the other route and maintains genetic diversity; some nurseries raise their stock from seed of known Arizona origin (Yavapai County in the case of Plant Delights). Seed from garden plants may be hybrid if other agaves, such as Agave chrysantha, flowered nearby. No precise germination data specific to Agave parryi var. couesii was found; the general guidance for agaves (sowing on a well-drained mix in warmth) applies.

Pests and diseases

The main cause of loss is rot of the roots and crown after prolonged wet conditions, especially cold and wet together. The agave snout weevil (Scyphophorus acupunctatus) attacks many agaves where it is established, and nothing suggests Agave parryi var. couesii is resistant; no report specific to this variety was found.

Cold hardiness

Agave parryi var. couesii is one of the more cold-hardy agaves, with nursery ratings around USDA zone 6 (about −23 to −18 °C), but those ratings assume dry soil. The documented data are ratings from nurseries and reference sites:

  • Plant Delights Nursery (North Carolina, United States): zones 6b to 8b, "possibly colder" (6b ≈ −20.6 to −17.8 °C), for plants raised from Yavapai County seed.
  • Phoenix Perennials (British Columbia, Canada): zone 6b, with protection from winter rain in the Pacific Northwest.
  • World of Succulents: zones 6a to 10b and hardy "to well below −10 °F" (−23 °C), described as native to snow-covered, frozen ground. This is a compilation source.
  • Llifle: reports that the variety has grown without significant damage through wet, cold winters down to −20 °C or lower when drainage is good; no location or year is given.

No dated, verifiable garden report giving a measured minimum temperature was found for this variety, and the ratings above come from the United States and Canada. No dated report for Agave parryi var. couesii was found from the Mediterranean Basin, Atlantic Europe, southern Africa or Australasia. The ratings suggest a mature plant in dry, stony ground can survive about −20 °C; in wet, heavy soil, damage can occur at much milder temperatures. Young plants and potted plants should be treated as less hardy. Duration of the cold, wind and wetness matter as much as the minimum.

Toxicity and precautions

Handle it with care: no toxicity study targets Agave parryi var. couesii, but the risk is documented for agaves in general. The Botanical Dermatology Database attributes agave sap dermatitis to needle-like calcium oxalate crystals (raphides), probably aggravated by steroidal saponins and other sap constituents. Contact can cause burning, redness, itching welts and blisters. A veterinary report (dvm360) describes hives, marked facial swelling, redness and skin sloughing in a dog exposed to agave sap, and notes that digestive upset is also possible.

The sharp terminal spine and marginal teeth are the more immediate hazard. Wear gloves and eye protection when dividing clumps, and plant away from paths used by children and pets. In case of ingestion or of eye or skin exposure, contact a poison control centre, a doctor or a veterinarian.

Traditional uses

No use specific to Agave parryi var. couesii is documented. At the species level, Agave parryi was an important food plant for Apache and other peoples of the South-West: the hearts and young leaves were slow-roasted for days in earth pits, then eaten or sun-dried for storage. Arizona public radio reports that some of the last traditional agave roasts in Arizona took place around the Second World War, including on Yavapai-Apache land. Whether Agave parryi var. couesii itself was harvested in its central Arizona range is plausible given its distribution, but no source confirms it.

FAQ

How cold-hardy is Agave parryi var. couesii?

Very hardy in dry cold, around USDA zone 6 (about −20 °C). Nurseries rate it zone 6a to 6b; no verified garden report of a measured minimum was found. Wet soil in winter lowers that limit dramatically, so drainage matters more than the thermometer.

Is it a species, a subspecies or a variety?

A variety. POWO accepts Agave parryi var. couesii; the old name Agave couesii is a synonym. The "ssp. couesii" often seen on nursery labels is not an accepted combination.

How does it differ from ordinary Agave parryi?

It is smaller, with narrower, greener leaves and a looser rosette. Its flowers are shorter (about 4.3–5.5 cm against 5.5–7.5 cm) and the tepal tips more papillate. Young plants can be hard to tell apart, so provenance helps.

How big does it get?

Small for an agave: individual rosettes reach 35–55 cm tall and 40–65 cm across. Offsets can extend a clump well beyond that width over the years, but growth is slow.

Can it grow in a wet climate?

Yes, but only with help. Nurseries in rainy regions recommend extremely sharp drainage or an overhead cover in winter. A raised, stony bed against a sunny wall, or a pot moved under shelter, gives the best chance.

Is it dangerous for children and pets?

It can be. The sap of agaves irritates skin, and dogs that chew agaves can show swelling and digestive upset. The spines are sharp, so keep the plant away from play areas.

Does it die after flowering?

The flowering rosette does, the plant usually does not. Each rosette is monocarpic, but Agave parryi var. couesii offsets freely, so the clump normally survives. How many years a rosette takes to flower is not documented for this variety.
