Macrozamia communis is the most widespread cycad of New South Wales, in eastern Australia, where it forms dense stands under coastal eucalypt forest from the Taree district south to Bega. Known in Australia as the burrawang, this species of the genus Macrozamia usually keeps its trunk below ground and carries a crown of up to 150 flat, dark green leaves 1.4 to 2.5 m long. It was only described in 1959: before then, botanists in New South Wales called it Macrozamia spiralis, and many older texts published under that name are really about this plant. It is one of the Macrozamia most often grown in Europe, and the IUCN lists it as Least Concern.

Key facts about Macrozamia communis

  • Accepted name: Macrozamia communis L.A.S.Johnson (1959), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15), the Australian Plant Census, GBIF and Species+
  • Former names and synonyms: no synonym; long misidentified as Zamia spiralis and Macrozamia spiralis (Johnson, 1959)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales; section Macrozamia
  • Common names: burrawang (Australian Plant Census, Flora of Australia)
  • Natural range: Australia, eastern New South Wales, from the Taree district to the Bega district, along some 600 km of coast
  • IUCN status: Least Concern (LC), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated September 2020 and published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II, like all cycads not listed in Appendix I; Annex B in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: in southern France, foliage scorched from −4 °C (24.8 °F) and partly or wholly lost between −7 and −9 °C (19.4 and 15.8 °F) in the ground, with regrowth from the stem (reports from 2009 to 2012; see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: open eucalypt forest on old beach sands or thin sandy or stony soils; light shade suits it best in cultivation
  • Habit and adult size: trunk usually underground, sometimes up to 1.2 m above it (1.5 m exceptionally); 50 to 150 leaves 1.4 to 2.5 m long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: grown under glass at Cambridge since 1964 and declared by Kew, Edinburgh and Tresco among others; five accessions at Montgomery (Florida); 194 plants recorded at Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria in 2026
  • Toxicity: the whole plant is toxic, the seed kernel above all (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Macrozamia communis

Macrozamia communis forms a large clump of flat, dark green, rather dull leaves above a trunk that is nearly always buried. The stiff, sharp leaflets spread flat on either side of the axis and point forwards; the lowest ones are reduced to spines. The measurements below come from the Flora of Australia (Hill, 1998), with additions from Johnson (1959) and from the account of the Palm and Cycad Societies of Australia (PACSOA).

  • Trunk: usually underground, drawn into the soil by contractile roots; on shallow soils it may rise to about 1.2 m (1.5 m for the tallest plant seen by the PACSOA authors), 40 to 90 cm across; it very rarely branches.
  • Leaves: 50 to 150 in the crown, 1.4 to 2.5 m long, flat in cross-section (not keeled), dark green, semi-glossy and becoming dull with age; erect at first, then arching; petiole 12 to 40 cm; axis not twisted.
  • Leaflets: 70 to 150 per leaf, 16 to 36 cm long and 8 to 13 mm wide, thick and rigid, with stomata on the lower surface only, set at 45 to 60° towards the leaf tip, with a whitish callus at the base (sometimes pinkish in the Sydney suburbs, according to the PACSOA).
  • Male cones: up to 10 per plant (most often 3 to 5), narrowly ovoid to spindle-shaped, 25 to 45 cm long and 8 to 12 cm wide, the scales ending in spines 5 to 50 mm long.
  • Female cones: 1 to 6, ovoid, 20 to 45 cm long and 10 to 20 cm wide, weighing up to about 5 kg and holding about 150 seeds; the terminal spines of the scales reach 7 to 8 cm towards the top of the cone.
  • Seeds: flattened ovoid, 30 to 45 mm long and 20 to 30 mm wide, with a fleshy coat that is usually red, sometimes yellow or orange; all three colours grow side by side in some stands on the central coast.

Plants, cones and seeds become larger from north to south across the range. According to Paul Kennedy (1997), the average maximum height of the plants is 1.2 to 1.8 m near Gosford and 1.8 to 2.4 m around Batemans Bay.

Hybrids

Johnson (1959) had seen no hybrid, either in the wild or in cultivation, though he expected that some would turn up. Kennedy (1997) later reported hybrids, seen or reported to him, with Macrozamia spiralis, Macrozamia secunda and Macrozamia flexuosa, species of section Parazamia whose ranges overlap with its own, but none with Macrozamia plurinervia. These observations predate the separation of Macrozamia montana and Macrozamia reducta in 1998, so some of the populations concerned may no longer belong to the species as it is understood today. No hybrid has been formally named.

In cultivation, several Macrozamia coning at the same time can cross, so seed collected in a mixed collection is not necessarily pure.

Possible confusion

Confusion arises from its former name, Macrozamia spiralis, from the species split off from it in 1998, and from the other large species of section Macrozamia sold in Europe and elsewhere.

With Macrozamia spiralis

Until 1959, the common cycad of the Sydney region went by the name Macrozamia spiralis in New South Wales. Johnson (1959) showed that this name, based on Salisbury's Zamia spiralis, belongs to another species, and described the burrawang as new. The Port Jackson plants that Robert Brown called “Zamia spiralis”, those that Miquel called Macrozamia spiralis from 1863 onwards, and those studied by Brough and Taylor (1940) are all Macrozamia communis. Yet the two plants are very different: Macrozamia spiralis is a small species of section Parazamia, growing as scattered plants or small groups, with only 2 to 12 leaves standing 60 to 90 cm high, and the PACSOA sees no close affinity between the two.

With Macrozamia montana and Macrozamia reducta

Until 1998, Macrozamia communis also covered the plants now called Macrozamia montana (Hill, 1998) and Macrozamia reducta (Hill and Jones, 1998). According to the PACSOA, the first is very close to it and the second, much smaller, less so. Texts written before 1998 therefore give the species a much wider range, inland to the Armidale and Mudgee districts: those populations no longer belong to Macrozamia communis as now defined. Kennedy (1997) also described stands of dwarf plants (0.6 to 0.8 m) in the Newcastle and Cessnock area; their present placement is not stated.

With Macrozamia moorei and Macrozamia johnsonii

Macrozamia moorei, from central Queensland, forms a true trunk that can exceed 7 m; its leaves are keeled (V-shaped) in cross-section, whereas those of Macrozamia communis are flat, a difference already pointed out by an Italian grower on the Tropicamente forum in 2008. Macrozamia johnsonii, another New South Wales species, once called the “New South Wales form” or “green form” of Macrozamia moorei, has much larger cones, bigger seeds, longer leaves and a taller, thicker trunk (up to 2.4 m) than Macrozamia communis, according to Kennedy (1997). In cultivation, plants sold as Macrozamia moorei have turned out to be Macrozamia communis.

With Macrozamia miquelii and Macrozamia riedlei

Macrozamia miquelii, from Queensland, has thinner, softer and glossier leaflets, short narrow spines on the cone scales (2 to 5 mm wide at the base, against 5 to 12 mm) and smaller seeds (2.5 to 3.5 cm, against 3 to 4.5 cm), according to Johnson (1959). Seedlings sold under that name have sometimes proved to be Macrozamia communis. Macrozamia riedlei, from Western Australia, is of similar size but differs, according to Kennedy (1997), in its leaflets raised in a V on either side of a diamond-shaped axis and in a lighter, sometimes glaucous green.

Taxonomy

Macrozamia communis is an accepted species with no synonym. Lawrence Johnson described it in 1959 in his revision of the Australian Zamiaceae (Proceedings of the Linnean Society of New South Wales 84: 98). The holotype, a female plant, was collected by Joyce Vickery in April 1958 about three miles west of Mossy Point, near Mogo, on the south coast, and is kept at the National Herbarium of New South Wales in Sydney (NSW). Johnson noted that the type represents the most vigorous forms of the south coast, and that the species, though variable, is clearly separated from its relatives in section Macrozamia.

Johnson gave it a range from the Macleay River to Bega and west to the headwaters of the Goulburn River. In 1998, Ken Hill, in the Flora of Australia, and then Hill and David Jones separated Macrozamia montana and Macrozamia reducta, which restricted the species to the coast between Taree and Bega. The chromosome number is 2n = 18 (Brough and Taylor, 1940, cited by Johnson; Marchant, 1968).

A genetic study by Norman Ellstrand, Robert Ornduff and Michael Clegg (1990), based on five populations spread from north to south, found low genetic diversity in the species but strong differentiation between populations: the three southernmost populations resemble one another and stand clearly apart from the other two.

Etymology and history

The epithet comes from the Latin communis, “common”: Johnson chose it because the species is abundant in the coastal districts of New South Wales (Haynes, 2022, as given by The World List of Cycads). The genus name joins the Greek makros, “large”, to the genus name Zamia.

Familiar to every botanist around Sydney, the plant was grown in gardens in Australia and abroad long before it was described, under the mistaken name Macrozamia spiralis, as Johnson himself recorded.

Common names

The Australian Plant Census and the Flora of Australia give “burrawang” as its English name. Johnson (1959) noted that the name, usually spelt “Burrawang”, was by then pronounced “Burrawong”. In Australia the name is sometimes applied to other Macrozamia too.

In the wild

Macrozamia communis is endemic to eastern New South Wales, from the Taree district on the mid-north coast to the Bega district on the far south coast. According to the Flora of Australia, it keeps strictly to the coast in the north of its range but reaches the foothills of the coastal ranges in the south. It grows in wet to dry sclerophyll forest, mainly on old beach sands, but also on thin sandy or stony soils on ridges.

It is the commonest cycad in New South Wales. According to the PACSOA, its densest stands grow on stabilised dunes near the ocean, notably around Batemans Bay, where thousands of plants grow within a 20 km radius and the leaves overlap so much that they hide the ground. On the south coast it forms the dominant understorey beneath spotted gum (Corymbia maculata); on the central coast, beneath Angophora costata. The trunk tends to stay buried on the dunes and emerges more readily on thin sandstone or quartzite soils.

The climate is temperate and humid. The PACSOA gives annual rainfall of 880 to 1,220 mm for Taree, Sydney and Bega, spread through the year; there are 10 frost days a year at Taree, none in Sydney and 61 at Bega, where the temperature falls to −2.5 °C (27.5 °F) at least once a week in July.

Coning is irregular. According to Robert Ornduff (1990), during the coning event of 1987 no mature plant bore cones in three southern populations, whereas up to 68% did elsewhere; reproductive plants in the south carry on average about twelve times as many leaves as those in the north. An eleven-year survey showed irregular coning, neither cyclical nor synchronised between plants (Kennedy et al., 2001, cited by Asmussen, 2011); southern populations release their seed a little later than northern ones.

The species is pollinated by insects. Irene Terry (2001) showed that it depends on two specialist pollinators from different insect orders, the thrips Cycadothrips chadwicki and the weevil Tranes lyterioides, both of which breed in the male cones. Kangaroos and possums eat the fleshy coat of seeds fallen from the cones and “clean” them, and many seedlings come up around female plants (PACSOA).

Conservation status and regulation

Macrozamia communis is not threatened: the IUCN Red List classes it as Least Concern (LC). The current assessment, by J.D. Bösenberg, is dated September 2020 and was published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, GBIF and Crossref, checked on 26 October 2026); an earlier assessment by K.D. Hill was published in 2010. The Atlas of Living Australia shows no listing for the species under national or New South Wales threatened-species legislation.

The species is nonetheless under local pressure. According to the PACSOA, many stands on the south coast have been cleared for housing, farms and tourist developments. In Murramarang National Park, dieback is attributed to an undescribed armoured scale insect close to oleander scale, named Aspidiotus cf. nerii: mortality of seedlings and of plants with a trunk is markedly higher there than in healthy areas (McDougall et al., 2021).

Like all Zamiaceae outside Appendix I, it is listed in CITES Appendix II, with annotation #4 in force since 5 March 2026, and in Annex B of the European Union wildlife trade regulations (Species+, checked on 26 October 2026). Buy plants or seed of documented, cultivated origin, and check the regulations in force in your country.

Growing Macrozamia communis

Macrozamia communis is one of the easiest species in the genus, provided its soil drains very freely. In the wild it grows in open forest under a light canopy; in cultivation, light shade suits it well.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

At the Jardin zoologique tropical in La Londe-les-Maures, on the Mediterranean coast of the Var in southern France, the species grows in the ground, partly in shade. The garden's experience, useful to readers in comparable climates such as coastal California or southern Australia, gives the following pointers:

  • Watering: the plant tolerates long spells without water, but in summer, when it does not rain, one watering a week is the minimum for good growth and two a week are ideal.
  • Exposure: light shade suits it and protects it better from frost; plants growing beneath evergreen trees are damaged by cold less often.
  • Winter: a dry frost is less dangerous than the same frost after a rainy spell; perfectly free-draining soil is essential.
  • Deficiencies: like other Macrozamia, it can show magnesium, manganese or iron deficiency (yellow-green or yellow-speckled foliage), which a complete, balanced fertiliser usually corrects.
  • Reproduction: male cones were photographed in June 2022 and a female cone in August 2022 (see the photographs above).

A slow-release fertiliser, preferably organic, applied in spring speeds growth without excess; a grower in the Paris suburbs noted on the Fous de palmiers forum in 2010 that the plant “responds well to fertiliser”. A mulch 5 to 10 cm deep over about a metre around the plant keeps the roots cool. Young plants are more prone to root rot than well-grown ones, so for planting out it is better to start with a sturdy plant.

Alkaline soil does not seem to trouble it: a grower at Cagnes-sur-Mer, on the French Riviera, wrote in 2012 that his plant on chalky soil “even seems to like it”. Established, acclimatised plants tolerate full sun, but a young plant moved suddenly into the sun will scorch: in the Vendée, on the French Atlantic coast, a young potted plant put out on a south-east-facing terrace in spring 2014 had its leaves burnt, then recovered in light shade. Young plants grow slowly; an adult, on the other hand, produces new leaves steadily and recovers fairly quickly from defoliation, according to a grower who had kept one for some fifteen years.

Provenance matters. According to Kennedy (1997), a New Zealand enthusiast found that plants raised from seed collected near Batemans Bay, in the south of the range, grew much better in New Zealand's temperate climate than plants raised from the small seeds of Gosford, further north.

Container cultivation

In a pot, choose a deep container with good drainage holes and a very free-draining mix; water thoroughly, then let the mix dry out, and keep the plant on the dry side in winter. If it has to spend the winter indoors, a cool, very bright room is better than a heated living room.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

  • United Kingdom: Cambridge University Botanic Garden, whose cycads are kept in its glasshouses, lists in its online catalogue a plant accessioned in 1964; Edinburgh lists the species in its Garden Explorer. Kew, Glasgow, Paignton Zoo, Treborth (Bangor), the University of Leeds experimental garden and Tresco Abbey Garden on the Isles of Scilly also declare it to BGCI. At Tresco, an open-air garden, it is the only cycad declared, but no published source describes the plant.
  • United States: the Montgomery Botanical Center, at Coral Gables in Florida, holds five accessions in its online catalogue, the latest of which (20230284) dates from 2023. Some twenty other American gardens declare it to BGCI, among them the Huntington, Lotusland, San Diego Botanic Garden, the San Diego Zoo, the Los Angeles County Arboretum, the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley, Naples Botanical Garden, Longwood Gardens and the New York Botanical Garden.
  • Australia: the census of Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria, downloaded on 19 October 2026, recorded 194 plants, 129 of them at Cranbourne (86 in the Gum Garden) and 65 at Melbourne Gardens, in outdoor beds; the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney lists it in its Garden Explorer (updated on 1 October 2026). The Australian National Botanic Gardens, Mount Annan, Adelaide, Hobart, the Hunter Region Botanic Gardens, Booderee and James Cook University at Townsville, among others, also declare it to BGCI.

A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not necessarily a living plant on display today, and it does not say whether the plant grows outdoors or under glass. In Canada, a nursery on the Sunshine Coast of British Columbia reported on the UBC Botanical Garden forum in 2006 that it had planted out three-year-old plants of the species, without giving a result for them.

Propagation

The species is propagated from seed; it does not produce offsets. A male and a female plant must cone at the same time and, outside Australia, without the insect pollinators, hand pollination is needed. Wear gloves to remove the toxic fleshy coat before sowing in a free-draining mix with bottom heat. In the wild, regeneration around female plants is abundant. Growth in the first years is slow: on the Tropicamente forum, a grower reported in 2008 that a six-year-old seedling had hardly grown.

Pests and diseases

Scale insects are the main problem. In Australia, the armoured scale Aspidiotus cf. nerii causes dieback in the populations of Murramarang National Park; similar scales have been found on plants grown in botanic gardens, and the authors advise precautions before any reintroduction of nursery-grown plants (McDougall et al., 2021). Fire does not kill the scale but may reduce its effects. In cultivation, too much water in heavy soil leads to rot of the stem and roots.

Cold hardiness

The stem of Macrozamia communis is hardier than its foliage. In its range, frosts are frequent in the south (61 frost days a year at Bega according to the PACSOA), but recorded lows remain moderate. The extension service of Mississippi State University, in its guide to palms and cycads for the American Midsouth (publication 2948, 2020), places the species in USDA zone 9a, noting that some sources say it is hardy to zone 8b and others only to around −4 °C (25 °F).

Mediterranean and European experience

The following reports come from the French Fous de palmiers forum; they are given without names and are only indicative.

  • Montpellier (Hérault, Mediterranean France), winter 2011-12: several plants in the ground, unprotected for years, were completely defoliated by lows of −8 to −9 °C (17.6 to 15.8 °F); all had grown new leaves by summer 2012, with no loss.
  • Roussillon (Pyrénées-Orientales), winter 2009-10: a plant in the ground was undamaged down to about −4 °C (24.8 °F) and lost three-quarters of its leaves at −7 °C (19.4 °F), in a winter with 27 days of frost. The same grower added in 2011 that, without protection or tree cover, the leaflet tips burn at −5 °C (23 °F) and 30 to 50% of the foliage at −7 °C, but that another of his plants came through −7 °C almost unharmed; in December 2012, at −4 °C, his plants turned purplish “as usual”.
  • Hinterland of Cannes and Grasse (Alpes-Maritimes), December 2009: in the ground, unprotected, leaves browned but not burnt at −4 °C (25 °F).
  • Var, February 2012: a large potted plant sheltered under a Phoenix palm had its foliage burnt (−6.7 °C, 19.9 °F, recorded under a carport); new leaves appeared from April and the plant looked presentable again by late August.
  • Paris suburbs: a plant at the foot of a Trachycarpus came through lows of −10 to −12 °C (14 to 10.4 °F) under a cover that kept it dry (posts from 2010 to 2012); in some fifteen years it was defoliated only twice, including in winter 2011-12 under makeshift protection.
  • Béarn (Pyrénées-Atlantiques): a very small plant exposed to −10 °C (14 °F) without protection was putting out a new leaf in May 2012.
  • Near Bordeaux (Gironde): a very small plant in the ground, in the shade of a Trachycarpus, had its only leaf slightly browned at −6 to −7 °C (21.2 to 19.4 °F) (post of June 2008).
  • South of Nantes (Loire-Atlantique, Atlantic France): two plants in the ground, under the light cover of a eucalyptus, were slightly scorched at −6 °C (21 °F) under protection in winter 2007-08; in January 2009, at −8 °C (17.6 °F) and despite protection, one was badly damaged and the other spotted with burns, and both went back into pots. The grower noted in 2013 that the species scorches at his garden from −4 °C (25 °F).
  • Bouaye (Loire-Atlantique), February 2012: a large plant, protected but without a heating cable, was completely “burnt” by the cold spell.
  • Concarneau (Brittany), winter 2011-12: a plant in the ground lost its leaves and was “recovering” in October 2012.

In Italy, at Ravenna, a potted plant left outside, sheltered from rain and wind, came through −3 °C (26.6 °F) (Tropicamente forum, July 2009). At Focene, on the coast of Lazio, a plant bought as Macrozamia moorei and identified from a photograph as Macrozamia communis by another member, in the ground for about four years and never protected, suffered no damage in winter 2011-12 at −2.5 to −3 °C (27.5 to 26.6 °F) (Tropicamente, 2012). The Negombo park on the island of Ischia grows in the ground plants imported from Australia in 1990.

Taken together, these reports suggest that the species can be planted out in mild Mediterranean coastal gardens, under trees and in very free-draining soil, accepting the loss of its foliage in a hard winter; elsewhere, it needs protection that keeps it dry. The figures sometimes quoted online (“−9 °C according to the literature”, “behaves like Cycas revoluta”, −4 °C (25 °F) as the absolute minimum of its range) do not rest on any published trial.

To protect a plant in the ground, wrap the foliage in horticultural fleece and cover the base with a thick layer of straw, which protects the central bud from which the leaves arise.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

In the United Kingdom, the species is grown under glass in the botanic gardens that list it; the only open-air garden that declares it is Tresco, in the mild climate of the Isles of Scilly. In New Zealand, Kennedy (1997) reports that plants from southern seed did better in a temperate climate than plants from the north. In Australia, it is planted in large numbers in the outdoor beds of Cranbourne and Melbourne, well south of its natural range. No English-language grower's report with a dated minimum temperature has been found.

Toxicity and precautions

Yes, Macrozamia communis is poisonous, like all cycads. Macrozamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a toxin of the liver and nervous system; the seeds are the most dangerous part.

The species has been implicated in livestock poisoning in New South Wales. Healy (1967) cites a case described in 1931: 30% mortality in a flock of 6,000 merino ewes that had eaten the ripe seeds of “Macrozamia spiralis”. At that date the name meant, in New South Wales, the plant since called Macrozamia communis (Johnson, 1959), but the source does not allow this to be stated with certainty, and it gives no locality. In August 2006, on the mid-north coast, at least two steers died of acute liver necrosis after probably browsing the leaves of “burrawangs” on a steep riverbank (Glassop and Gill, 2007; the plants were not identified botanically). Local graziers call the nervous form of the poisoning “rickets” or “Burrawang staggers”: lesions of the spinal cord make cattle stagger.

Wear gloves to clean seeds and keep plants and seeds out of reach of children and animals. In case of ingestion, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

Macrozamia seeds were a food of the Aboriginal peoples of New South Wales after lengthy detoxification. According to the review by Brit Asmussen (2011), accounts gathered from the founding of the Port Jackson colony onwards describe seeds placed in still or running water for three days to two weeks, then eaten or roasted in the ashes; around Newcastle, in 1836, the seeds were roasted, pounded and then leached for two to three weeks. These accounts speak of “Zamia spiralis” or “Macrozamia spiralis”, but many probably concern Macrozamia communis, the commonest species of the region. According to Miller et al. (1993), cited by Asmussen, the kernel contains 14.4% carbohydrate. These methods rest on traditional knowledge: never eat cycad seeds.

Frequently asked questions

Where does Macrozamia communis grow wild?

Only in Australia, on the east coast of New South Wales, from Taree to Bega. It forms very dense stands under eucalypts there, especially on the old dunes of the south coast.

Is it the same plant as Macrozamia spiralis?

No. Before 1959, the large common cycad of the Sydney region was wrongly called Macrozamia spiralis. Johnson then described it as Macrozamia communis; the true Macrozamia spiralis is a small species with only a few leaves.

How can it be told from Macrozamia moorei?

By its habit and the shape of its leaves. Macrozamia communis usually keeps its trunk underground and has flat leaves; Macrozamia moorei forms a tall trunk and has keeled, V-shaped leaves.

Is it hardy?

Partly. In mild Mediterranean gardens it survives unprotected, but its foliage scorches from −4 °C (24.8 °F) and may be lost at around −7 to −9 °C (19.4 to 15.8 °F); the plant then regrows from the stem. In colder areas it only gets through the winter with protection that keeps it dry. Mississippi State University's extension service places it in USDA zone 9a.

Does it grow in full sun?

Yes, once mature and acclimatised, but it prefers light shade, its natural habitat being the forest understorey. A young plant moved suddenly into the sun will scorch.

Is it poisonous?

Yes, like all cycads, and the seed kernel most of all. It has been implicated in the poisoning of sheep and cattle in Australia. Contact a poison centre in case of ingestion.

Is it endangered?

No. The IUCN classes it as Least Concern and it remains very abundant, even though stands have been cleared for building and a scale insect causes local dieback. Its international trade falls under CITES Appendix II.
