Cycas revoluta, the sago palm or king sago, is a stout-trunked cycad from southern Japan, mainly the Ryukyu Islands, and possibly the coast of Fujian in China, where it grows on rocks and cliffs, often on limestone. It is the most widely grown species of the genus Cycas and the cycad most often seen in gardens and homes, from the Mediterranean to the Gulf Coast of the United States and the gardens of Australia. Despite its English name it is not a palm but a gymnosperm. It tolerates moderate frost, especially when dry, but suffers in prolonged cold and wet, and every part of the plant is poisonous. This site treats it as separate from Cycas taitungensis of Taiwan, which POWO includes in Cycas revoluta (see Taxonomy).

Key facts about Cycas revoluta

  • Accepted name: Cycas revoluta Thunb. (1782), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and by the World Checklist of Vascular Plants, the basis of POWO (consulted through GBIF)
  • Former names and synonyms: Cycas miquelii Warb. (1900) and Epicycas miquelii (Warb.) de Laub. (1998); garden varieties brevifrons, planifolia, prolifera and robusta, of no taxonomic value; the World Checklist (POWO) also includes Cycas taitungensis, which The World List of Cycads and this site treat as a separate species
  • Family: Cycadaceae, order Cycadales; section Asiorientales, of which it is the type species
  • Common names: sago palm, king sago, sago cycad, Japanese sago palm, fern palm; sotetsu in Japanese
  • Natural range: southern Japan (Ryukyu Islands, Tanegashima; Kyushu according to the Flora of China); China (Fujian), where the survival of wild populations is uncertain
  • IUCN status: Least Concern (LC), assessment by J. D. Bösenberg published in 2023; Vulnerable, if not Endangered, in China according to the Flora of China
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (seeds exempt under annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: leaves damaged from about −3 to −6 °C (26.6 to 21.2 °F); established trunks have regrown after −10 to −12 °C (14 to 10.4 °F) when the ground barely froze; plants in the ground killed at around −14 °C (6.8 °F) and below; RHS rating H3 (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, rocks, cliffs and stony slopes; in cultivation, full sun or bright filtered light, very free-draining soil or compost, dry in winter
  • Habit and adult size: trunk 1 to 3 m, rarely 7 to 8 m, often branching or suckering; leaves 50 to 150 cm, sometimes longer
  • In the UK, US and Australia: RHS Award of Garden Merit; grown outdoors at Ventnor Botanic Garden (Isle of Wight), where it has coned; 63 accessions at Montgomery Botanical Center (Florida); outdoor beds at the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria (Melbourne)
  • Toxicity: all parts, especially the seeds; dangerous to children, dogs, cats and horses (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Cycas revoluta

Cycas revoluta is recognised by its stiff, dark green, glossy leaves, clearly keeled (V-shaped in cross-section), carrying crowded, narrow, rigid leaflets whose edges are strongly rolled under: this “revolute” margin is what gave the species its name (Hill 2008).

The trunk is usually 1 to 3 m tall, exceptionally 7 m according to Hill (2008) and 8 m according to the Flora of China, and about 20 cm across at its narrowest point; at the base, and sometimes higher up, it often forms offsets, so that old plants have several trunks. The crown holds many leaves, up to 110 to 160 according to Hill. The leaves are 50 to 150 cm long (up to 1.8 m according to the Flora of China) and carry 100 to 240 leaflets; young leaves are covered in an orange down that later falls off. The short petiole (6 to 10 cm according to Hill, 10 to 20 cm according to the Flora of China) is spiny for almost its whole length. The median leaflets are 8 to 18 cm long and 3 to 6 mm wide, with a sharp tip.

Like all cycads, the species has separate male and female plants. A male plant bears a spindle-shaped yellow cone 30 to 60 cm long. A female does not form a true cone: at the centre of the crown it produces a rosette of megasporophylls, modified leaves 12 to 22 cm long covered in yellow to pale brown felt, whose upper part is deeply cut like a comb and which each carry 2 to 6 downy ovules. The seeds are orange to red and egg-shaped; their size varies between authors, from about 2.5 cm (Hill 2008) to 3.5 to 5 cm (de Laubenfels and Adema 1998; Flora of China). In China, pollination takes place from May to July and the seeds ripen in September and October (Flora of China).

Like other cycads, Cycas revoluta forms coralloid roots near the soil surface, which house symbiotic cyanobacteria able to fix nitrogen from the air (Jardin botanique Val Rahmeh and Giardini Botanici Hanbury).

Hybrids

No natural hybrid is documented. In cultivation, several hybrids with other Asian cycads circulate among collectors. BGCI PlantSearch lists Cycas revoluta × Cycas taitungensis in the collections of RHS Garden Wisley. In North Carolina, the Juniper Level Botanic Garden grows Cycas revoluta × Cycas guizhouensis and Cycas panzhihuaensis × (Cycas revoluta × Cycas taitungensis), which survived its winters in the ground without protection (2014). On the French forum Fous de palmiers, growers mention between 2011 and 2020 hybrids with Cycas taitungensis, Cycas debaoensis and Cycas bifida, and a complex hybrid (Cycas revoluta × Cycas micholitzii) × Cycas tropophylla. These plants are often described as faster-growing; no published measurement allows their cold tolerance to be compared with that of Cycas revoluta.

Possible confusion

  • Cycas taitungensis, from Taiwan, is the main source of confusion. It has longer (100 to 180 cm) and flatter leaves, longer and wider leaflets (6 to 8 mm) whose edges are only recurved, a longer petiole (15 to 20 cm), tighter, cabbage-like female “cones” and darker seeds (Hill 2008). De Laubenfels and Adema (1998) add the ribbed surface of the seed shell. An article on this site about the Cycas taitungensis of the Jardin zoologique tropical at La Londe-les-Maures, in the Var (south-east France), describes the same difference in the garden: longer leaves and a slightly more massive habit.
  • Cycas panzhihuaensis, from Sichuan and Yunnan, has softer, often bluish leaves with flat leaflets, and hairless ovules (Hill 2008).
  • The tropical cycads with broad, flat leaflets sold as “Cycas circinalis” or “Cycas rumphii” (queen sago) have no rolled leaflet margins and are suited only to frost-free climates (USDA zones 10 and 11 according to the University of Florida).

Taxonomy

The Swedish botanist and physician Carl Peter Thunberg described Cycas revoluta in 1782, referring in particular to cultivated plants, probably gathered in Japan in 1775–1776; it was the second Cycas species to be recognised, after Cycas circinalis. As no type had been designated, Ken Hill chose in 2008 as lectotype a sheet in Thunberg’s herbarium at Uppsala (UPS 23734), the only one annotated “e Japonia” (Hill 2008).

Cycas revoluta is the type species of section Asiorientales (Schuster 1932), defined by firm, waxy male cones, comb-like megasporophylls, downy ovules and red seeds with non-fibrous flesh. This relict section contains only two very close species, Cycas revoluta and Cycas taitungensis (Hill 2008). De Laubenfels and Adema (1998) renamed it subgenus Revoluta.

Cycas miquelii, described in 1900 by Warburg from a spineless plant grown in Amsterdam and said to come from southern China, is merely an aberrant form of Cycas revoluta (Hill 2008), and The World List of Cycads lists it as a synonym of this species. The name has nevertheless been applied to other cycads: the Flora of China (Chen and Stevenson 1999) uses it for Cycas sexseminifera, a dwarf cycad of the limestone karst of Guangxi and northern Vietnam, and the neotype chosen for it by de Laubenfels and Adema (1998) is a specimen of Cycas clivicola from southern Thailand, a choice that Hill (2008) rejected. Plants and texts labelled “Cycas miquelii” therefore do not necessarily refer to Cycas revoluta. The varieties brevifrons and planifolia (Miquel), prolifera (Siebold and Zuccarini) and robusta (Messeri, 1927, from plants grown in Florence) are garden variants with no taxonomic value.

The status of Cycas taitungensis divides the references. Described in 1994, it is accepted by the Flora of China, Hill (2008), de Laubenfels and Adema (1998), The World List of Cycads and Species+. In 2022 Chang and colleagues (Frontiers in Plant Science), combining morphology and genomic data (RAD-seq), found continuing gene flow between the two lineages, by pollen and by offsets carried by the Kuroshio current, and overlapping characters; they concluded that there is a single species and reduced Cycas taitungensis to a synonym of Cycas revoluta. The World Checklist of Vascular Plants, on which POWO is based, has followed them, which is why POWO extends the range of Cycas revoluta to Taiwan. This site follows The World List of Cycads and keeps a page for each of the two species: an editorial choice that departs from POWO.

Forms and cultivars

Variegated forms exist but are rare (University of Wisconsin). In Japan, many garden forms have long been selected. The name “Cycas aurea”, sometimes given as a synonym, does not appear in any of the synonym lists consulted.

Etymology and history

The epithet comes from the Latin revolutus, “rolled back”, referring to the leaflet margins (Hill 2008; Haynes 2022). The genus name Cycas comes from a Greek word used by Theophrastus for a kind of palm (Jardin botanique Val Rahmeh).

Before Thunberg, the plant had already been recorded by Kaempfer (1712) and by Rumphius (1741) under the name “Tessio” (Hill 2008). The species was already grown in Europe at the end of the eighteenth century: in Italy, the Palermo botanic garden presents its first plant, a gift from Queen Maria Carolina, as the first Cycas revoluta grown outdoors in Europe; the planting date differs between the garden’s own sources (1793 or 1799).

Common names

In English the species is the sago palm (Species+, ASPCA), king sago (Hill 2008), sago cycad and Japanese sago palm (Mississippi State University Extension; University of Florida) or fern palm (Species+). “Sago palm” is ambiguous: the ASPCA also applies it to Zamia, and most commercial sago starch comes from a true palm, Metroxylon sagu (University of Florida). In Japanese the species is called sotetsu; Hill (2008) lists dozens of regional names in the Ryukyu Islands. In Chinese it is su tie or tie shu, “iron tree” (Flora of China; Hill 2008). In French it is the cycas du Japon or sagou du Japon (Jardin botanique Val Rahmeh).

In the wild

Cycas revoluta is widespread in the Ryukyu Islands of southern Japan, mostly on steep to precipitous stony slopes, sometimes on coastal cliffs; it once also grew on flatter ground, since cleared (Hill 2008). De Laubenfels and Adema (1998) describe it from rocky places, often on limestone. Hill also cites collections from the island of Tanegashima, south of Kyushu, and the Flora of China includes Kyushu in its range.

In China, the Flora of China records thickets on island slopes and open forest on the mainland, between 100 and 500 m, in Fujian (Lianjiang and Ningde counties and some islands). It adds that this once wide distribution has been greatly reduced by commercial collecting and habitat destruction, and that it is unknown whether any wild populations remain; for Hill (2008), the evidence for a natural presence in Fujian is indirect but strong, without recent confirmation. Species+ also gives its presence in China as uncertain.

Conservation status and regulation

The IUCN lists Cycas revoluta as Least Concern (LC), in the assessment by J. D. Bösenberg published in 2023 (category checked on GBIF). The previous assessment was by Ken Hill (2010); already in 2008, Hill considered the species not threatened, whereas Donaldson (2003) had listed it as Near Threatened. Hill (2008) notes that several natural stands are protected and that the species is grown in huge numbers from propagated plants. In China, on the other hand, the Flora of China regards it as Vulnerable, if not Endangered.

Like the whole Cycadaceae family, the species has been listed in CITES Appendix II since 4 February 1977. The listing carries annotation #4 (CoP20 version, in force since 5 March 2026): seeds, pollen, seedlings and tissue cultures obtained in vitro and transported in sterile containers, and cut flowers of artificially propagated plants, are not covered by CITES. In the European Union it falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, whose annexes are currently set by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, in force since 29 June 2026. Species+ confirms this status and gives Japan and, with doubt, China as range countries (consulted on 19 October 2026).

Plants sold in garden centres are raised in cultivation. This general information does not replace the official texts: check the regulations in force before any purchase or exchange across borders.

Growing Cycas revoluta

Cycas revoluta is easy to grow if it gets plenty of light, a compost or soil that drains quickly, water during the warm season and dry conditions in cold weather. Growth is slow and comes in flushes: a ring of new leaves every year or every other year, the plant meanwhile thickening its trunk and roots (Cycadales.eu). The University of Wisconsin notes that, indoors, cycads may only produce leaves every two years. It has the Royal Horticultural Society’s Award of Garden Merit.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

Where frost is light, the RHS advises a moist but well-drained soil in full light, with shelter from the hottest sun. In Australia, the Palm and Cycad Societies of Australia (PACSOA) describe it as the most popular and widely grown cycad, an excellent landscape plant that tolerates dry periods and light frosts and prefers a sunny, well-drained spot with deep soil; plant it away from paths, as the leaves are spiny. In the United States, a Mississippi State University extension publication calls it the common species planted along the Gulf Coast, and the University of Florida notes that it grows well in the subtropical parts of the country, particularly Florida, California, Georgia and Puerto Rico, and that once established outdoors it is drought-resistant but not freeze-tolerant.

On the Mediterranean coast of Europe, large specimens grow outdoors, for example at the Val Rahmeh botanic garden in Menton and the Villa Thuret in Antibes (information sheet of the Hanbury and Val Rahmeh gardens), or in the town centre of Menton; in Italy, the botanic gardens of Palermo, Naples and Florence grow it in the open ground. Further north and west, winter defoliation is common (see Cold hardiness). The University of Arkansas stresses that drainage is critical: in central Arkansas, plants in the ground often freeze back to the ground and grow again in spring, and some were lost after a wet, colder than normal winter.

Container cultivation

Cycas revoluta does well in containers and, according to the University of Wisconsin, thrives indoors if it gets plenty of light. For plants under glass, the RHS recommends a peat-free, loam-based compost with added leaf mould and grit, full light with shade from hot sun, moderate watering in growth and sparing watering the rest of the time. The University of Wisconsin suggests a cactus mix, or a potting compost lightened with 30 to 40% coarse perlite, horticultural pumice or baked clay granules. Overwatering is one of the most common causes of death in cultivated cycads (University of Wisconsin).

Repotting

Cycads do not mind being pot-bound: they do not need frequent repotting and can be kept in a slightly small pot. Repot in spring or summer (University of Wisconsin). A smaller pot also limits the volume of compost that stays wet around the roots; the fresh compost must be very free-draining.

Watering and feeding

Contrary to a widespread idea, cycads like water in summer, provided the soil drains quickly (Cycadales.eu). Watering matters most while a new flush of leaves is forming, until the leaves harden a few weeks later; otherwise the young leaves may scorch or harden while still curled (University of Wisconsin). In winter, water very little, especially if the plant is kept cool. Despite their slow growth, cycads need feeding: a slow-release fertiliser or a liquid organic feed during the growing season is enough (Cycadales.eu), but too much fertiliser can kill the plant (University of Wisconsin).

Light and acclimatisation

A plant raised in shade or under glass can scorch if it is moved suddenly into full sun: the leaflets turn white, then brown. Damaged leaves do not turn green again, but the next ones will be adapted to the sun (Cycadales.eu). Acclimatise plants bought from a garden centre gradually. On the Fous de palmiers forum, a grower reported in April 2014 scorch on a Cycas revoluta moved into the sun after only a fortnight in partial shade. Conversely, a grower in Roussillon (southern France) noted in January 2011 that, in the hottest corner of his south-facing patio, his plants turned yellow from late May.

Pruning

Remove leaves that have turned yellow or brown, cutting them at the base, close to the trunk, without wounding it (University of Wisconsin). After frost, wait until spring before cutting anything, then remove only the dead leaves (Cycadales.eu); the University of Arkansas advises waiting until late February or early March. Wear gloves and long sleeves: the leaflets end in a hard point, the petiole is spiny and the plant is poisonous. Pick up all the debris.

Winter care and protection

In the ground, protection aims first of all to keep the crown and the soil dry. Growers on the Fous de palmiers forum use horticultural fleece, sometimes doubled, a cloche or a cover, and a mulch around the base; the University of Arkansas advises adding several extra inches of mulch over winter. These measures limit damage without preventing it: at Marennes, on the French Atlantic coast, plants in the ground since 2005 lost their leaves in the winters of 2008–2009, 2009–2010 and 2011–2012 despite fleece and a tunnel (Fous de palmiers forum, 2015).

Container plants can spend the winter in a cool, bright, frost-free place, with sparing watering outside the growing season (RHS). The RHS H3 rating corresponds to minimum temperatures of about −5 to 1 °C (23 to 34 °F).

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

Cycas revoluta is by far the most widely represented cycad in the collections of the three countries.

  • United Kingdom: at Ventnor Botanic Garden, on the Isle of Wight, whose microclimate the garden describes as about 5 °C (9 °F) warmer than the rest of the country, it grows outdoors; a male plant produced a cone in 2012 or 2013 according to the garden’s sources, and in the summer of 2019 a larger male cone and a female cone appeared together, presented by the garden as the first outdoors in the UK. At the Chelsea Physic Garden (London), a plant received in 2018 grows in a glasshouse bed; the garden rates it “Half hardy – unheated greenhouse / mild winter: 1 to −5 °C”. Kew, Edinburgh, Oxford, Wisley, the Eden Project, Glasgow and more than ten other gardens declare it to BGCI PlantSearch.
  • United States: the online catalogue of Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida) lists 63 accessions, most received between 2024 and 2026; Lotusland (California) names it among the cycads of its Cycad Garden; the Huntington, the San Diego Botanic Garden, the San Diego Zoo, the Missouri Botanical Garden, Naples Botanical Garden, the UC Berkeley Botanical Garden and more than sixty other gardens declare it to BGCI PlantSearch.
  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria grow it in outdoor beds at Melbourne Gardens (living collection census); it is also in the online catalogue of the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney, and Brisbane, Cairns, Adelaide and James Cook University, among others, declare it to BGCI PlantSearch.

A BGCI entry shows that a garden has listed the species at some point, not that a plant is alive today.

Propagation

Cycas revoluta is propagated from seed or by separating offsets (RHS). A male and a female plant are needed to obtain fertile seed. Cycad seeds have no dormancy: sow them fresh, bearing in mind that germination is often slow and uneven (University of Wisconsin). Wear gloves to remove the flesh of the seeds, which is poisonous (see Toxicity and precautions). Seeds are exempt from CITES.

Offsets, or pups, grow at the base of the trunk and sometimes higher up. Remove them when they reach about 5 cm (2 in) across, take off most of their leaves and set them half-buried in the same compost as for adults, watering sparingly until roots form, which takes a few months (University of Wisconsin). PACSOA points out the advantage of offsets: you know the sex of the plant, whereas a seedling takes years to show it. Cycas revoluta is also used as a rootstock: the French nurseryman Simon Lavaud (Cycadales.eu) grafts onto it Australian Cycas that often decline on their own roots in France.

Pests and diseases

Outdoors in Europe, Cycas revoluta has few enemies: the RHS describes it as generally free of pests and diseases outdoors but prone to red spider mite under glass. The real problems mostly come from overwatering, nutrient deficiencies and scale insects.

Scale insects

Scale insects are fairly common on cycads (Cycadales.eu). The most feared is the Asian cycad scale, Aulacaspis yasumatsui: described from Thailand, it invaded Florida from 1996, where Cycas revoluta, the most planted cycad, was heavily infested (University of Florida). In Europe, the EPPO database (update of 16 October 2026) lists it as present, with few occurrences, in several countries including the United Kingdom; in France it has only been intercepted. Against scale, Cycadales.eu recommends specific insecticides or, as a biological alternative, soft soap; check the undersides of the leaflets and the leaf bases.

The butterfly Chilades pandava

The caterpillars of the butterfly Chilades pandava eat the young leaves of cycads in Asia and on Réunion. It has not been reported as established in mainland France, Italy, Spain, Greece or Cyprus.

Rots

Rot of the trunk, at the base or at the top, almost always comes from a compost that holds water (Cycadales.eu). In Sicily, a decline of potted Cycas revoluta (stunted growth, yellowing and drying of the crown, root rot, browning of the base of the trunk) has been attributed to Phytophthora pseudocryptogea, with two less virulent species (Aloi and colleagues 2023; first report worldwide on this species).

Yellowing leaves and deficiencies

A yellowing leaf is not necessarily a sign of disease: leaves live for a few years, then turn yellow and die. Other causes are lack of fertiliser (magnesium deficiency in Cycas revoluta: the leaflet tips turn yellow, then brown; manganese deficiency: yellowing of the leaf edges, then necrosis), lack of water in summer, a soil or water pH of 8 or more, and nearby concrete, which makes fertilisers less available (Cycadales.eu). Yellow spots on the leaflets point to scale insects or hail damage.

Cold hardiness

Cycas revoluta is one of the more cold-tolerant cycads, but its leaves suffer long before the trunk. The Val Rahmeh botanic garden puts the first leaf damage at around −3 °C (26.6 °F) and the death of the plant from −10 °C (14 °F). The University of Wisconsin gives survival to about −9 °C (15 °F), with frost damage to the leaves at around −8 °C (the high teens °F); the University of Arkansas also gives about −9 °C (15 °F), with leaves nipped at about −5 to −6 °C (the low 20s °F) or by ice, and adds that a frozen plant should regrow in spring as long as the crown is firm and undamaged. Growing reports show that the trunk of an established plant can regrow after harder frosts if the ground has barely frozen, and that the length of the cold spell and the wet matter as much as the minimum.

Mediterranean and Atlantic Europe

These results come mostly from France, whose Mediterranean south has a climate comparable to parts of California and southern Australia.

  • In Roussillon and Provence, a grower reports on the Fous de palmiers forum (February 2012) that trunks of Cycas revoluta regrew after −10 to −12 °C (14 to 10.4 °F) when the ground barely froze, as in 1956 and 1985, but that in the colder parts of the Hérault, where −15 °C (5 °F) can be reached, even large plants can be lost, as at the Jardin des plantes in Montpellier in the winter of 1985–1986; he adds that the foliage suffers from −6 °C (21.2 °F).
  • Near Carpentras (Vaucluse), a plant in the ground since 1986 went through −12 °C (10.4 °F) in February 2012 and its head split in two the following spring; its owner added in 2016 that it freezes at around −8 to −10 °C (17.6 to 14 °F) but makes new leaves every spring. In Montpellier, the winter of 2012–2013 (−8 to −9 °C, 17.6 to 15.8 °F) stripped almost all of a gardener’s cycads, Cycas revoluta included.
  • On the Atlantic coast, a garden plant at Talence, near Bordeaux, came through the cold wave of February 2012 unharmed (−8.8 °C, 16.2 °F, at Mérignac); near Nantes, a grower recorded −9.5 °C (14.9 °F) in February 2012, with several days below freezing, wind and snow: under fleece, his plant lost half its leaves, the one planted near a wall only a fifth.
  • Inland, failures are more common: near Toulouse, a plant in the ground was lost at −14 °C (6.8 °F) in February 2012 despite fleece (Au jardin forum), and a large plant set out in 1980 died in January 1985 after a week of −6 to −8 °C (21.2 to 17.6 °F) by day and −15 °C (5 °F) at night, with wind, snow and a minimum of −18 °C (−0.4 °F) (Fous de palmiers forum).
  • In southern Switzerland, plants set out in the mid-1980s at Locarno came through February 2012 almost unharmed (about −6.5 °C, 20.3 °F), whereas at Lugano (−8.5 °C, 16.7 °F, two or three days below freezing) damage varied greatly from plant to plant (Fous de palmiers forum, 2012).
  • In Italy, a grower near Ravenna reports on the Tropicamente forum that the winter of 2009–2010 (−7 to −10 °C, 19.4 to 14 °F, with snow) defoliated and then killed his container plants, while plants near the sea suffered little; in Milan, two large plants turned completely yellow after −9.7 °C (14.5 °F) in February 2012.

The Jardin zoologique tropical at La Londe-les-Maures, on the Mediterranean coast of the Var, has published no observations on Cycas revoluta; its experience with the closely related Cycas taitungensis is described on that species’ page.

United Kingdom

The RHS rates Cycas revoluta H3: hardy in coastal and relatively mild parts of the UK, with minimum temperatures of about −5 to 1 °C (23 to 34 °F), and advises growing it under glass, or outdoors only in frost-free areas. Outdoor success is documented at Ventnor, on the Isle of Wight, where the plants have coned (see In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia). Elsewhere, a container plant brought into a cool, frost-free conservatory or glasshouse for the winter is the safe option, and wet winter weather is as much of a risk as the cold itself.

United States

The University of Wisconsin places the species in zone 8, and a Mississippi State University extension publication in zone 8b, “reportedly hardy to 19 °F”, about −7 °C. At the Juniper Level Botanic Garden in North Carolina, Cycas revoluta appears in the list of cycads that did not survive outdoors, after winters with minima of 4 to 9 °F (−15.6 to −12.8 °C) depending on the site (list published in August 2014). In Florida, the University of Florida counts the cycad scale among the most damaging agents for this species.

Australia

PACSOA notes that the species tolerates light frosts; it grows in outdoor beds at the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria in Melbourne. No measured frost data from Australia are documented.

Compared with Cycas panzhihuaensis and Cycas taitungensis

At the Juniper Level Botanic Garden, Cycas panzhihuaensis and Cycas taitungensis survived the same winters in the ground without protection, whereas Cycas revoluta did not; the garden adds that Cycas revoluta holds onto its foliage to a lower temperature than Cycas taitungensis, but that the latter is ultimately the hardier plant. For measured data, see the pages of those species.

Toxicity and precautions

Cycas revoluta is poisonous to people and animals, above all through its seeds. It contains cycasin, which is converted in the body into methylazoxymethanol, toxic to the liver and nervous system, along with a neurotoxic amino acid, BMAA (beta-N-methylamino-L-alanine) (Jardin botanique Val Rahmeh; NC State Extension). The ASPCA lists it among plants toxic to dogs, cats and horses: vomiting, black stools, jaundice, clotting disorders, liver damage and failure, sometimes death. In a study of 34 poisoned dogs in Louisiana between 2003 and 2010, half died or were euthanised (Ferguson and colleagues 2011). The RHS also describes it as a skin irritant.

Keep seeds and pruning debris out of reach of children and pets, and wear gloves to handle the seeds and to cut the leaves, whose leaflets end in a hard point and whose petiole is spiny. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet straight away.

Traditional uses

In Japan, the starchy pith of the trunk yielded a starch, “false sago”, and the seeds were eaten after cooking; the flour must be washed at length and then cooked to remove the toxins (Jardin botanique Val Rahmeh and Giardini Botanici Hanbury). The University of Florida also mentions its use as famine food in Japan. The leaves are used in flower arrangements and, in the French West Indies, sometimes replace palm fronds on Palm Sunday (same sources). These preparations must not be copied.

Frequently asked questions

Is Cycas revoluta a palm?

No. It is a gymnosperm of the family Cycadaceae, closer to conifers and ginkgo than to palms. Its large feather-like leaves and its English name, sago palm, keep the confusion alive.

How cold can a sago palm take?

It depends on the length of the cold and on the wet. The leaves are damaged at around −3 to −6 °C (26.6 to 21.2 °F); the trunk of an established plant can regrow after −10 to −12 °C (14 to 10.4 °F) if the ground has barely frozen. Plants in the ground have died at around −14 °C (6.8 °F) near Toulouse in 2012 and at lower temperatures in 1985.

Can I grow a sago palm outdoors in the UK?

Only in the mildest coastal areas, and with some risk. The RHS rates it H3 and recommends outdoor planting only in frost-free areas; it has coned outdoors at Ventnor, on the Isle of Wight. Elsewhere, grow it in a container and bring it into a cool, bright, frost-free place for the winter.

My sago palm has frozen: what should I do?

Wait until spring before cutting anything, then remove only the dead leaves. If the trunk is hard and firmly anchored, the plant is probably alive and will make new leaves; a soft trunk is rotting inside (Cycadales.eu; University of Arkansas).

Why are the leaves of my sago palm turning yellow?

There are several possible causes: normal ageing of the lowest leaves, lack of fertiliser (magnesium, manganese), lack of water in summer, very alkaline soil or water, or overwatering. Start by checking drainage and feeding.

Should I cut the yellow leaves off?

Yes, once they are fully yellow or brown: cut them close to the trunk without wounding it. After frost, wait until spring before cutting.

Is the sago palm toxic to dogs and cats?

Yes, seriously. The seeds are the most dangerous part; poisoning affects the liver and can be fatal. See a vet straight away if any part is swallowed.

How does it differ from Cycas taitungensis?

Cycas taitungensis has longer, flatter leaves, wider leaflets with only recurved margins and a more massive habit. POWO includes it in Cycas revoluta, following Chang and colleagues (2022), but The World List of Cycads and this site treat it as a separate species.
