Zamia imperialis is a cycad endemic to central Panama (the provinces of Coclé, Colón, Panamá and Veraguas), where it grows in the understorey of tropical rainforest, along streams. This species of the genus Zamia stands out for its above-ground stem, its huge pleated leaflets, up to 75 cm long and 21 cm wide, and above all for its new leaves, which emerge a dark red, from burgundy to reddish brown, before turning green. It was described only in 2008, by Alberto S. Taylor, Jody L. Haynes and Greg Holzman; until then it had been confused with Zamia skinneri and informally called the “red-leafed skinneri”. The IUCN lists it as Endangered, and its authors knew of fewer than 200 plants in the wild.

Key facts about Zamia imperialis

  • Accepted name: Zamia imperialis A.S.Taylor, J.L.Haynes & Holzman (2008), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and by GBIF; POWO could not be consulted directly
  • Former names and synonyms: no synonym; before 2008 these plants were included in Zamia skinneri (the “red-leafed” or “red-emergent” form)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: none in English apart from the informal trade name “red-leafed skinneri”; “cebolla” (Spanish for “onion”), a name given collectively to these plants in some Panamanian localities (original description)
  • Natural range: Panama (Coclé, Colón, Panamá, Veraguas); two populations studied, one from sea level to about 200 m, the other at about 600 m or more
  • IUCN status: Endangered (EN), criteria B1ab(iii,v); C2a(i), assessment published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (the whole genus; seeds exempt under annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: no data (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, shady rainforest understorey near streams, on stream gravels covered with rich humus; hot, humid climate all year round, without a dry season
  • Habit and adult size: above-ground stem 1 m tall or more and up to 22 cm in diameter, sometimes branched; leaves 52 to 257 cm long, with 2 to 9 pairs of leaflets
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Montgomery Botanical Center (Florida), 14 accessions from 2000 to 2018; Fairchild, Huntington and three other American gardens declare it to BGCI; no British or Australian record
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; treat it as poisonous, like all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Zamia imperialis

Three characters together point to it: a clearly visible above-ground stem, huge pleated leaflets and dark red new leaves. Its origin, central Panama, completes the identification. The figures below come from the original description (Taylor and colleagues, 2008).

Stem

The above-ground stem commonly reaches 1 m in height or more and up to 22 cm in diameter on old plants. It may branch at the base, at the top, or both. Like all cycads, the plant forms coralloid roots that house nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria.

Leaves and leaflets

Each crown carries on average three leaves, exceptionally up to twelve. The leaves are 52 to 257 cm long and bear 2 to 9 pairs of leaflets, most often 4 or 5. The petiole, a very dark green that is almost black, is 37 to 164 cm long and on average 1.87 times as long as the rachis, a much higher ratio than in Zamia skinneri. Both petiole and rachis carry prickles.

The leaflets, elliptic to acuminate, have a finely toothed margin and are pleated between the veins, which gives the blade a corrugated look. Median leaflets measure 27 to 75 cm by 6.5 to 21 cm; apical leaflets 19 to 67 cm by 6.3 to 17 cm, basal ones 25 to 66 cm by 5.5 to 18.5 cm. Young leaves emerge dark red, burgundy or reddish brown, lightly covered with a tawny down, whereas those of Zamia skinneri in the strict sense come out bright green and hairless. The first leaves of seedlings usually bear four long-pointed leaflets. The colour of the new leaves explains the old informal name “red-leafed skinneri”.

Cones and seeds

The species is dioecious. Male cones are 8.5 to 19 cm long and 2 to 3 cm wide; conical-cylindrical to elongate-cylindrical, yellowish to brownish yellow, they stand on a stalk 4.8 to 8 cm long. Female cones, usually one per plant, are 21 to 31 cm long and 7 to 11 cm wide; cylindrical to cylindrical-globose, on a stalk 6 to 9 cm long, they are covered with a reddish down when young, then turn brown. At maturity their scales are brown, not green as in Zamia skinneri. The seeds, with a bright red fleshy coat, measure 1.9 to 3 cm by 1.1 to 1.9 cm, and a ripe cone can hold 370 or more.

Hybrids

No hybrid has been documented. Taylor and his colleagues (2008) suggested that the pleated-leaved cycads of Panama form a species complex shaped by repeated episodes of convergence and hybridisation, linked to Pleistocene changes in sea level and to the fragmentation of the forests; this is a biogeographical hypothesis, not a record of hybrid plants. According to The World List of Cycads, the provinces of Colón and Panamá also hold Zamia obliqua, but no hybrid between the two species has been reported.

Possible confusion

The main risk of confusion concerns the other members of the Zamia skinneri complex, all from Panama or the Central American isthmus:

  • Zamia skinneri in the strict sense, from the mainland coast of Bocas del Toro (north-western Panama): new leaves bright green and hairless, smaller leaflets, more numerous leaves and leaflets, a petiole much shorter in relation to the rachis, smaller female cones with green scales, smaller seeds.
  • Zamia neurophyllidia, from Nicaragua to western Panama: young leaves bright green according to its 2008 recharacterisation, with different venation and range.
  • Zamia hamannii (Bocas del Toro), described in the same 2008 paper: shorter leaves and less broadly pleated leaflets.
  • Zamia nesophila (islands of Bocas del Toro): island habitat and a different colour of the new leaves.
  • Zamia pseudoparasitica, the only epiphytic cycad, which grows as an adult on tree branches: confusion is possible only with young plants on the ground.

In the trade, Zamia imperialis still sometimes circulates under the old informal names “Zamia skinneri red form” or “red-leafed skinneri”.

It is often mentioned, with Zamia wallisii from Colombia, among the Zamia with the largest leaflets: those of Zamia imperialis are the longest, those of Zamia wallisii the broadest. The two species live far apart and are not confused.

Taxonomy

Zamia imperialis A.S.Taylor, J.L.Haynes & Holzman is accepted by The World List of Cycads and by GBIF; POWO, the site’s nomenclatural authority, could not be consulted directly. The species was published in November 2008 by Alberto S. Taylor B., Jody L. Haynes and Greg Holzman in the Botanical Journal of the Linnean Society (volume 158, issue 3, pages 399 to 429, figures 7c, 8c and 11), in a paper entitled “Taxonomical, nomenclatural and biogeographical revelations in the Zamia skinneri complex of Central America”. The holotype (Taylor and Quirós ASTB01-LRica 1) was collected on 18 November 2004 in Coclé province, Panama, in the understorey of an open upland forest, on a loamy, humus-rich soil among very muddy, mostly shrubby secondary vegetation. It is kept in the herbarium of the University of Panama (PMA), with isotypes at the Missouri Botanical Garden (MO), the New York Botanical Garden (NY), the Smithsonian Institution (US) and Xalapa (XAL).

The break-up of the Zamia skinneri complex

Before 2008, the name Zamia skinneri, described in 1851 from material collected by Józef Warszewicz in Panama, covered all the pleated-leaved cycads with stems from Panama and north-western Central America. Growers had long distinguished a form with green new leaves and a form with red new leaves from central Panama. Taylor, Haynes and Holzman (2008):

  • restricted Zamia skinneri in the strict sense to the green-flushing populations of the mainland coast of Bocas del Toro;
  • recharacterised Zamia neurophyllidia, published in 1993, from the populations near its type locality, close to Changuinola (Bocas del Toro), noting that its true range was still to be established;
  • described three new species: Zamia hamannii, Zamia imperialis (the red-flushing populations of central Panama) and Zamia nesophila.

For the reader, the consequence is simple: the plants of central Panama with red new leaves, once sold as Zamia skinneri, are now called Zamia imperialis. Its exact position in the molecular phylogenies of the genus has not been checked for this page.

Etymology and history

The epithet imperialis, “imperial”, alludes to the majestic habit of the plant and the regal look of its huge pleated leaflets, red when they emerge (Haynes, 2022, as quoted by The World List of Cycads). The history of the species is that of the break-up of the Zamia skinneri complex (see Taxonomy); no account of its introduction into cultivation has been found.

Common names

There is no English name in common use; the English-speaking trade still uses the informal “red-leafed skinneri”. In Spanish, in Panama, these plants are called “cebolla” (“onion”) in some localities, according to the original description, which does not explain the name; it is not peculiar to this species.

In the wild

The World List of Cycads places Zamia imperialis in the Panamanian provinces of Coclé, Colón, Panamá and Veraguas. The area it actually occupies is much smaller than these provinces suggest: the authors studied two populations, one of a little under 100 plants, the other of a little over 100, widely scattered and hard to find. One grows at about 600 m or more, the other from sea level to about 200 m.

The plants grow near streams, often in the catchments of their tributaries and sometimes at their source, in dark forests rich in ferns and palms, on loose stream gravels covered with rich humus; the climate is hot and humid all year round, with regular rain and no dry season. The seeds, often carried away by the current, give seedlings far from the adult plants. Many plants grow in deep shade, where they rarely produce cones: in nine years of study the authors saw only six female cones, two in one population and four in the other, and they note the same scarcity in plants grown in shade. Male cones develop from late July to early November, pollen being shed from late October to November, and the seeds ripen about a year after pollination. In 2008 no pollination had yet been observed directly (Taylor and colleagues, 2008).

Conservation status and regulation

Zamia imperialis is listed as Endangered (EN) on the IUCN Red List, under criteria B1ab(iii,v); C2a(i), in the assessment by G. Holzman, A. Taylor-Blake and J. Haynes published in 2022 and cited by The World List of Cycads; GBIF gives the same category (the Red List itself could not be consulted). These criteria reflect a small range with a decline in habitat quality and in the number of mature individuals, and a small, fragmented population. In 2008 the authors recalled that the 2006 Red List classed these plants, then under the name Zamia skinneri, as Endangered (EN A2acd; C1), and proposed raising them to Critically Endangered (CR B2abc) in view of the destruction they had observed.

The threats described are of several kinds:

  • Habitat loss: deforestation in central Panama. The smaller of the two populations studied, although it lies within a national park, remains exposed to poaching and destruction.
  • Machete cutting: according to the original description, local people, both indigenous inhabitants and settlers, who walk up the headwater streams where villages draw their water, cut down the prickly plants that get in their way. Many adults bear scars and have resprouted, and many small plants are in fact the regrown heads of large plants cut years earlier. In several villages, people said there used to be many more plants.
  • Poor reproduction: cones are rare in deep shade and the populations are scattered.

Like all Zamia except Zamia restrepoi, Zamia imperialis falls under CITES Appendix II, where the genus has been listed since 4 February 1977. The listing carries annotation #4 (version adopted at CoP20, in force on 5 March 2026): seeds, pollen, seedlings and tissue cultures obtained in vitro and transported in sterile containers, and cut flowers of artificially propagated plants, are not covered by CITES. International trade in the plants themselves, on the other hand, requires an export permit. In the European Union the species falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, whose current annexes are set by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026.

This general information does not replace the official texts: check the regulations in force before buying or exchanging plants (databases consulted in October 2026), ask the seller for proof of origin and prefer plants raised from cultivated seed.

Growing Zamia imperialis

Zamia imperialis remains rare outside specialist collections, and no growing guide based on trials has been published. The advice below is inferred from the natural habitat described in the original description and should be read as such.

The French specialist nursery Cycadales.eu, whose author lives in Montpellier, ranks Zamia imperialis among the “difficult” lowland Zamia of Central and South America and describes it as rather temperamental in cultivation, happy only in a tropical glasshouse. It notes that at one locality of the species the temperature stays between 22 and 30 °C (71.6 and 86 °F) all year round, with air humidity of 85 to 95 % and about 4,000 mm of rain a year, and that Zamia imperialis copes very badly with swings in temperature, unlike the closely related Zamia neurophyllidia. For this group the nursery recommends a free-draining, neutral to acid compost and water, about 22 °C (71.6 °F) at night, with brief drops to around 15 °C (59 °F) tolerated, 28 to 30 °C (82.4 to 86 °F) by day and 70 to 90 % air humidity (Cycadales.eu, 2023). These are a grower’s recommendations, not measurements made on the species.

  • Light: in the wild the plant often grows in deep shade, but rarely cones there, in cultivation as in nature (Taylor and colleagues, 2008). Under glass, bright but filtered light, without direct sun in the hottest hours, therefore seems preferable.
  • Compost and watering: the natural habitat, humus-covered stream gravels by the water, suggests a compost rich in organic matter but very free-draining, kept moist, and water low in lime; the species comes from a climate without a dry season, so long dry spells should be avoided.
  • Temperature and humidity: it is a tropical species of the lowlands and lower mountains, warm and humid all year round. No minimum temperature has been measured.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

Planting out is conceivable only in a humid tropical climate, entirely free of frost, comparable to its native forests. No outdoor result has been published, in any country.

Container cultivation

Everywhere else, including the UK, it is a plant for a heated glasshouse or a warm, humid room, in a deep, wide container that leaves room for the stem and the roots; repotting is done in spring. A grower in France is mentioned on the Fous de palmiers forum, in October 2015, as having succeeded in growing Zamia imperialis indoors. A French grower who raises seedlings of these Panamanian Zamia advises shading them from the sun, otherwise the leaves turn yellow (May 2013), and reports that his young Zamia imperialis produced yellowish leaves during the winter of 2013–2014, which he puts down to poor growing conditions (February 2014). None of these messages gives a temperature.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In short: a small number of American collections; no British or Australian record.

  • United States: Montgomery Botanical Center, in Coral Gables (Florida), lists 14 accessions in its online catalogue, three from 2000, eight from 2001, one from 2012 and two from 2018 according to the garden’s numbering (consulted on 24 October 2026). Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden lists it in its online Garden Explorer (inventory of 19 October 2026). The Huntington (San Marino, California), Marie Selby Botanical Gardens (Sarasota, Florida), the National Tropical Botanical Garden (Kalāheo, Hawaii) and the botanical garden of the University of Hawaii at Hilo also declare it to BGCI.
  • United Kingdom and Australia: no record in BGCI or in the British and Australian catalogues checked in October 2026.

A BGCI declaration shows that a garden held the plant at some point, not that it is alive today. The sources consulted do not say whether these plants grow outdoors or under glass. No English-language growers’ report could be read: PalmTalk and Dave’s Garden were not accessible.

Propagation

The species is propagated from seed. Outside its range, male and female plants coning at the same time and hand pollination are needed. Sow the seeds fresh, after removing the flesh with gloves, in a free-draining compost kept warm and moist. On the Fous de palmiers forum, a grower who had sown several pleated-leaved Panamanian Zamia reports in January 2013 that all these species, Zamia imperialis included, germinate very quickly in his glasshouse. The first leaves of seedlings usually carry four long-pointed leaflets. Dividing branched plants is possible in theory, but risky.

Pests and diseases

In the wild, the authors observed neither pests nor diseases (Taylor and colleagues, 2008). No pest specific to the species is documented in cultivation; the risks are those of tropical glasshouse cycads in general: scale insects, including Aulacaspis yasumatsui, feared in cycad collections, mealybugs at the base of the leaves, and rots in an airless, waterlogged compost or stagnant air. Good air movement in the glasshouse limits fungal problems.

Cold hardiness

Zamia imperialis is not hardy: it is a humid tropical species, and no data on its resistance to cold exist, either in the wild or in cultivation.

Mediterranean and European experience

No outdoor cultivation has been reported in France, in the ground or in a pot, and no dated frost episode concerns it. On the Fous de palmiers forum, a grower in Provence (France) reports in May 2016 that he grows a young Zamia imperialis in the shade of a fig tree, without saying whether it is in a pot or whether it spends the winter outside, and without any wintering result. The Jardin zoologique tropical at La Londe-les-Maures, in the Var, has published no observation of this species. In Italy, the Tropicamente forum has no message about it.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No cold report was found in these countries, nor anywhere else. Outside the humid tropics, the species belongs under glass.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity study specific to Zamia imperialis has been found. The risk is, however, well documented in the genus and in other cycads: Zamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, harmful to the liver and the nervous system, and for Zamia furfuracea the ASPCA also cites BMAA, a neurotoxic amino acid, with vomiting, bloody diarrhoea and liver damage in dogs, cats and horses; the seeds are the most dangerous part. In the absence of evidence to the contrary, treat the plant as poisonous: keep seeds and leaves out of reach of children and animals, and wear gloves to clean seeds. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet straight away.

Traditional uses

The original description records one craft use: the mucilage that flows from cut stems is used to glue the parts of traditional Panamanian string instruments, such as the guitar and the mejoranera, a small guitar (Taylor and colleagues, 2008). No food or medicinal use specific to the species is documented.

Frequently asked questions

Why is Zamia imperialis considered so spectacular?

Because of three characters together: leaflets among the largest in the genus (up to 75 × 21 cm) and pleated, dark red new leaves and an above-ground stem a metre or more tall. The name imperialis, “imperial”, chosen in 2008, refers to this majestic look.

How does it differ from Zamia skinneri?

By its origin and the colour of its new leaves. Until 2008 both were united under Zamia skinneri; that name now applies to the green-flushing plants of Bocas del Toro, while the red-flushing plants of central Panama are Zamia imperialis, which also has larger leaflets and a much longer petiole.

How many plants are left in the wild?

Few. Its authors knew of two populations, one of a little under and the other of a little over 100 plants, fewer than 200 in all; only one of the two lies within a national park.

Why do local people cut the plants with machetes?

Because they are prickly and get in the way along the streams where water is drawn. Many cut plants resprout, but people in several villages say there used to be many more.

Is it endangered?

Yes. The IUCN lists it as Endangered (2022); in 2008 its authors even proposed the category Critically Endangered.

Can I grow it in the UK?

Yes, but only in a heated, humid glasshouse or a warm room indoors. It does not tolerate cold, and planting out is possible only in the humid tropics; buy plants raised from cultivated seed.

Is it poisonous?

It should be treated as such, like all cycads: the seeds are the most dangerous part for people and animals.
