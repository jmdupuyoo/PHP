The genus Zamia brings together 88 American cycads, ranging from Florida and the Caribbean to Mexico, Central America and northern South America, from Colombia to Bolivia and Brazilian Amazonia. It is the largest cycad genus in the New World and the most varied in form and habitat: it includes the smallest cycad in the world, Zamia pygmaea, the only known epiphytic gymnosperm, Zamia pseudoparasitica, species of swamps and flooded forest, and coastal plants. Two species are familiar to gardeners: Zamia furfuracea, the cardboard palm of garden centres, and Zamia integrifolia, the coontie of Florida, long sold as Zamia floridana or Zamia pumila. The genus has also absorbed the former Colombian genus Chigua, whose remaining species is now called Zamia restrepoi.

Key facts about Zamia

  • Accepted name: Zamia L., Species Plantarum, 2nd ed., 2: 1659 (1763), family Zamiaceae (The World List of Cycads; POWO not consulted directly)
  • Number of species: 88 species and 2 varieties, both of Zamia integrifolia, i.e. 90 accepted names (The World List of Cycads, version 2026.08.15)
  • Type species: Zamia pumila L., from the Greater Antilles
  • Former genus names: Chigua D.W.Stev., Aulacophyllum Regel and Palmifolium Kuntze, all synonyms of Zamia
  • Common former species names: Zamia floridana and Zamia umbrosa (for Zamia integrifolia), Chigua restrepoi and Chigua bernalii (for Zamia restrepoi), Zamia latifolia (for Zamia furfuracea), Zamia picta (for Zamia variegata)
  • Common names: coontie and Florida arrowroot (Zamia integrifolia); cardboard plant, cardboard cycad or cardboard palm (Zamia furfuracea)
  • Natural range: south-eastern United States (Florida, Georgia), the Caribbean, Mexico, Central America, Colombia, Venezuela, Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia and Brazil; Colombia (27 species), Mexico (20) and Panama (17) are the richest countries
  • IUCN status: 55 of the 88 species threatened (15 Critically Endangered, 33 Endangered, 7 Vulnerable), 9 Near Threatened, 13 Least Concern and 11 Not Evaluated, according to the 2022 assessments cited by The World List of Cycads
  • CITES: the whole genus in Appendix II since 4 February 1977, with annotation #4 (seeds, pollen and in vitro cultures exempt); Zamia restrepoi in Appendix I since 18 January 1990; Annexes B and A in the European Union (Regulation (EU) 2026/1383)
  • Documented hardiness: most species are tropical and do not tolerate frost. The University of Florida rates Zamia integrifolia for USDA zones 8b to 11; in Roussillon (southern France) a plant in the ground lost its leaves at −7 °C (19.4 °F) under snow, then regrew them. Zamia furfuracea (zones 9b to 11) was killed at −4 °C (24.8 °F) in damp cold in Brittany in December 2007 (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: depending on the species, from forest-floor shade to full sun; always a free-draining medium
  • Habit and size: from 25 cm (Zamia pygmaea) to large trunked plants; stems often underground
  • Toxicity: all species should be treated as poisonous, the seeds above all (see Toxicity and precautions)

Characteristics of the genus Zamia

A Zamia is recognised by its pinnate leaves, whose leaflets have no midrib but many parallel veins and are jointed to the leaf axis, and by its cones, whose scales end in a flattened face, often hexagonal, never drawn out into a spine or horn. Everything else varies a great deal from species to species. The description of the genus given by The World List of Cycads covers the main points.

  • Habit: dioecious plants (each plant is male or female), looking like a small palm or a fern. The stem, thick and full of reserves, is above or below ground, cylindrical or globose. Leaf bases do not usually persist on the stem. In species with an underground stem, the stem often branches into several heads; some species produce offsets at the base.
  • Leaves: pinnate, arranged in a spiral and interspersed with cataphylls (small leaves reduced to scales). They emerge one at a time or in flushes. The petiole often carries prickles, but the lowest leaflets are never reduced to spines. Leaves are hairy, at least when young.
  • Leaflets: simple, the margin sometimes toothed or spiny, with no distinct midrib and no coloured gland at the base. Their shape ranges from needle-like to broadly oval and pleated.
  • Cones: male and female cones stalked or stalkless. The scales of female cones each carry two ovules, rarely three, facing the cone axis.
  • Seeds: almost spherical to ovoid, enclosed in a fleshy coat (sarcotesta) that is red, orange, yellow or rarely white.

Zamia, Ceratozamia or Microcycas?

The American cycads are told apart mainly by their cones. In Ceratozamia, from Mexico and Central America, the tip of each scale carries two clearly visible spines or horns, which Zamia never has. Dioon are recognised by their broad-based leaflets, not jointed to the leaf axis. The Cuban genus Microcycas also has a link with Zamia: its only species, Microcycas calocoma, was first described as Zamia calocoma Miq. (1852).

Taxonomy and classification

Zamia is an accepted genus in the family Zamiaceae. Version 2026.08.15 of The World List of Cycads, the specialists' reference checklist, recognises 88 species and 2 varieties, as the genus page consulted on 1 October 2026 confirms. POWO, the site's nomenclatural authority, could not be consulted directly. The number has grown considerably within a few decades: some twenty species have been described since 2008, ten of them since 2018, the latest, Zamia urarinorum, in 2026.

A genus that absorbed Chigua

The genus Chigua was described in 1990 by Dennis W. Stevenson, from two Colombian species, Chigua restrepoi and Chigua bernalii. It stood apart from all other cycads by leaflets with a prominent midrib from which branched side veins arise. In 2009 Anders Lindström re-examined the genus in the journal Taxon: he placed Chigua in the synonymy of Zamia, made the combination Zamia restrepoi (D.W.Stev.) A.Lindstr. and placed Chigua bernalii under the same species. The World List of Cycads follows this treatment, so there is no accepted Zamia bernalii. The article could be read only through its abstract, which does not detail the arguments. For the reader, the practical consequence is simple: Chigua restrepoi, the name still used by CITES at the 1990 listing, refers to the same plant as Zamia restrepoi. Beware of another source of confusion: Zamia chigua Seem. (1854), a species of the Colombian Chocó, has nothing to do with the former genus; its name simply comes from “chigua”, the indigenous name for cycads in Colombia.

The Zamia pumila complex of the Caribbean

The Zamia of the Caribbean and Florida form a set of very close plants, the Zamia pumila complex, named after the type species of the genus. This group is monophyletic: all its members descend from a common ancestor (Salas-Leiva et al., 2017). In The World List of Cycads, the Zamia of the Caribbean and Florida are now divided into eight species: Zamia pumila (Cuba, Dominican Republic, Puerto Rico), Zamia integrifolia (Florida, Georgia, the Bahamas, the Cayman Islands, Cuba), Zamia angustifolia, Zamia erosa, Zamia lucayana, Zamia portoricensis, Zamia pygmaea and Zamia stricta. Genetic studies show a complicated history. On Puerto Rico, Zamia erosa may result from an independent colonisation of the island, while Zamia portoricensis and Zamia pumila may have separated through geographical isolation (Meerow et al., 2012). In the Bahamas, populations were shaped by changes in sea level during the ice ages; those of Long Island (Zamia lucayana) may come from a separate colonisation (Salas-Leiva et al., 2017).

In practice, the name Zamia pumila was long applied to the Florida coontie, and often still is in the trade. A University of Florida fact sheet notes that the name Zamia pumila refers to a species from the Dominican Republic but is often used as the botanical name of the coontie in nurseries. For The World List of Cycads, the coontie is Zamia integrifolia L.f. (1789), a name proposed for conservation with a conserved type (Stevenson and Reveal, 2011), a proposal recommended by the Nomenclature Committee for Vascular Plants (Applequist, 2012); Zamia floridana A.DC. (1868) and Zamia umbrosa Small (1921) are synonyms. Two varieties have been recognised since 2016: Zamia integrifolia var. integrifolia and Zamia integrifolia var. umbrosa (Small) D.B.Ward, the latter named after the shady places where it grows.

Seven major geographical lineages

Recent molecular studies have shown that the lineages of Zamia closely match regions. The first complete, dated phylogeny of the genus was published by Calonje et al. (2019). In 2024 Lindstrom et al. analysed more than 2,900 loci (portions of the genome) in 77 species, about 90 % of the genus, and identified seven major clades: the Caribbean (I), three species of eastern Mexico (II), most Mesoamerican species (III), Zamia soconuscensis on its own (IV), the Isthmus of Panama (V), and two South American clades (VI and VII). According to this work, the genus began to diversify about 29 million years ago in southern Mexico and Central America, then spread to the Caribbean and South America; the present major clades formed 8 to 20 million years ago (Lindstrom et al., 2024, known from its abstract and from the commentary by Gutiérrez-Ortega and Villarreal, 2024). The authors also detected ancient crosses between lineages, notably in the ancestors of Zamia fischeri and Zamia soconuscensis. Some of these results are still debated: the position of clade II and the make-up of clades VI and VII change with the method of analysis.

Names that no longer refer to Zamia

In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the name Zamia was given to cycads from other continents, and even to a palm. The World List of Cycads lists, for example, Zamia cycadis L.f. and Zamia afra (now Encephalartos afer), Zamia horrida (Encephalartos horridus), Zamia spiralis (Macrozamia spiralis), Zamia maelenii (Dioon edule), Zamia tonkinensis (Cycas tonkinensis) and Zamia pungens, which actually refers to the date palm, Phoenix dactylifera. Conversely, Zamia mexicana Miq. (1861) is not a Ceratozamia: it is a synonym of Zamia loddigesii. Finally, a species can come back: Zamia lawsoniana Dyer (1884), long regarded as a synonym of Zamia loddigesii, was reinstated in 2024 (Nicolalde-Morejón et al., 2024).

Etymology and history of the genus

The name Zamia is said to come from a misreading, but its exact origin is uncertain. The World List of Cycads links it to the word azaniae, which referred to a pine cone. The Flora of North America, cited by the Gymnosperm Database, calls this etymology “equivocal”: the name may come from a misreading of the Latin azania, a kind of pine cone, or from the Latin zamia, “loss”, alluding to the barren look of the male cones.

Linnaeus published the genus in 1763, in the second edition of Species Plantarum, with a single species, Zamia pumila, which is its type species. Its type, chosen later (lectotype), is a plate in Commelin's Horti Medici Amstelodamensis, published at the end of the seventeenth century. The epithet comes from the Latin pumilus, “dwarf”, because of the plant's small size (Haynes, 2022). The species described later in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries often came from plants grown in Europe: Zamia integrifolia and Zamia furfuracea were published in 1789 by the younger Linnaeus in the Hortus Kewensis, the type of the coontie being a Florida plant grown at Kew. Several epithets honour European horticulturists of that period, such as Joachim Conrad Loddiges, a London nurseryman (Zamia loddigesii), or Jean Linden, who introduced Zamia lindenii into cultivation.

Common names

Most known common names are American English and refer to two species; “zamia” itself is also used as a common name.

  • Coontie (United States): Zamia integrifolia. According to the University of Florida extension service, the word comes from a Seminole expression, “conti hateka”, meaning “white root” or “white bread”. The plant is also called “Seminole bread”.
  • Florida arrowroot: trade name of the starch extracted from the coontie, recorded around 1911.
  • Cardboard plant, cardboard cycad, cardboard palm: Zamia furfuracea, because of its thick, stiff leaflets; also “Mexican zamia” in a University of Florida fact sheet. The name “palm” is misleading: it is not a palm.
  • Chigua (Colombian Spanish): indigenous name for cycads in Colombia, the origin of the epithet of Zamia chigua; Species+ gives it as the Spanish name of Zamia restrepoi.

Biogeography and ecology

Zamia live in almost every habitat of the American tropics, from sandy coasts to cloud forest. Colombia has 27 species, 18 of them endemic, Mexico 20 and Panama 17, according to the distributions given by The World List of Cycads. Only one species reaches the continental United States, Zamia integrifolia, in Florida and south-eastern Georgia; Puerto Rico, a US territory, is home to three more.

  • Pinelands and sandy soils: the coontie grows in pine-dominated habitats on well-drained sandy or loamy soils (University of Florida).
  • Cliffs: Zamia cremnophila, from Chiapas and Tabasco, is named after its habitat on rock faces.
  • Canopy: Zamia pseudoparasitica, from Panama, lives on the trunks and branches of rainforest trees, up to 20 m (66 ft) high. It is the only known epiphytic gymnosperm. Its seeds are dispersed in particular by a small nocturnal mammal, the northern olingo (Bassaricyon gabbii).
  • Swamps and mangroves: Zamia roezlii, in Colombia, can live in swamps and mangroves (Gutiérrez-Ortega and Villarreal, 2024).
  • Flooded forest: Zamia urarinorum, described in 2026, comes from the swamp forests of Loreto province in Peruvian Amazonia (Calonje et al., 2026).
  • Rainforest understorey: many species of Central America and Colombia, with large, often pleated leaflets, such as Zamia skinneri or Zamia imperialis.

No strictly aquatic species is recognised in the genus: the name “Zamia hydrophila” does not appear in The World List of Cycads, either as an accepted name or as a synonym, and no reliable source confirms it.

Pollination and roots

The Zamia studied are pollinated by beetles, not by wind. In Florida, observations and experiments have shown that Zamia furfuracea, introduced there, is pollinated by a weevil, Rhopalotria mollis (now Rhopalotria furfuracea), and the native coontie, then called Zamia pumila, by another weevil and a small beetle; plants and insects depend on each other, the insects finding food, shelter and a breeding site in the cones (Norstog et al., 1992). In Zamia furfuracea, Salzman et al. (2020) demonstrated a “push-pull” mechanism: the cones heat up each day and give off a volatile compound, 1,3-octadiene, which attracts the weevils in moderate amounts and repels them in larger amounts, which moves them from male to female cones. Like all cycads, Zamia also have coralloid roots, which grow up towards the surface and house cyanobacteria able to fix nitrogen from the air.

The coontie and the atala butterfly

The coontie feeds the caterpillar of a Florida butterfly, the atala (Eumaeus atala), a small velvety black hairstreak with a red abdomen. According to the Florida Museum of Natural History, the coontie is the only native host plant of its caterpillars. These store the plant's toxins, which makes them, like the adults, distasteful to predators, birds in particular; their bright colours serve as a warning. The atala almost disappeared from Florida when the coontie was over-harvested for its starch: it was thought extinct until a small population was found in 1979 on a barrier island in Miami-Dade County. It has since settled in gardens, nurseries and botanic gardens where cycads are planted, but remains almost absent from its historical habitat, the pine rocklands.

Traditional uses: coontie starch

The coontie was an important source of starch in Florida, first for indigenous peoples and then for a genuine industry. According to a University of Florida fact sheet (Culbert, 2010), sixteenth-century Spanish texts report that the Timucua and the Calusa removed the toxin from the stem, cycasin, by grinding and washing it, then made bread from the remaining starch. The Seminoles took up the technique, and settlers around Fort Lauderdale learnt it from them around 1825. Mills were running in Miami in the 1880s; during the First World War, one of them processed up to 18 tonnes of coontie a day for the army. The starch was sold as “Florida arrowroot”; according to the same fact sheet, the plant's starch content varied from 8 % in summer to 20 % in winter. This massive harvest made wild coontie decline sharply. Do not try to reproduce this preparation: the stem is poisonous if it is not properly processed.

Elsewhere, documented uses are rare. The epithet of Zamia cunaria honours the Kuna people of Panama, who use its seeds to make necklaces. Toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, have been found in the genus, and some species have been used in Brazil against snakebite, according to an ethnobotanical work (Schultes and Raffauf, 1990) cited by the Gymnosperm Database.

Regulation and conservation

Most Zamia are threatened in the wild, and the whole genus is listed under CITES. According to the 2022 assessments cited by The World List of Cycads, 15 species are Critically Endangered, 33 Endangered and 7 Vulnerable, i.e. 55 of the 88 species threatened; 9 are Near Threatened, 13 Least Concern and 11, often recently described, have not yet been evaluated. For Zamia furfuracea, the Friends of Belfast Botanic Gardens note that the steep decline of the species in its coastal habitat in Veracruz is due mainly to the removal of plants, and that it is now more common in cultivation than in the wild. The IUCN Red List itself could not be consulted directly.

Zamia have been in CITES Appendix II since 4 February 1977, like the other cycads not listed in Appendix I. The listing carries annotation #4, in its CoP20 version in force on 5 March 2026: seeds, spores and pollen, seedlings and tissue cultures obtained in vitro and transported in sterile containers, and cut flowers of artificially propagated plants are not subject to CITES. One species is an exception: Zamia restrepoi has been in Appendix I since 18 January 1990, listed at the time as Chigua restrepoi; annotation #4 does not apply to it, and its seeds are covered. The United Kingdom, the United States and Australia apply CITES through their own national legislation and permit systems. For readers in the European Union, CITES is implemented by Regulation (EC) No 338/97: Zamia are listed in Annex B and Zamia restrepoi in Annex A, according to the list set by Commission Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026. These statuses were checked on Species+ on 1 October 2026; this general information does not replace the official texts, which should be checked with the national CITES authority before any purchase or import.

In Florida, collecting coontie from the wild is prohibited: the species is on the state's list of commercially exploited plants, and plants sold for gardens come from nurseries (Culbert, 2010). For an amateur the rule is the same everywhere: buy only seed-grown plants of traceable origin.

Growing Zamia

Cultivation depends greatly on the species: a few Zamia tolerate sun and a little frost, but most are tropical forest plants that need warmth, humidity and filtered light all year round. Outside the tropics and subtropics, only Zamia integrifolia and, at greater risk, Zamia furfuracea lend themselves to outdoor trials; the others are grown in a heated glasshouse or indoors.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Light: the coontie copes with anything from deep shade to full sun, according to the University of Florida, which also recommends partial shade or sun for Zamia furfuracea. A plant raised under glass or in shade scorches if moved suddenly into the sun: on the Fous de palmiers forum (France), several growers advise acclimatising the plant for several weeks before planting.
  • Soil: always well drained. The University of Florida states that the coontie and Zamia furfuracea tolerate sandy, loamy, acid or alkaline soils; a grower in Palermo reports that a Zamia furfuracea has lived for more than twenty years in the ground in heavy soil (see below).
  • Watering: the two cultivated species cope well with drought once established (University of Florida). Too much water causes disease in the coontie. Tropical forest species, on the other hand, need a medium that never dries out completely, but is never sodden.
  • Coastal gardens: the University of Florida rates the salt tolerance of the coontie as moderate in one fact sheet and good in another; that of Zamia furfuracea is rated good. No European data on salt spray were found.
  • Propagation: from seed, sometimes by dividing clumps. The sarcotesta must be removed, because it contains germination inhibitors; the coontie germinates slowly, and a nursery plant takes about five years to reach a saleable size (Culbert, 2010). Seeds of tropical species keep poorly and must be sown promptly. To obtain seed you need a male plant, a female plant and hand pollination where the weevil pollinators are absent.
  • Pests: scale insects (notably the Florida red scale, Chrysomphalus aonidum, the main enemy of Zamia furfuracea according to the University of Florida) and mealybugs, which encourage sooty mould. In Florida, atala caterpillars eat the leaves of the coontie. In several regions where it has been introduced, the cycad blue butterfly, Chilades pandava, a pest of Cycas, also attacks Zamia.

Container cultivation

In cool climates, a pot is the normal way to grow a Zamia. Zamia furfuracea is widely sold as a house plant and spends the winter indoors or in a glasshouse; the forest species need a heated glasshouse all year. Use a free-draining mix, water regularly in the growing season and much less when the plant is resting in a cool place; give bright light without sudden exposure to full sun. A potted plant left outdoors loses the protection of the ground: in Belgium, a coontie wintered in a pot in an insulated garden shed did not survive a long spell at −6 °C (21.2 °F) (see below). In the UK, the botanic gardens that grow Zamia do so under glass (see In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia).

Cold hardiness

Neither of the two outdoor candidates is truly hardy in a cool-temperate climate. Zamia integrifolia is the more resistant: in the ground in Roussillon, in the far south of France, it lost its leaves at −7 °C (19.4 °F) and then regrew them, and frosts of −4 to −5 °C (24.8 to 23 °F) cause it only slight damage. Zamia furfuracea loses its leaves at the first frosts and can die at −4 °C (24.8 °F) when the cold is damp; according to Italian growers, it succeeds in the ground over the long term only in the mildest regions, such as Sicily. No botanic garden or institution has published results of in-ground cultivation in France for these two species; the data below come from amateur growers.

What the reference sources say

In the United States, the University of Florida places Zamia integrifolia in USDA zones 8b to 11 and states that it should withstand a winter minimum of 15 °F, about −9 °C; Zamia furfuracea is given for zones 9b to 11. For the other species, no reliable hardiness data were found: they are plants of tropical climates.

Growers' reports from Europe

At the Jardin zoologique tropical in La Londe-les-Maures (Var, south-eastern France), a Zamia integrifolia survived −7 °C (19.4 °F) in February 2012, with leaf damage. This Mediterranean experience may help readers in comparable climates, such as coastal California or southern Australia, without giving an exact equivalent for any particular place.

By climate. On the Mediterranean coast, the coontie has held on in the ground for about ten years in Roussillon, with leaf damage below −5 °C (23 °F); the same grower keeps Zamia furfuracea in a pot, sheltered from rain, because its foliage suffers at the slightest frost. On the Atlantic coast, a Zamia furfuracea died in Brittany at −4 °C (24.8 °F), and a coontie planted in full sun in the Vendée was completely scorched a few weeks after planting. No results were found for inland or mountain areas. For Zamia furfuracea, several members of the Fous de palmiers forum advise against planting out almost everywhere; for the coontie, they advise burying the stem deeply so that, if the leaves freeze, the growing point survives in the soil. These Atlantic results are the closest available guide for mild, damp climates such as south-west England.

Reports published on forums. These are amateur observations of indicative value. Messages on the Fous de palmiers (France) and Tropicamente (Italy) forums were re-read in their threads in October 2026.

  • A grower in Concarneau (Finistère, Brittany, France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his Zamia furfuracea, grown in the garden, died in mid-December 2007 after a low of −4 °C (24.8 °F) in damp weather; he replaced it with a Cycas revoluta.
  • A grower in Ravenna (Emilia-Romagna, Italy) reports on the Tropicamente forum that his Zamia integrifolia, kept in an unheated cold glasshouse, died immediately after the −7 °C (19.4 °F) frost of winter 2009–2010.
  • A grower in Chimay (Hainaut province, Belgium) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his Zamia integrifolia (which he calls Zamia floridana), wintered in a pot in an insulated garden shed, endured −6 °C (21.2 °F) and several days without thaw in winter 2009–2010: foliage 40 % scorched in January, 95 % in February, plant dead in March 2010.
  • A grower in Roussillon (Pyrénées-Orientales, France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his Zamia integrifolia, a small plant in the ground for about ten years, has its leaves damaged below −5 °C (23 °F) but did not lose its leaves at −5 °C in winter 2010–2011; it was completely defoliated by −7 °C (19.4 °F) and snow in a winter he does not date, then regrew its leaves at the end of the following spring. Another plant, in a pot under the eaves, withstood −4 °C (24.8 °F) (message of March 2012).
  • A grower in Naples (Campania, Italy) reports on the Tropicamente forum that his two large Zamia furfuracea, left outdoors in pots during winter 2008–2009 with a bark mulch and a double layer of horticultural fleece from late December to late March, had a good part of their leaves “burnt”, whereas Dioon spinulosum protected in the same way hardly suffered. He gives no temperature.
  • A grower in Palermo (Sicily, Italy) reports on the Tropicamente forum that a Zamia furfuracea has lived for more than twenty years in the ground in full sun, in very heavy soil that has never been dug (message of October 2010). No temperature is given.
  • A grower in the south of the Vendée (France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his Zamia integrifolia (sold as Zamia floridana), received from a nursery and planted in full sun in March 2017, was “completely scorched” within three weeks; other members put it down to sunburn on leaves formed under glass.
  • A grower whose profile gives Belley (Ain) and Montpellier (Hérault) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that, in his glasshouse, temperatures of 45 to 50 °C (113 to 122 °F) in summer 2009 did not harm his watered and shaded Zamia, but caused the ovules in the cones of Zamia integrifolia and Zamia fischeri to die.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In short: in Britain, Zamia are glasshouse plants; in Florida the coontie is a native garden plant, and large collections of the genus grow in South Florida and in Australian botanic gardens. A species listed by a garden in the BGCI PlantSearch database (consulted in October 2026) shows that it has been declared there at some point, not that a living plant is there today.

  • United Kingdom: the Royal Botanic Garden Edinburgh's online Garden Explorer (updated 18 October 2026) lists Zamia integrifolia; the Cambridge University Botanic Garden catalogue lists Zamia furfuracea (accession of 1987); the Friends of Belfast Botanic Gardens describe Zamia furfuracea grown under glass in Belfast. Kew (Zamia integrifolia, Zamia loddigesii), Chelsea Physic Garden, Oxford and RHS Wisley (Zamia furfuracea) and the Eden Project (Zamia pumila) declare species in BGCI PlantSearch. No Zamia growing in the open ground in Britain was found in a verifiable source.
  • United States: Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida) holds one of the largest living collections of the genus: in October 2026 its online catalogue listed 112 accessions of Zamia integrifolia, 49 of Zamia pumila, 35 of Zamia furfuracea, 30 of Zamia loddigesii and 27 of Zamia decumbens, as well as undescribed Zamia from several Bahamian islands. Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden's Garden Explorer (updated 16 October 2026) has 39 Zamia entries. The rare Zamia restrepoi is listed by Montgomery's catalogue.
  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney has 29 Zamia entries in its Garden Explorer (updated 1 October 2026); at Melbourne, the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria census places Zamia furfuracea and Zamia pumila in outdoor beds; Cairns Botanic Gardens declares 29 species of Zamia in BGCI PlantSearch.

Toxicity and precautions

Yes, Zamia are poisonous, especially their seeds. For Zamia furfuracea, the ASPCA and North Carolina State University Extension cite as toxic principles cycasin, β-methylamino-L-alanine (BMAA) and an unidentified neurotoxin; the signs in dogs, cats and horses are vomiting, sometimes with blood, bloody diarrhoea, jaundice, bruising and liver failure, and one or two seeds can be fatal. The coontie's toxin is the same cycasin that the peoples of Florida removed by washing before eating the starch. Zamia furfuracea, often sold as a house plant, should therefore be kept out of reach of children, dogs and cats; pick up fallen seeds. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Frequently asked questions about Zamia

How many species are there in the genus Zamia?

Eighty-eight, plus two varieties of Zamia integrifolia, according to version 2026.08.15 of The World List of Cycads. The figure keeps rising: ten species have been described since 2018, the latest in 2026. Older sources often give “about 60” or “about 80” species.

Are Zamia pumila, Zamia floridana and Zamia integrifolia the same plant?

Not quite. The Florida coontie is now called Zamia integrifolia; Zamia floridana is a synonym. Zamia pumila is a distinct species from the Greater Antilles (Cuba, Dominican Republic, Puerto Rico), but its name is still often given to the coontie in the trade. All belong to the Zamia pumila complex, a group of close species from the Caribbean and Florida.

Can a Zamia stay outdoors over winter?

It depends on the species and the climate. The coontie is hardy in Florida and is rated for USDA zones 8b to 11 by the University of Florida; in Europe it can be tried in the ground on the Mediterranean coast, stem well buried, in free-draining soil: in Roussillon a plant lost its leaves at −7 °C (19.4 °F), then regrew them. Zamia furfuracea loses its leaves at the first frosts and can die at −4 °C (24.8 °F) when the cold is damp; it is better grown in a pot and brought in for winter, which is the rule in the UK. The other species are heated-glasshouse plants.

Is the cardboard palm dangerous to cats and dogs?

Yes. The whole plant should be treated as poisonous, the seeds in particular, one or two of which can kill a dog according to the ASPCA. Keep the plant out of reach of pets and see a vet at the slightest suspicion that some has been eaten.

What happened to the genus Chigua?

It was merged into Zamia in 2009. Its two species described in 1990, Chigua restrepoi and Chigua bernalii, are now united under the name Zamia restrepoi, a Critically Endangered Colombian cycad listed in CITES Appendix I.

Is there an epiphytic Zamia?

Yes, Zamia pseudoparasitica, from Panama. It lives on trunks and branches, up to 20 m (66 ft) high in the rainforest, and is the only known epiphytic gymnosperm. Its name, “false parasite”, is a reminder that it grows on trees without feeding on them.

Do I need a CITES permit to buy a Zamia?

It depends on what you buy and where it comes from. All Zamia are in CITES Appendix II, but seeds are exempt under annotation #4, except for Zamia restrepoi, which is in Appendix I. For a plant imported from another country, check with the national CITES authority (in the UK, the US, Australia or the EU) before buying; a nursery-grown plant bought within your own country does not normally involve a permit.

Why is my Zamia losing its leaves in winter?

Most often because of cold, lack of light or too much water while it is resting. In Zamia furfuracea and the coontie, losing leaves after a cold snap is not necessarily fatal: as long as the stem stays firm, the plant will produce new leaves in the growing season.
