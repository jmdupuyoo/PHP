Encephalartos dyerianus is a blue-leaved cycad of South Africa known from a single granite hill in the Lowveld of Limpopo, north of Mica, where it grows in open grassland and shrubland on the slopes. This species of the genus Encephalartos, with pale bluish-green leaves gathered in a very dense crown, was described in 1988; it had previously been included in Encephalartos eugene-maraisii, and a description published the same year as Encephalartos graniticola is now a synonym. Listed as Critically Endangered by the IUCN, it has fewer than 500 adults in the wild and lost up to 107 plants to thieves in 2008.

Key facts about Encephalartos dyerianus

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos dyerianus Lavranos & D.L.Goode (1988), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15)
  • Former names and synonyms: Encephalartos graniticola Vorster, Robbertse & S.van der Westh. (1988); before 1988 the population was placed under Encephalartos eugene-maraisii (SANBI)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Lowveld cycad, Lillie cycad (English, SANBI and Species+)
  • Natural range: South Africa, Limpopo, a single granite hill of about 10 ha north of Mica
  • IUCN status: Critically Endangered (CR), criteria B1ab(v)+2ab(v), assessment published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I (whole genus, since 1977); Annex A in the European Union; negative EU opinion on imports of artificially propagated plants from South Africa; Specially Protected in Limpopo
  • Documented hardiness: a single report, of plants in the ground in a sheltered patio in Roussillon, southern France, unharmed at about −3 to −4 °C (26.6 to 24.8 °F) (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, grassland and open shrubland on the slopes of a granite hill; in cultivation, full sun to light shade and well-drained soil according to PlantZAfrica
  • Habit and adult size: aerial trunk up to 5 m long and 50 cm in diameter, often leaning, with suckers at the base; leaves up to 1.3 m
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Montgomery Botanical Center in Florida, several Californian gardens, two Hawaiian gardens and the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney; no British record
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; treat it as poisonous like all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos dyerianus

Look for a very dense crown of pale bluish-green leaves that spread outwards just above the base and then curve gently inwards, on top of an aerial trunk that usually leans. The original description, published in 1988 in the Bulletin du Jardin botanique national de Belgique, could not be consulted. The PlantZAfrica account of SANBI (L. Zondi, N. Muthuhadini and C. N. Sitoe, Walter Sisulu National Botanical Garden, September 2022) gives these characters:

  • Trunk: aerial, unbranched, up to 5 m long and 50 cm in diameter, erect but usually leaning, especially when tall, with suckers at the base; much of it covered by a uniform layer of leaf bases
  • Leaves: rigid, pale bluish green on both faces, up to 1.3 m long, curving outwards for a short distance above the base, then gently inwards; basal leaflets reduced to a series of spines
  • Leaflets: leathery, narrow, 17 to 24 cm long and 10 to 18 mm wide, with entire, unthickened margins, barely or not overlapping
  • Petiole: about 6 cm long, curved in an S at the base and almost hidden by brown hairs
  • Male cones: egg-shaped, 27 to 50 cm long and 9 to 12 cm in diameter, on a stalk 10 to 27 cm long, blue-green turning yellow when ripe, up to eight per stem; they appear in December and shed pollen from February to April
  • Female cones: rounded, green turning yellowish green, up to five per stem, 30 to 60 cm long and 10 to 17 cm in diameter, on a stalk 6 to 12 cm long hidden in the wool of the stem apex; they break up in winter (June to July)
  • Seeds: light brown, 41 to 45 mm long and 30 to 37 mm in diameter

The San Diego nursery Jungle Music describes leaves ranging from yellowish blue to powdery silver depending on soil, culture and exposure, a semi-erect crown that can lie down as it fills out, female cones bluish green and male cones bluish yellow. Some of its figures (leaves of 1.5 to almost 2 m, trunks over 6 m, few suckers, spiny leaflets) differ from the SANBI account, which is followed here.

Hybrids

No natural hybrid of Encephalartos dyerianus is documented. SANBI notes, however, that the plants reintroduced on the hill came from a nursery and may not have been genetically pure (see Conservation status).

Possible confusion

The risk of confusion concerns the blue-leaved Encephalartos of northern South Africa. Before 1988, the Mica population was placed in Encephalartos eugene-maraisii, the Waterberg species: a 2019 article in a South African journal recalls that Giddy (1989) still described forms of that species at Middelburg, in the Waterberg, the Wolkberg and the Mica district. The Middelburg plants are now Encephalartos middelburgensis, and those of Mica Encephalartos dyerianus. PlantZAfrica lists Encephalartos dolomiticus, Encephalartos eugene-maraisii, Encephalartos middelburgensis and Encephalartos nubimontanus as its close relatives, and separates Encephalartos dyerianus by its leaves curving outwards above the base and then inwards, its very dense crown and its yellowish-green cones. The World List of Cycads also cites a comparison with Encephalartos hirsutus in the description of that species (1995).

Growers meet the same difficulty: in September 2013, on the French Fous de palmiers forum, a grower in southern Corsica wondered whether his plant, sold as Encephalartos middelburgensis, was in fact Encephalartos dyerianus, without reaching a conclusion. Only a documented origin settles the question.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos dyerianus is an accepted name. Lavranos and Goode published it in 1988 in a paper titled “Notes on Southern African Cycadales” (Bulletin du Jardin botanique national de Belgique 58(1–2): 219–224), the same paper that described Encephalartos eugene-maraisii subsp. middelburgensis. The holotype (J. Smit s.n., PRE 32864), kept at the National Herbarium in Pretoria, was collected in February 1966 on the farm Lilie, north of Mica, in the Lowveld, on an isolated granite hill at about 700 m: more than twenty years before the description.

In the same year, Vorster, Robbertse and van der Westhuizen described the same plants as Encephalartos graniticola (South African Journal of Botany 54(4): 363–366). The World List of Cycads places that name in synonymy under Encephalartos dyerianus; Species+ also lists the spelling graniticolus. The exact publication dates of the two papers, which decide priority, have not been checked.

Etymology and history

The epithet honours Robert Allen Dyer (1900–1987 according to Haynes, 2022, and PlantZAfrica; another source gives 1986 for his death), South African botanist, monographer of the South African cycads and head of the Botanical Research Institute in Pretoria, which he directed from 1944 to 1963. Dyer himself described several Encephalartos, among them Encephalartos heenanii in 1972, and published an account of the cycads of southern Africa in Bothalia in 1965.

The species entered cultivation even before it was described: according to SANBI, seedlings were available from the provincial nursery at Hartebeesthoek, in the former Transvaal, which operated from 1975 to 1998, while no permit to collect it in the wild was ever issued. International trade began in 1995: 1,359 specimens were exported from South Africa up to the end of 2011, with peaks in 1999 and then in 2007 (172 specimens) and 2008 (169). PlantZAfrica notes that it has become common in private collections even as it has come close to extinction in the wild.

Common names

SANBI and Species+ use the English names Lowveld cycad and Lillie cycad, the latter after the farm Lilie where the type was collected. Afrikaans names built on “broodboom” quoted by some websites have not been found in a reliable source.

In the wild

Encephalartos dyerianus grows only on one granite hill in Limpopo, over about 10 ha. According to SANBI and PlantZAfrica, the plants grow in open grassland and shrubland on the slopes of this low hill, at 600 to 700 m, with an annual summer rainfall of about 500 mm. Most of the population lies within a provincial nature reserve, named Lilly Nature Reserve in the 2017 management plan, but a few plants grow outside it.

Fewer than 500 adult plants were counted in a 2008 survey. According to PlantZAfrica, the population numbered about 600 plants in the 1970s before collectors reduced it to a single viable subpopulation and a few isolated plants. PlantZAfrica also suggests that the species was never abundant because of poor seedling regeneration, which it attributes, as a hypothesis, to the absence of suitable insect pollinators; SANBI's NDF mentions possible reproductive failure linked to the small population. The 2017 biodiversity management plan, by contrast, reports that the population is recruiting well and needs neither hand-pollination nor augmentation. The pollinators of the species have not been identified.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos dyerianus is listed as Critically Endangered (CR) on the IUCN Red List (assessment by J. D. Bösenberg, published in 2022) under criteria B1ab(v)+2ab(v): a tiny range and area of occupancy, a single location and a continuing decline in the number of adults. The Red List itself could not be consulted directly; these data come from The World List of Cycads and SANBI.

The main threat is poaching for collections. According to SANBI's non-detriment finding (NDF, updated in 2015):

  • in 2008, between 78 and 107 plants were removed illegally (PlantZAfrica gives 107 plants in January 2008); they were reintroduced plants, raised at the Hartebeesthoek nursery, which may not have been genetically pure
  • the theft coincided with the peak of legal seedling exports
  • a permanent guard was posted on the site; in 2011–2012 all ranger posts in the reserve were vacant, and the population was watched by rangers from a neighbouring protected area
  • two plants were found in the illegal trade in Gauteng

The 2017 Biodiversity Management Plan for critically endangered and endangered Encephalartos (Government Gazette No. 40793) states that armed guards on site had virtually eliminated poaching and that viable ex situ collections exist in the national botanical gardens. It recommends proclaiming the reserve a specially protected area, adapting its management plan to monitor the cycads, testing microdots and the University of Kent's theft-detection system on the population, and setting up breeding colonies with neighbouring landowners.

Collecting wild cycads has been banned throughout South Africa since February 2007 (under the TOPS regulations, replaced in May 2012 by Government Notice No. 371), and the species is Specially Protected under the Limpopo Environmental Management Act (No. 7 of 2003). Like the whole genus, it has been listed in CITES Appendix I since 4 February 1977 and, in the European Union, in Annex A of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383. According to Species+, consulted in October 2026, the EU Scientific Review Group has held a negative opinion since 7 February 2013 on imports of artificially propagated plants of this species from South Africa, and a review covering the whole genus was under consultation in September 2026. Check the current rules and ask for proof of cultivated origin before any purchase.

Growing Encephalartos dyerianus

According to PlantZAfrica, the species grows best in full sun but tolerates light shade and frost; it is slow-growing and does well in cultivation in well-drained soil, but not with excessive moisture. It makes a striking focal plant or a group with succulents. Jungle Music calls it a moderate grower that flushes regularly and keeps a full crown.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

Jungle Music recommends full sun, or some shade, in coastal and inland southern California, part sun or filtered light in desert areas, and a well-drained soil; it advises planting on a mound to avoid rot. In France, a grower on the Roussillon plain (Pyrénées-Orientales) has grown it in the ground since at least 2011, in a semi-enclosed patio, with Encephalartos horridus and Encephalartos arenarius; he wrote in September 2011 on the Fous de palmiers forum that the plants were “holding up perfectly” in the patio, where the coldest winters bring −3 to −4 °C (26.6 to 24.8 °F), against −7 °C (19.4 °F) in the rest of his garden. He also noted that disturbing the roots can stop growth for several months.

Container cultivation

No published account describes pot culture of this species. Its large size at maturity limits long-term container growing, but young plants can be kept in a deep pot in a mineral, free-draining mix, sheltered from frost and winter wet, in line with the PlantZAfrica advice on drainage and moisture.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

Montgomery Botanical Center, in Coral Gables, Florida, lists two accessions in its online catalogue (200067 and 20230171, the latter from 2023 according to its number). The Huntington, the San Diego Botanic Garden, the San Diego Zoo, Descanso Gardens and the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley in California, and the National Tropical Botanical Garden and the University of Hawaii at Hilo's botanical garden in Hawaii, report it to BGCI PlantSearch, which shows that a garden has declared the species at some point, not that the plant is alive today. In Australia, the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney records it in its living collection. No British garden has been found listing it.

Propagation

The species is propagated from seed and from suckers, which it usually produces at the base of the trunk. PlantZAfrica describes the method: in cultivation, hand-pollination is needed; male plants should be checked regularly to note when cones appear, pollen collected as soon as it is shed and stored at −15 °C (5 °F), and female cones watched, since their scales stay open for three days to two weeks depending on the plant. Seeds are left to mature for a year and sown at the beginning of summer in river sand on a heated bench at 24 to 28 °C (75.2 to 82.4 °F); germination starts after four to six weeks, later without heat. Suckers are removed at about 8 cm in diameter with a clean saw, chisel or knife, the cut treated with fungicide, sulphur or a tree sealant; suckers of at least 25 cm with their own roots can be planted out directly, smaller or rootless ones are left to callus for a few weeks before potting.

Pests and diseases

No disease specific to the species is documented. The Roussillon grower reports that the dense planting of his Encephalartos in a semi-enclosed patio favours scale insects, which he has to treat regularly.

Cold hardiness

No measured cold limit has been published for Encephalartos dyerianus. PlantZAfrica describes it as frost-tolerant, and Jungle Music notes that its habitat can experience freezing temperatures in winter; the only growing report concerns light frosts of −3 to −4 °C.

Mediterranean and European experience

A grower on the Roussillon plain (Pyrénées-Orientales, France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his plants of Encephalartos dyerianus, in the ground in a semi-enclosed patio, “hold up perfectly” at −3 or −4 °C (26.6 or 24.8 °F) in the coldest winters (message of September 2011, without a precise date for the episodes; no protection mentioned other than the shelter of the patio). In Italy, a grower in Ravenna listed the species in 2013 among his plants that had not yet produced new leaves by late June, without any cold data. For readers in comparable Mediterranean climates, these reports show only that the species tolerates light frost in a sheltered spot.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No dated, quantified English-language growing report has been found, and no USDA zone is published for the species by a botanical source; values quoted on some websites (zone 9b or 10a) are not based on any cited measurement. In Encephalartos, survival in cold areas often concerns young plants whose caudex is still underground, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil; an adult with an aerial trunk, as in this species, has no such protection. In Britain it can only be grown under glass; it is held in collections in southern Florida, California, Hawaii and Sydney, but none of these gardens publishes cold data for it.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data are published for Encephalartos dyerianus itself, but the risk is documented in the genus: the toxicity of Encephalartos horridus was demonstrated as early as 1944 by Van der Walt, and Encephalartos, like other cycads, contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; the seeds are the most dangerous part. The ASPCA reports vomiting, diarrhoea, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses that have eaten cycads.

Treat the plant as poisonous: wear gloves to clean seeds, pick up any that fall, and keep plants and seeds away from children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use specific to the species is documented.

Frequently asked questions

Is Encephalartos dyerianus frost-hardy?

It tolerates at least light frost, but its limit is unknown. PlantZAfrica calls it frost-tolerant, and the only growing report, from a sheltered patio in Roussillon, France, concerns plants unharmed at about −3 to −4 °C (26.6 to 24.8 °F). No lower value has been documented.

How does it differ from Encephalartos eugene-maraisii?

Mainly by origin and by its crown. Both have blue leaves and were long treated as one species, but Encephalartos dyerianus grows on a single granite hill in the Lowveld north of Mica, has leaves that curve out and then in, a very dense crown and yellowish-green cones, whereas Encephalartos eugene-maraisii grows in the Waterberg and has reddish-brown cones. Without a known origin, a young plant cannot be assigned with certainty.

What is Encephalartos graniticola?

A synonym. The name was published in 1988, the same year as Encephalartos dyerianus, for the same plants. The World List of Cycads accepts Encephalartos dyerianus.

Why is it so threatened?

Because it exists on a single hill and is targeted by poachers. Fewer than 500 adults were counted in 2008, the year in which up to 107 plants were stolen; armed guards have since curbed poaching, according to the 2017 management plan.

How big does it get?

Large for a blue Encephalartos: its trunk can reach 5 m long and 50 cm thick, usually leaning, and its leaves 1.3 m, according to SANBI.

Can I buy one legally?

Yes, but only with documents. The species is in CITES Appendix I and EU Annex A, and the EU has a negative opinion on imports from South Africa. Ask for the permits and proof of cultivated origin, and check the rules before buying.

Is it poisonous?

It must be assumed so. No study concerns this species, but Encephalartos seeds contain toxins dangerous to people and animals.
