Zamia pseudoparasitica is a cycad from Panama that lives attached to the trunks and branches of trees in the rainforests of the Caribbean slope, usually 7 to 20 m above the ground. This species of the genus Zamia is the only cycad, and more broadly the only gymnosperm, known to be an obligate epiphyte: no young plant has ever been seen establishing itself on the ground. Described in 1854 from a plant sent from Chagres by the collector Józef Warszewicz, it has a short, curved trunk from which 3 to 10 leaves, 1 to 3 m long, hang down, and seeds with yellow flesh, unique among the Zamia of the isthmus. The IUCN rates it Near Threatened, and growing it, in a basket or a pot in a warm, humid glasshouse, remains a challenge.

Key facts about Zamia pseudoparasitica

  • Accepted name: Zamia pseudoparasitica J.Yates (1854), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and by GBIF; POWO could not be consulted directly
  • Former names and synonyms: Palmifolium pseudoparasiticum (J.Yates) Kuntze (1891), the only synonym accepted by The World List of Cycads
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: no English, Spanish or local name attested in the sources consulted
  • Natural range: endemic to Panama (Bocas del Toro, Coclé, Colón, Veraguas), on the Caribbean slope
  • IUCN status: Near Threatened (NT, criterion C1), assessment published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (listing of the family Zamiaceae; seeds exempt under annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union; zero export quota set by Panama for wild-collected specimens
  • Documented hardiness: no frost tolerance known; brief dips to about 15 °C (59 °F) survived under glass according to a specialist nursery; at the Jardin zoologique tropical (Var, France), grown in a pot and kept at a minimum of 10 °C (50 °F) in winter, a growing rule rather than a measured limit (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, on the trunks and branches of rainforest trees; in cultivation, partial shade and a very open epiphyte compost, in a basket or a pot
  • Habit and adult size: short trunk, upright at first then curved, up to 1 m long and 15 cm in diameter; 3 to 10 hanging leaves 1 to 3 m long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Montgomery Botanical Center (Florida) holds six accessions (2000 to 2005); several American gardens and the Cairns Botanic Gardens (Queensland) declare it to BGCI; grown under shade cloth in north Queensland and under glass in California; no British record
  • Toxicity: no analysis specific to the species; treat it as poisonous, like all cycads; the sap of the cones irritates the skin (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Zamia pseudoparasitica

A cycad hanging in a tree, with long drooping leaves, can hardly be anything other than Zamia pseudoparasitica. The measurements below come from the description by David Jones (1993), as given by The Gymnosperm Database, and from the field study by Monteza-Moreno and colleagues (2022); the very short original description is presented further down.

Trunk and roots

The trunk is cylindrical, unbranched, up to 1 m long and 15 cm in diameter; it is curved and covered with tufts of adventitious roots. Jay Vannini (2019), who observed the species in Panama, gives long, curving trunks of about 95 cm for adult plants, and Simon Lavaud (2023), of the French nursery Cycadales.eu, a stem of 50 to 100 cm in the oldest plants. According to Jones, the trunk is held firmly to the tree by the taproot, while many adventitious roots hang down with the leaves. The coralloid roots, which house nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria, form dense hemispherical clusters 5 to 25 cm across. An adult plant can weigh several tens of kilos, which calls for a strong support in cultivation (Lavaud, 2023).

Leaves and leaflets

The sparse crown carries 3 to 10 leaves 1 to 3 m long, hanging, greyish green, flat or slightly keeled (an inverted V) in section. The long, slender petiole (30 to 100 cm) is smooth or carries a few scattered prickles. Each leaf has 40 to 64 leaflets, about 20 to 32 pairs, 20 to 50 cm long and 2 to 4 cm wide: oblanceolate, sickle-shaped, curved, with an entire margin and an acuminate tip, set on the rachis at an angle of about 50°. Vannini has seen leaves exceptionally longer than 3.10 m, and a member of the French forum Fous de palmiers who visited the Panamanian populations reports leaves of 3 to 3.5 m. Lindstrom and colleagues (2024) point out that its numerous leaflets have inconspicuous veins, unlike those of its closest relative, Zamia dressleri, whose leaflets are pleated. In one of Lavaud’s photographs a young leaf turns from copper to green as it matures.

Cones and seeds

Like all cycads, the species is dioecious. Each plant bears a single cone at a time according to Monteza-Moreno and colleagues (2022): a male cone, cylindrical, cream to light brown, 25 to 50 cm by 2 to 4 cm, or a female cone, cylindrical to ovoid, yellow-green to light brown, 25 to 50 cm by 8 to 12 cm. The ovoid seeds measure 1.5 to 2.5 cm by 1 to 1.5 cm and are attached in pairs to each fertile scale. Their sarcotesta, the fleshy outer coat, is yellow, thick and mucilaginous, and gives off a peculiar sour smell when ripe. Taylor and colleagues (2012) note that among the Zamia of the Isthmus of Panama it is the only species with a yellow sarcotesta: all the others have a red one. Its leaves, cones and seeds are among the largest in the genus (Jones, 1993; Stevenson, 1993, as cited by Monteza-Moreno and colleagues). In cultivation, plants start to produce cones when the trunk is about 7 cm in diameter for males, a little more for females (Lavaud, 2023).

What the original description said

The Latin diagnosis of 1854 is a single line: a cylindrical trunk and sickle-shaped, entire, hairless leaflets, narrowed at the base and ending in a point; the cone was unknown. The accompanying note by James Yates describes a short, cylindrical piece of stem, rotten in its lower part, bearing the remains of seven leaves, and six leaflets 35 cm long and 4 cm wide, without a tooth at the tip, curved twice so as to recall the letter S, with broad, strong veins forking twice. The lack of a tooth at the leaflet tip even made Yates hesitate: he wondered whether the plant might be a Ceratozamia.

Hybrids

No natural or horticultural hybrid involving Zamia pseudoparasitica is documented in the sources consulted. In Panama the species nevertheless shares areas with other Zamia: Taylor and colleagues (2012) describe wide sympatry with Zamia obliqua and Zamia imperialis in north-central Panama, and found that some pollinators are not restricted to a single species, without drawing any conclusion about hybridisation.

Possible confusion

An adult plant in place, up a tree, cannot be mistaken. Confusion arises mostly from herbarium specimens, potted plants and the history of the name:

  • Ceratozamia: Yates himself had doubts about the genus, because the leaflets lack a tooth at the tip (see above);
  • Zamia skinneri and the pleated-leaved Zamia of Costa Rica: in their revision of the skinneri complex, Taylor and colleagues (2008) cite Gómez (1982), for whom Costa Rican collections of Zamia had been filed sometimes under Zamia skinneri, sometimes under Zamia pseudoparasitica, although they mixed several species; today Zamia pseudoparasitica is known only from Panama;
  • Zamia dressleri: its closest relative according to Lindstrom and colleagues (2024), but a small terrestrial plant with an underground stem and one or two pleated leaves;
  • Zamia obliqua, Zamia elegantissima and Zamia stevensonii: according to the same study, these three terrestrial species with trunks are closer to it in morphology; the table below compares Zamia obliqua, with which it shares its range.

Taxonomy

The World List of Cycads and GBIF accept the name Zamia pseudoparasitica J.Yates; POWO, the site’s nomenclatural authority, could not be consulted directly. The name was published in 1854 by Berthold Seemann in The Botany of the Voyage of H.M.S. Herald (page 202, with an addition on page 253), from a manuscript by James Yates. The type, collected by Józef Warszewicz at Chagres, without a number, is kept at the Natural History Museum in London (BM). In 1891 Otto Kuntze moved the species into his genus Palmifolium, as Palmifolium pseudoparasiticum, a combination abandoned along with that genus and treated as a synonym. The World List of Cycads accepts no other synonym.

The name has therefore never changed in everyday use, but the range of the species long remained uncertain. In his 1854 addition, Seemann referred to the species a leaflet that Yates had received from Joseph Hooker, said to come from the “eastern slope of the Andes of Peru”, and he considered it a “doubtful” member of the flora of the isthmus: he had never seen it there himself, Warszewicz had given two provenances, Chagres and the eastern slope of the Andes, and the specimens in Hooker’s herbarium bore the second. Modern work records it only from Panama.

Place in the genus

Molecular studies place Zamia pseudoparasitica among the Zamia of the Isthmus of Panama. Caputo and colleagues (2004) had even named “pseudoparasitica clade” the group that brought together Zamia neurophyllidia, Zamia skinneri, Zamia acuminata and Zamia obliqua (according to Taylor and colleagues, 2008); Taylor and colleagues (2012) speak of a “Panama clade”. The most recent work, the transcriptome analysis by Lindstrom and colleagues (2024), places it in clade V, the isthmian clade, which has 15 described species and which the authors consider the most varied in the genus in morphology and habitat, from the only epiphytic cycad to species of salty beaches. Zamia pseudoparasitica forms a subclade with Zamia dressleri, a grouping the authors find “peculiar”, so different are the two plants; the sister subclade brings together Zamia obliqua, Zamia elegantissima and Zamia stevensonii, which are closer to it in morphology. Calonje and colleagues (2019) had already found a similar position, with modest support. The specimen sequenced in 2024 came from the province of Coclé.

Etymology and history

The epithet comes from the Greek pseudo-, “false”, and the Latin parasiticus, “parasite”, alluding to its epiphytic life (Haynes, 2022, as given by The World List of Cycads). Yates explains it himself in his 1854 note: the plant is so called because it grows on the trunks of trees. It draws no food from them, however: it is a false parasite.

The story of its discovery is bound up with that of Zamia skinneri. James Yates, who kept one of the largest collections of cycads in Europe and was regarded as one of the best specialists of his time (Taylor and colleagues, 2008), learned in Berlin in the autumn of 1850 that the Polish traveller Józef Warszewicz had found remarkable Zamia in the mountains of the Isthmus of Panama. He ordered plants from him. Warszewicz shipped three stems with leaflets from the port of Chagres: the one of Zamia lindleyi arrived completely rotten, the one of Zamia pseudoparasitica largely rotten, and only that of Zamia skinneri was in good condition. The 1854 description rests on this material. No source consulted traces how the species later came into cultivation.

Common names

No English name exists, and no Spanish or local name is attested in the sources consulted: The World List of Cycads, Species+ and the scientific literature use only the scientific name. Phrases such as “epiphytic zamia” or “epiphytic cycad” are descriptions, not established names.

In the wild

Zamia pseudoparasitica is endemic to Panama, where The World List of Cycads records it in the provinces of Bocas del Toro, Coclé, Colón and Veraguas. According to Taylor and colleagues (2012), it is perhaps the most widespread cycad in the country: it lives on high, sometimes lower, branches of trees, from Bocas del Toro in the west to the province of Colón, near the northern entrance of the canal. It survives mostly on very high branches in Veraguas, Coclé and Colón, and large populations still occur outside protected areas.

The sources do not give quite the same altitude range or habitat. Stevenson (1993), cited by Monteza-Moreno and colleagues (2022), gives 50 to 1,000 m on the Atlantic slope of the cordillera; Vannini (2019) finds it in lowland rainforest and lower cloud forest up to about 1,100 m. Taylor and colleagues (2012) place it only in submontane evergreen forest, between 700 and 1,200 m, with more than 3,000 mm of rain a year and temperatures of 21 to 24 °C (69.8 to 75.2 °F). Lavaud (2023) describes a humid tropical climate with 2,500 to 3,000 mm of rain and 20 to 28 °C (68 to 82.4 °F), sometimes cooler at the highest sites.

A life in the trees

All the scientific sources consulted agree that the species is an obligate epiphyte. Taylor and colleagues (2012) present it as the only known obligately epiphytic gymnosperm: germinated seeds have been found on the ground, but they are apparently eaten by unidentified animals, and neither young plants nor adults are ever seen in the soil. Lindstrom and colleagues (2024) call it “the only truly epiphytic cycad”, Bell-Doyon and colleagues (2020) “the only known strictly epiphytic gymnosperm”, and Monteza-Moreno and colleagues (2022) the world’s only epiphytic gymnosperm. It is therefore also the only epiphytic Zamia. In 1993 Jones mentioned a second epiphytic cycad, an apparently undescribed Zamia from Ecuador; no later source consulted has confirmed it, and recent authors do not mention it.

Two observations qualify this picture. Vannini (2019) has seen plants that fell to the ground re-root, produce new leaves and survive for a long time on a well-drained slope, and he reports, on someone else’s authority, rare plants on rocks along the Caribbean coast. These remain accidental survivals of adults, not seedlings establishing on the ground.

The choice of trees is also debated. According to Jones (1993), the species is limited to steep slopes in primary forest and prefers trees without other epiphytes. Lavaud (2023), on the contrary, describes it as almost exclusively tied to very large primary-forest trees, probably because their branches carry a thick layer of mosses and epiphytes in which it can settle. A Fous de palmiers member who observed it in Panama saw it only on large trees, very rarely less than 7 m above the ground. Vannini, however, has also found it on fairly small trees and thin branches, in deeply shaded understorey, and photographed it high in the canopy of a degraded forest in the Caribbean lowlands.

Who disperses the seeds?

For a cycad to live in trees, its seeds have to be deposited there. In 2012 Taylor and colleagues knew of no established disperser: field collaborators mentioned bats and toucans, seeds had been seen germinating on the branches of trees without cycads, and the authors had seen a third of a ripe cone disappear in one night. Monteza-Moreno and colleagues (2022) set up camera traps 15 to 20 m up, facing the cones of three female plants, in the Palo Seco, Santa Fe and Omar Torrijos (El Copé) national parks, from October 2019 to March 2020. Seven mammal species came near the plants, but only the northern olingo (Bassaricyon gabbii), a small nocturnal, tree-dwelling carnivore, carried seeds away, 2 to 8 per visit; its visits became longer and more frequent after the cones opened, in late January and mid-February, at the height of the dry season. A toucan, the yellow-eared toucanet (Selenidera spectabilis), was seen destroying seeds, and no bat appeared. The authors present the olingo as a likely disperser that still needs confirmation: they could not follow what became of the seeds and suggest it might cache some in the canopy.

Bacteria in place of soil

Cut off from the soil, the plant has to find its nitrogen elsewhere. Bell-Doyon and colleagues (2020) analysed the DNA of the coralloid roots of plants from Omar Torrijos National Park, in Coclé province: they house cyanobacteria of the order Nostocales, able to fix nitrogen from the air, but also a varied community of other bacteria (Rhizobiales, Acetobacterales). The cyanobacterium isolated is close to those living in symbiosis with mosses, liverworts and lichens. Rodríguez-Castro and colleagues (2025) then studied the bacteria living on the leaf surfaces of 30 plants, at three sites and in two seasons: the nitrogen-fixing community there is dominated by cyanobacteria and varies little in composition, which suggests strong selection by the host plant.

The pollinators of the species have not been identified in the sources consulted. For the Zamia of the isthmus, Taylor and colleagues (2012) name as the main pollinator a small beetle of the genus Pharaxonotha, sometimes with weevils related to Rhopalotria, and note that several pollinators are not specific to one species; in their view this partly explains the wide range of Zamia obliqua and Zamia pseudoparasitica across the isthmus.

Conservation status and regulation

Zamia pseudoparasitica is rated Near Threatened (NT) on the IUCN Red List, under criterion C1, in the assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated 26 June 2020 and published in 2022 (category confirmed by GBIF on 24 October 2026; Red List page not consulted, access blocked). An earlier assessment, by A. Taylor, was published in 2010; its category could not be rechecked at the source.

The threats described for the cycads of Panama apply to it. Taylor and colleagues (2012) found destruction and illegal collecting in almost every population they visited, and thousands of plants destroyed to open roads or pasture. The national protected-area system covers 36 % of the country, but large populations of Zamia pseudoparasitica still live outside it; the authors argue for these forests to be included. A species that depends on large trees disappears with them: felling a forest also takes the plants clinging to its canopy.

The species falls under CITES Appendix II through the listing of the family Zamiaceae (except the species in Appendix I), in force since 4 February 1977 according to Species+. Annotation #4 (version in force since 5 March 2026) exempts in particular seeds, pollen, seedlings and tissue cultures obtained in vitro and transported in sterile containers; international trade in the plants themselves requires a permit. In the European Union it falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, whose current annexes are set by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026; like the whole genus Zamia, it was in Annex A until February 2017. Species+ also reports a stricter national measure by Panama: since 19 June 2025 a zero export quota applies to wild-collected specimens (CITES notification No. 2025/079), except for scientific, personal, medical, educational or judicial purposes; it replaced a suspension of export permits for commercial trade in wild specimens decided in May 2023.

This general information, checked on 19 October 2026, does not replace the official texts: check the regulations in force before buying or exchanging plants, ask the seller for proof of origin and prefer plants raised from seed in cultivation.

Growing Zamia pseudoparasitica

Zamia pseudoparasitica is grown in a warm, humid glasshouse, a heated conservatory or a bright room indoors, in a basket or a pot, in an epiphyte compost. It is demanding: it dislikes cold and wet conditions and dries out quickly. Three sources written by growers describe its cultivation; no botanic garden has published a guide specific to the species.

Temperatures and humidity

Simon Lavaud, of the Cycadales.eu nursery in Montpellier (southern France), published a growing guide in 2023 drawn from his own glasshouses. For best growth he recommends at least 20 °C (68 °F) at night and no more than 32 °C (89.6 °F) by day; nights of 17 to 18 °C (62.6 to 64.4 °F) remain possible if the day climbs to 25 °C (77 °F) or more. His plants have withstood extremes of about 15 °C (59 °F) and, during the 2022 heatwave, up to 40 °C (104 °F) in fairly humid air. Outside the preferred range, the risk is root rot when it is too cool and dehydration when it is too hot. The ideal air humidity is around 80 to 90 %, but the species, like many epiphytes, does not seem to suffer from spells at 50 or 60 %. In his general article on Zamia, Lavaud places the species in the group of Central American and South American lowland forest Zamia, which he considers “difficult” outside the tropics because European climates are too cool and too dry, even in summer.

Basket or pot, and which compost

Both specialist growers grow the species in baskets. Lavaud uses open plastic baskets, which do not rot, in a very humid tropical glasshouse, with a mix of equal parts sphagnum moss, coconut chips and pumice, plus a slow-release fertiliser; a compost for epiphytic orchids, based on pine bark and perlite, also works. He warns that in a dry climate a basket dries out too fast and that a plastic pot is then better, and he prefers slightly small containers, which let the compost dry between waterings in cool weather. Vannini (2019) grows the species in plastic or wooden baskets, in a mix of equal parts fine and medium conifer bark, horticultural charcoal and coarse perlite, bound with a little sphagnum. Other growers, like the garden in the Var described below, simply keep it in a pot.

Watering, light and feeding

In the wild the plant receives only rainwater and grows in decomposed organic matter: Lavaud recommends soft, acid water, rainwater or demineralised water. At 25 to 35 °C (77 to 95 °F) the compost can stay very moist all the time; when temperatures drop, water less often and let it dry a little, but never let it dry out completely, because the plant carries a large mass of leaves on a small mass of roots and dehydrates quickly. Partial shade suits it; Lavaud advises against full sun in southern Europe, especially in dry air, and points out that a plant that has spent the winter indoors will scorch if moved abruptly into the sun. A slow-release fertiliser for foliage plants is enough. Green leaves should not be cut, since they send their reserves back to the plant as they dry, and a hanging plant needs a strong support.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

Vannini (2019) writes that once acclimatised the species can live outdoors in a tropical climate, sheltered from wind and from the midday and afternoon sun. Outside the humid tropics no outdoor or in-ground cultivation has been reported: in the UK, in most of the United States and in temperate Australia it is a plant for a heated glasshouse or a warm room.

Container cultivation

Container cultivation is the rule outside the tropics: a basket in a very humid glasshouse, or a plastic pot where the air is drier, in an open epiphyte compost that is never sodden nor completely dry, in partial shade, with soft water. The experience of the Jardin zoologique tropical, below, shows what this means in practice in a Mediterranean climate.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In short: present in a dozen American collections and declared by one Australian garden; grown under shade cloth in north Queensland and under glass in California; no British record.

  • United States: the online catalogue of Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida) lists six accessions, dating from 2000 to 2005 according to their accession numbers (consulted on 24 October 2026). The Huntington (San Marino, California), San Diego Botanic Garden (Encinitas, California), the Missouri Botanical Garden (St Louis), Naples Botanical Garden and Marie Selby Botanical Gardens (Florida), Atlanta Botanical Garden, the New York Botanical Garden, the National Tropical Botanical Garden (Kalāheo, Hawaii) and the Botanical Garden at the University of Hawaii at Hilo declare it to BGCI.
  • Australia: the Cairns Botanic Gardens (Queensland) declare it to BGCI. Vannini (2019) shows three large plants with leaves 2.5 m long, grown under shade cloth in north Queensland.
  • California: Vannini (2019) also shows a 20-month-old seedling, grown in a basket in a tropical glasshouse, already unfolding a leaf more than 90 cm long.
  • United Kingdom: no record in BGCI or in the online catalogues of British gardens (Edinburgh, Cambridge, Chelsea Physic Garden) checked on 19 October 2026.

A BGCI declaration shows that a garden held the plant at some point, not that it is alive today, and the sources do not say how these plants are grown. Vannini also notes that the species is much sought after by public gardens and collectors for its hanging habit.

Propagation

The species is propagated from seed, after pollinating a female plant with a male plant coning at the same time, usually by hand in cultivation. No sowing protocol specific to Zamia pseudoparasitica has been published. Vannini (2019) found it surprisingly fast from seeds produced by hand pollination in cultivation (see the Californian seedling above). For the Zamia of Panama, Taylor and colleagues (2012) state that seeds need a few weeks of after-ripening once the cone breaks up, and that in an experiment at the University of Panama on other species their germination rate fell over the year: it is best to sow fresh seeds, cleaned of their fleshy coat while wearing gloves. Seeds are exempt from CITES under annotation #4, but they must come from legally sourced cultivated plants.

Pests and diseases

In cultivation, Lavaud (2023) warns of scale insects, which are “very fond” of this species, and of root rot in cold, overly wet compost. Among scale insects, Aulacaspis yasumatsui (cycad aulacaspis scale), which can kill an adult cycad within months (see the article linked below), counts the genus Zamia among its host plants according to the EPPO database, after Marler, Lindström and Watson (2021); no attack has been reported on Zamia pseudoparasitica in particular. In the wild in Panama, the main herbivore of cycads is the caterpillar of the butterfly Eumaeus godarti, which eats leaves and cones; according to Taylor and colleagues (2012), attacked plants usually recover quickly.

Cold hardiness

Zamia pseudoparasitica is not hardy: it is a plant of humid tropical forest, and no frost tolerance is documented. The only low values known are growing conditions, not survival limits.

Mediterranean and European experience

At the Jardin zoologique tropical in La Londe-les-Maures, on the coast of the Var in south-eastern France, Zamia pseudoparasitica is grown in a pot and kept at a minimum of 10 °C (50 °F) through the winter. It is found there to be more delicate to grow than other cycads, and more sensitive to dehydration. This 10 °C minimum is the rule the garden applies, not a measured survival threshold. This experience may help growers in comparable climates, such as coastal California or southern Australia; it suggests that even there the species is best wintered under cover, a deduction rather than a tested result.

In Montpellier, Lavaud reports that his plants have survived extremes of about 15 °C (59 °F) under glass, and he places optimal growth above 20 °C (68 °F) at night. On the Fous de palmiers forum, a grower (location not given) reported in October and November 2015 that a young plant, bought a few weeks earlier and placed in a growth chamber at 25 °C (77 °F) and 70 % humidity, produced a leaf more than 30 cm long in a month; in January 2017 he added that he had gradually accustomed it to the house over the summer and that, near a window at an average of 20 °C (68 °F) in winter, it kept producing new leaves. Another member, who had seen the species in Panama, warned in the same thread that an ordinary cycad compost gives poor growth or makes the plant rot, and that it needs warmth and humidity. No in-ground cultivation has been reported in France, and the Italian Tropicamente forum has only a request to buy the species, without growing data.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No cold report was found in any of these countries. The successful examples cited by Vannini (2019) come from tropical climates or shelters: plants under shade cloth in north Queensland and in a tropical glasshouse in California. In its natural habitat temperatures range from 20 to 28 °C (68 to 82.4 °F) according to Lavaud, and from 21 to 24 °C (69.8 to 75.2 °F) in the submontane forests of Panama according to Taylor and colleagues (2012).

Toxicity and precautions

The chemistry of the tissues of Zamia pseudoparasitica and its effects on animals are unknown (Monteza-Moreno and colleagues, 2022). One observation by these researchers is worth noting: contact with the sap produced by the cones causes moderate skin irritation in people. The risk of poisoning is otherwise documented in the genus and in other cycads: Zamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived (pharmacological studies cited by The Gymnosperm Database), and for Zamia furfuracea the ASPCA cites cycasin and BMAA, a neurotoxic amino acid, with vomiting, bloody diarrhoea and liver damage in dogs, cats and horses; the seeds are the most dangerous part. In the absence of evidence to the contrary, treat the plant as poisonous: keep seeds and leaves out of reach of children and animals, and wear gloves to handle cones and clean seeds. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet straight away.

Traditional uses

No traditional use of Zamia pseudoparasitica is documented in the sources consulted. The available data concern botany, ecology and ornamental cultivation.

Frequently asked questions

Is Zamia pseudoparasitica a parasite?

No. It is an epiphyte: it grows on trees without feeding on them. Its coralloid roots house cyanobacteria that fix nitrogen from the air, and similar bacteria live on its leaves. Its name, “false parasite”, was chosen in 1854 precisely because it grows on tree trunks.

Is it really the only epiphytic cycad?

Yes, according to all the recent sources consulted. Taylor and colleagues (2012) describe it as the only known obligately epiphytic gymnosperm, and Lindstrom and colleagues (2024) as the only truly epiphytic cycad. A second species from Ecuador, mentioned by Jones in 1993, has never been confirmed in the sources consulted. Fallen plants can survive on the ground, but no seedling has been seen establishing there.

How do its seeds get into the trees?

Probably thanks to the northern olingo. In 2019–2020 camera traps placed in the canopy showed that this small tree-dwelling mammal is the only animal that carries the seeds away. The researchers remain cautious: they could not follow what happens to the seeds, and bats and toucans, long suspected, were not seen dispersing them.

How does it differ from Zamia obliqua?

Above all in its way of life. Zamia obliqua is a terrestrial understorey plant, with an upright trunk up to 5 m tall, short toothed leaflets and red seeds; Zamia pseudoparasitica lives in trees and has a short, curved trunk, long leaflets with an entire margin and yellow seeds. The two species grow together in north-central Panama.

Does it have to be grown in a basket?

Not necessarily. A basket works well in a very humid glasshouse, but in a dry climate it dries out too fast and a plastic pot is better, according to Lavaud (2023). The Jardin zoologique tropical grows it in a pot. What matters is a very open epiphyte compost, never sodden nor completely dry.

What minimum temperature does it need?

It depends on the aim: survival or growth. Lavaud recommends at least 20 °C (68 °F) at night for good growth, and his plants have withstood extremes of about 15 °C (59 °F); the Jardin zoologique tropical keeps its plant at a minimum of 10 °C (50 °F) in winter, a growing rule rather than a measured limit. No frost tolerance is known.

Can I grow it in the UK?

Only indoors or under glass: in a warm, humid glasshouse, a heated conservatory or a bright, warm room, with careful watering, since it dehydrates quickly. Outdoor cultivation is not possible anywhere in the UK.

Is it endangered?

It is Near Threatened (NT) according to the IUCN assessment published in 2022. It depends on the large trees of the forests of Panama’s Caribbean slope, and a large share of its populations lies outside protected areas.
