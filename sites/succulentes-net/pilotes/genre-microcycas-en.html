The genus Microcycas has a single species, Microcycas calocoma, a cycad that grows wild only in western Cuba, on the mogotes, hills and valleys of Pinar del Río province. Cubans call it “palma corcho”, the cork palm. Despite its name, which means “little Cycas”, it is a tree-like, palm-like plant that can reach about ten metres. Described in 1852 as Zamia calocoma, a name still found in old catalogues, it was placed in a genus of its own in 1868. It is one of the most threatened cycads in the world: it is classed as Critically Endangered by IUCN, has been listed in CITES Appendix I since 1975, and its reproduction in the wild is very weak, for lack of pollination and of young plants.

Key facts about Microcycas

  • Accepted name (The World List of Cycads; WCVP): Microcycas (Miq.) A.DC. (1868), published in the Prodromus systematis naturalis regni vegetabilis 16(2): 538; a name based on Zamia sect. Microcycas Miq. (1852)
  • Number of species: 1, Microcycas calocoma (Miq.) A.DC.; no infraspecific taxa (The World List of Cycads, version 2026.08.15)
  • Former name: Zamia calocoma Miq. (1852), basionym of the species
  • Family: Zamiaceae, subtribe Microcycadinae in Stevenson's classification (1992)
  • Common names: “palma corcho” or “corcho” in Cuba (Spanish); “cork palm” in English
  • Natural range: endemic to western Cuba, Pinar del Río province; a few of the municipalities cited have belonged since 2011 to the neighbouring province of Artemisa
  • IUCN status: Critically Endangered (CR, criterion C2a(i)), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg published in 2022, cited by The World List of Cycads
  • CITES: Appendix I since 1 July 1975, without annotation (seeds included); Annex A in the European Union; National Natural Monument in Cuba since 1989
  • Documented hardiness: no measured value found anywhere (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, on limestone as well as on acid schist and clay soils; mostly on rocky slopes, under a low, open forest canopy that lets in moderate light, according to the Spanish Wikipedia
  • Habit and size: upright trunk, sometimes branched, 25 cm to 10 m (about 33 ft) tall and 1 to 6 dm across according to Caldwell and Baker (1907), 10 to 20 m (33 to 66 ft) and more, 10 to 90 cm across, according to the Flora of Cuba; rounded crown of 10 to 50 leaves
  • Toxicity: roots formerly used as rat poison in Cuba; toxins known in all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

Characteristics of the genus Microcycas

A Microcycas is recognised by its leaves, which look cut off square at the tip: the leaflets of the middle and top of the leaf are almost the same length, so that the leaf appears truncated. This character shows from an early age. It comes with a large trunk above ground and with jointed leaflets that have no midrib. The description below follows The World List of Cycads and the Flora de la República de Cuba (González Géigel, 2003).

  • Habit: Microcycas calocoma forms a thick (pachycaul), cylindrical trunk above ground, marked with the scars of leaves and cataphylls. The published measurements differ: 25 cm to 10 m tall and 1 to 6 dm across according to Caldwell and Baker (1907), Caldwell having measured a trunk of 9.5 m in Cuba; (0.25–)10 to 20 m, “or more”, and 10 to 90 cm across according to the Flora of Cuba. The figure of 11 m for the oldest plants, often quoted, appears only in the Spanish Wikipedia. Branching is rare: it mostly follows a broken trunk or damage to the terminal bud. Basal offsets sometimes form.
  • Leaves: 10 to 50, pinnate, spirally arranged and interspersed with cataphylls (small leaves reduced to woolly scales that protect the bud). They are 60 to 100 cm long and form a rounded crown. The petiole, about ten centimetres long, has no spines. According to the Flora of Cuba, the leaf axis stays in place for four to five years after the leaflets fall.
  • Leaflets: simple, leathery, with entire margins, crossed by many parallel veins that fork, with no midrib. They are jointed to the axis, that is, they come away cleanly at the base, and are reflexed according to the Flora of Cuba. They are hairy when young.
  • Male cones: one or two per plant, cylindrical, downy, usually 25 to 30 cm long (up to 54 cm) and 8 to 12 cm across, with about 500 fertile scales (microsporophylls) in some twenty vertical rows.
  • Female cones: conical to cylindrical, 50 to 70 cm long and 13 to 16 cm across according to the Flora of Cuba. Caldwell described in 1907 an exceptional cone 94 cm long. Each scale carries two ovules, rarely three.
  • Seeds: ellipsoid, about 3 to 3.5 cm long, with a fleshy coat (sarcotesta) pale pink according to the Flora of Cuba, red according to The World List of Cycads.
  • Chromosomes: 2n = 26 (Flora of Cuba, after Sax & Beal 1934 and Moretti 1990).

Microcycas or Zamia?

The closest genus is Zamia, which also has several species in Cuba. Confusing them is nevertheless difficult. The Cuban Zamia have an underground trunk, few leaves and cones about fifteen centimetres long, whereas Microcycas forms a trunk above ground, a full crown and cones over 20 cm long: this is the key used by the Flora of Cuba. The truncated leaves and large size are enough to recognise Microcycas in the field.

Taxonomy and classification

Microcycas is an accepted genus, placed in the family Zamiaceae and reduced to a single species. The name accepted by The World List of Cycads, the WCVP (Kew) and IPNI is Microcycas (Miq.) A.DC.: Alphonse de Candolle raised to generic rank, in 1868, a section created by Miquel in 1852. The older citation “Microcycas A.DC.”, used by the Flora of Cuba and by older records, is still met with.

The species name followed the same path. The Dutch botanist Friedrich Anton Wilhelm Miquel described it as Zamia calocoma in the Belgian journal Flore des serres et des jardins de l'Europe (vol. 7, p. 141), dated 1852 by IPNI and The World List of Cycads, 1851 by the Flora of Cuba. He placed it in a new section of the genus Zamia, section Microcycas. In 1868 de Candolle made it a distinct genus and created the combination Microcycas calocoma (Miq.) A.DC. Zamia calocoma is therefore a synonym. The Flora of Cuba also mentions an invalid name, “Dendrozamia calocoma”, published by Sauvalle in 1873.

The reference specimen for the name (neotype) was designated by Stevenson and Sabato in 1986: a collection by Charles Wright made in Cuba between 1861 and 1864 (no. 3193), kept in the de Candolle herbarium in Geneva.

In D.W. Stevenson's classification (1992), based on morphology, anatomy and chemistry, Microcycas forms on its own the subtribe Microcycadinae, next to Zamia, in the tribe Zamieae. Molecular studies confirm this relationship: Microcycas is the sister group of Zamia, according to several nuclear genes (Salas-Leiva et al., 2013) and then to the complete chloroplast genome, sequenced twice independently (Liu et al., 2019; Chang et al., 2020). According to Chang et al. (2020), the southern African genus Stangeria belongs to the same chloroplast clade as the American genera Ceratozamia, Zamia and Microcycas. The often-repeated idea of a genus “isolated, with no close relatives” is therefore wrong.

Etymology and history of the genus

Microcycas comes from the Greek mikros, “small”, and the genus name Cycas: “a little Cycas” (The World List of Cycads). The name is surprising for a ten-metre plant. The protologue explains it: Miquel described a young plant grown at the botanic garden in Amsterdam, with a trunk about ten inches (some 25 cm) tall, which “perfectly recalls the habit of a small Cycas revoluta”. The epithet calocoma combines the Greek calo-, “beautiful”, and come, “head of hair”, for the handsome crown of leaves (Haynes, 2022).

In that text Miquel calls the plant a “very distinct species, one of the most beautiful in the genus”, close to Zamia tenuis; he notes that its fruiting is not yet known and gives its origin as “the Isle of Pines, in the Antilles”. De Candolle then separated the genus in 1868 after examining herbarium specimens bearing cones. The tree-like habit was not described until 1907, by the American botanists Otis W. Caldwell and C.F. Baker, from observations made in Cuba: their article is fittingly entitled “The identity of Microcycas calocoma”. Caldwell published a detailed study of the plant the same year, followed by work on its reproduction and anatomy in the 1920s (Reynolds 1924, Chrysler 1926, Downie 1928).

In the 20th century the plant interested botanists mainly for its reproduction: Norstog (1990) studied its sperm cells under the electron microscope, motile as in all cycads. Since the 1980s the National Botanic Garden of Cuba and its partners have monitored the wild populations and studied germination, in vitro culture and pollination (Peña-García et al., 1982 to 1996).

Common names

The only well-established common name is Cuban: “palma corcho”, or simply “corcho”. Species+ adopts it as the Spanish name and renders it “Palm Corcho” in English; “cork palm” is also found in English and “kurkpalm” in Dutch. The origin of the name is debated: the Cuban word corcho refers to a hollow trunk housing a beehive according to the Spanish Wikipedia, which cites dictionaries; others explain it by the look of the seeds, the brittleness of the trunk or a fishing float. Whatever its name, the plant is not a palm.

Biogeography and ecology

Microcycas lives only in western Cuba, in a band about 90 km long in the north-centre of the former Pinar del Río province, according to the synthesis of Cuban work published by the Spanish Wikipedia. The plants form isolated colonies, from a few individuals to more than a hundred, scattered across the Guaniguanico range (Sierra de los Órganos, Sierra del Rosario, Alturas Pizarrosas del Sur) and on the higher ground of the southern alluvial plain.

  • Habitats: vegetation of the mogotes, the steep limestone hills typical of the Viñales valley, semi-deciduous forest, pine forest and remnants of gallery forest, from stream banks a few tens of metres above sea level up to 665 m (about 2,180 ft) in the Sierra del Rosario, according to the Cuban work summarised by the Spanish Wikipedia. The Flora of Cuba notes a preference for moist, shady places. According to the same work, the plant once grew even within the city of Pinar del Río, where a few isolated specimens survive in courtyards.
  • Soils: very varied for such a small range, from alkaline limestone soils to acid siliceous clays, by way of soils on schist and slate, often rich in organic matter at the surface.
  • Light: according to the Cuban work summarised by the Spanish Wikipedia, the species is most abundant on rocky or steep sites, under low forest with an open canopy that gives it moderate light: it finds enough light there without the habitat being too dry for the seedlings to survive.
  • Roots: as in all cycads, coralloid roots house nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria. Mercado (1977), cited by the same source, also found bacteria of the genus Beijerinckia in them.

Pollination and reproduction: the weak point

In the wild, Microcycas produces very few seeds and young plants. This is not a new problem: as early as 1942, Foster and Rodríguez San Pedro judged the species very rare and noted the limits of its reproduction. The IUCN action plan for cycads (Donaldson, 2003) lists reproductive failure among its threats and states that seed production in the wild is minimal.

As in most cycads, pollination is by insects rather than wind: this is what the field work of Vovides et al. (1997), taken up by the Flora of Cuba, suggests. The poor pollination was then attributed to the supposed disappearance of a still unknown pollinator. In 2005 Ramiro Chaves and Julio A. Genaro described a new small beetle, Pharaxonotha esperanzae (family Erotylidae), which feeds on the pollen of Microcycas calocoma and breeds in its male cones; they present it as its probable pollinator. Other Pharaxonotha pollinate American Zamia.

The cycle is slow. According to the Cuban observations summarised by the Spanish Wikipedia, the cones appear in May or June, open between August and October, and the female cones are receptive around September. They then remain on the plant for some ten months, until May or July of the following year, whether their seeds have been fertilised or not. According to the same source, seeds and seedlings are eaten by pigs and rodents, and the seed dispersers remain unknown. It is also often said that in many colonies the numbers of males and females are very unbalanced: the claim comes from the English Wikipedia, without a source; the only published count, made in one population in 2021–2022, concerns just 17 plants of known sex, 13 females and 4 males (Martínez Serrano et al., 2023). Again according to the Spanish Wikipedia, which cites Peña-García et al. (1998), only the well-preserved colonies of the Sierra de los Órganos still reproduce every year; elsewhere, deforested and then replanted mountains have not regained natural reproduction, and some lowland colonies, affected by dams or rice fields, are reduced to old plants that no longer set seed.

The genetic diversity of the species and its consequences for conservation have been studied by Pinares et al. (2009).

Regulation and conservation

Microcycas calocoma is Critically Endangered, and international trade in it is almost closed. The World List of Cycads cites the IUCN Red List assessment published in 2022 by J.D. Bösenberg: category CR, criterion C2a(i), that is, fewer than 250 mature individuals, a continuing decline and no subpopulation of more than 50 adults. GBIF relays the same category. In 2003 IUCN already gave the category CR, with 300 to 500 plants according to its table, whereas Peña-García et al. (1998), cited by the Spanish Wikipedia, estimated the population at 800 to 1,000 plants in 23 localities. These differences partly reflect what is counted: all individuals or only reproductive adults.

The species has been listed in CITES Appendix I since 1 July 1975, like Stangeria eriopus (Species+), even before all cycads were listed in Appendix II in 1977. It now shares this appendix with all Encephalartos and all Ceratozamia, as well as Cycas beddomei and Zamia restrepoi. The listing carries no annotation: unlike the cycads in Appendix II, Microcycas seeds are covered by CITES. International commercial trade is possible only for artificially propagated plants accompanied by the required documents. The United Kingdom, the United States and Australia apply CITES through their own national legislation and permit systems. For readers in the European Union, the species is in Annex A of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, in the list set by Commission Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026. Statuses checked on Species+ on 31 October 2026; check the rules in force with your national CITES authority before any purchase or shipment. This general information does not replace the official texts.

CITES figures show that trade has remained very small: between 1983 and 1999, member countries reported the export of only 30 plants and 162 seeds of Microcycas (Donaldson, 2003).

Conservation programmes in Cuba

In Cuba the palma corcho is protected by law and is the subject of in situ and ex situ programmes. It was declared a National Natural Monument in 1989 (Martínez Serrano et al., 2023). Several colonies lie within protected areas of the Cuban national system, notably Viñales National Park and the Mil Cumbres protected area of managed resources. The species is also on the red list of the Cuban vascular flora (Berazaín et al., 2005).

  • Population monitoring: since the 1980s, surveys have found localities better preserved than those previously known, which were more accessible and more disturbed. The National Botanic Garden of Cuba has published studies of in situ monitoring and reproduction (Peña-García et al., 1996).
  • In vitro culture and seeds: the same garden has worked since 1982 on in vitro propagation and seed germination (Peña-García et al., 1982, 1986, 1992).
  • Living gene bank: Lazcano Lara (2007) describes the ex situ conservation of Microcycas calocoma and four Cuban Zamia at the National Botanic Garden of Cuba. The national network of Cuban botanic gardens also keeps seedlings and young plants.
  • International cooperation: in 2016 Montgomery Botanical Center (Miami) surveyed the Cuban cycads with the Instituto de Ecología y Sistemática and the Empresa Nacional para la Protección de Flora y Fauna, and observed Microcycas calocoma in several localities; a conservation action plan was then in preparation.

Growing Microcycas

In practice, Microcycas is a plant for botanic gardens and specialist collections, grown in a tropical climate or under glass. It is rare and reputed difficult, and any plant must have a documented legal origin (Appendix I).

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

Outdoors, the species is documented only in tropical or near-tropical climates: in Cuba, at the National Botanic Garden in Havana (Lazcano Lara, 2007), and in the botanic gardens of the Miami area.

  • Light: according to Cuban sources, cultivated plants do better in full sun or light shade than in shade.
  • Soil: fertile and well drained. In the wild the species grows on limestone as well as on acid soils: drainage matters more than pH.
  • Watering: no rule specific to the genus is documented; as with other cycads, avoid standing water around the roots.
  • Propagation: from seed, the only common method. Montgomery Botanical Center has published its method (Kay et al., 2011). A Czech grower states that the seeds lose their viability quickly, that they must be sown soon after cleaning, that germination takes a few weeks and that the first cones may appear at about 16 years, exceptionally at 8.
  • Pollination: in cultivation it is done by hand, between a male and a female plant, the species being dioecious.

Container cultivation

Outside the tropics, Microcycas calocoma is a plant for a heated glasshouse or a warm conservatory, in a large, deep, free-draining pot that can stand outdoors in summer. In Europe it is seen under glass in a few botanic gardens: the Hortus Botanicus in Amsterdam, according to the Dutch botanic gardens network, and the botanic garden in Liberec, in the Czech Republic, which displays a large male plant. Even at the University of California Botanical Garden in Berkeley, where most cycads grow outdoors, Microcycas needs a glasshouse (Plant Collections Network, 2015).

Cold hardiness

No measured minimum temperature has been published for Microcycas. The values circulating on the web (USDA zone 10b or 11, a minimum of 1 to 10 °C, 33.8 to 50 °F) are not linked to any observation or any dated cold spell. The only reliable data are indirect: the species comes from a tropical region, and it is grown at the National Botanic Garden of Cuba, in Havana (Lazcano Lara, 2007), and in the botanic gardens of the Miami area.

In France, no trial in the ground has been published, on the Mediterranean coast or elsewhere, and no observation is recorded at the Jardin zoologique tropical. The French forum Fous de palmiers mentions the species in connection with trips to Cuba and regulations, but reports no cultivation in France; on the Italian forum Tropicamente it appears only in passing, notably in a list of Appendix I species. In Europe, Microcycas calocoma can therefore only be considered for a heated glasshouse or a warm conservatory.

In the United States, two articles in The Cycad Newsletter deal with cultivation in Florida: growth and phenology in the south of the state (Calonje, 2007) and cultivation in central Florida, more exposed to frost (Broome, 2007). No dated growing report (place, temperature, result) has been published.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In short: the two great American collections, in the Miami area, are the guardians of the species outside Cuba; elsewhere Microcycas is a rare glasshouse plant. A species listed by a garden in the BGCI PlantSearch database (consulted in October 2026) shows that it has been declared there at some point, not that a living plant is there today.

  • United States: the largest collections outside Cuba are in Florida, at Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden and Montgomery Botanical Center, two neighbouring gardens in the Miami area, both of which list the species in their online catalogues. Their staff have germinated Microcycas seeds since the 1980s (Hubbuch, 1987, cited by Donaldson, 2003). Montgomery Botanical Center has developed a protocol for hand pollination and propagation, sends pollen to other gardens and has published its experience (Kay et al., 2011). According to IUCN (Donaldson, 2003), Microcycas is well represented in at least one collection, whose seeds have been distributed to gardens around the world. At the Missouri Botanical Garden in St. Louis, a plant accessioned in 1987, probably raised from Montgomery seed, grows under glass (Deloso et al., 2025). The Huntington, Lotusland, the San Diego Zoo and Naples Botanical Garden declare it in BGCI.
  • United Kingdom: Kew and the University of Bristol Botanic Garden declare it in BGCI PlantSearch; a 2010 study of the Edinburgh collection found every cycad genus there except Microcycas. No plant on public display has been verified on site.
  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney lists Microcycas calocoma in its Garden Explorer (updated 1 October 2026); it is the only Australian garden to declare it.

Kay et al. (2011) argue for a simple idea: producing and selling cultivated plants reduces pressure on wild populations. Seeds from hand pollination were sold at public auction, in partnership with the Florida nursery growers' association; over fifteen years the average price per seed fell by more than half as supply increased, and the authors expect it to reach a level at which collecting in the wild will no longer pay.

Toxicity and precautions

Yes, Microcycas should be regarded as poisonous. Caldwell reported in 1907 that, according to local lore in the area where he studied it (the sierras of Pinar del Río), the roots were used as rat poison. No toxicity study specific to the genus is documented, but the Flora of Cuba recalls that the Zamiaceae contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, neocycasin, macrozamin) and a neurotoxic amino acid, BMAA. These toxins cause serious poisoning in other cycads, particularly through the seeds. Keep seeds and debris out of reach of children and animals, wear gloves to clean the seeds, and if any part has been eaten, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

The species of the genus Microcycas

The genus has a single species, accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and by the WCVP, which has an English page on the site.

  • Microcycas calocoma (Miq.) A.DC. (1868): western Cuba (Pinar del Río); a palm-like tree, trunk up to 10 m according to Caldwell and Baker (1907), 10 to 20 m and more according to the Flora of Cuba; leaves with a truncated tip; Critically Endangered (CR, 2022); CITES Appendix I; synonym: Zamia calocoma Miq.

Frequently asked questions

How many species of Microcycas are there?

One. Microcycas calocoma is the only species in the genus, with no subspecies or varieties, according to The World List of Cycads and the WCVP. The genus is therefore called monotypic.

Why call it a “little Cycas” when it is so tall?

Because the name was given from a young plant. Miquel, who described the species in 1852, knew only a small cultivated plant; the tree-like habit was not described until 1907, from observations made in Cuba. The name, chosen from the plants known at the time, has stuck.

Are Zamia calocoma and Microcycas calocoma the same plant?

Yes. Zamia calocoma is the name given by Miquel in 1852; Alphonse de Candolle moved the species to the genus Microcycas in 1868. The correct name today is Microcycas calocoma, and Zamia calocoma is a synonym.

Why does Microcycas reproduce so poorly in the wild?

Because several obstacles add up. The plant is dioecious, its colonies are small and isolated, and its probable pollinator, the beetle Pharaxonotha esperanzae, seems to have disappeared from degraded sites. Seeds and seedlings are also eaten by pigs and rodents.

Can I buy a Microcycas calocoma legally?

Yes, but only cultivated plants with their papers. The species is in CITES Appendix I, seeds included: you need artificially propagated plants accompanied by the CITES documents required in your country (and in the European Union, by EU documents). Keep invoices and certificates, and beware of any offer without provenance.

Can Microcycas be grown outdoors in the UK or a cool climate?

No, nothing supports it. No trial in the ground has been published in Europe and no measured minimum temperature has been published. It is a tropical plant from Cuba, to be grown in a heated glasshouse or warm conservatory, with the pot perhaps moved outdoors in summer.

Is Microcycas poisonous?

Yes, it should be treated as such. Its roots were used as rat poison in Cuba, and all cycads contain toxins, especially in the seeds. If a child or an animal swallows any part, contact a poison centre or a vet.
