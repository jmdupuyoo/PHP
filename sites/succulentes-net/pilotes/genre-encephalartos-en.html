The genus Encephalartos brings together the great cycads of Africa: 65 species, all African, ranging from the Eastern Cape in South Africa to South Sudan and Nigeria. They are palm-like plants with an above-ground or underground trunk, and their stiff leaves, often blue-grey and spiny, have made them famous among collectors. South Africa is home to 37 of them, 29 found nowhere else. The genus is also one of the most threatened in the plant kingdom: five species now survive only in cultivation, including Encephalartos woodii, known from a single male plant, and poaching for gardens and collections continues to empty wild populations. Every species is listed in CITES Appendix I. In Britain, Kew's Palm House holds an Encephalartos altensteinii planted in 1775. One species changed its spelling in 2024: Encephalartos caffer, a name still common in catalogues, is now written Encephalartos afer.

Key facts about Encephalartos

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos Lehm., published in 1834 by Johann Georg Christian Lehmann (Nov. Stirp. Pug. 6: 11); accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15); POWO could not be consulted directly
  • Number of species: 65 species and 6 infraspecific taxa, i.e. 71 accepted names (The World List of Cycads, version 2026.08.15)
  • Former names: no synonym at genus level; at species level Encephalartos caffer has become Encephalartos afer (2024), and several names described between the 1980s and the 2000s are now synonyms (see Taxonomy)
  • Family: Zamiaceae
  • Common names: bread tree (broodboom in Afrikaans) for Encephalartos altensteinii, and many English names for individual species, such as Wood's cycad, Eastern Cape blue cycad or Karoo cycad (see Common names)
  • Natural range: sub-Saharan Africa, from South Africa (37 species) to South Sudan and West Africa (Benin, Ghana, Nigeria, Togo); no species in Madagascar
  • IUCN status: 5 species Extinct in the Wild (EW), 19 Critically Endangered (CR), 8 Endangered (EN), 20 Vulnerable (VU), 6 Near Threatened (NT) and 7 Least Concern (LC); 2022 assessments, except eleven East African species assessed in 2010
  • CITES: the whole genus in Appendix I since 4 February 1977, with no annotation (seeds are included); Annex A in the European Union (Regulation (EU) 2026/1383)
  • Documented hardiness: no data published by a garden or institution. On a French growers' forum, Encephalartos friderici-guilielmi came through −9.5 to −10 °C (14.9 to 14 °F) in the ground in February 2012 (Nantes under a cloche, Béarn without protection) and Encephalartos lehmannii −7 °C (19.4 °F) without protection in Roussillon; Encephalartos horridus has not been tested in the ground below −4 °C (24.8 °F). In Ravenna (Italy), a potted Encephalartos friderici-guilielmi under a terrace and an Encephalartos lehmannii in a cold glasshouse did not survive −7 °C (19.4 °F) in December 2009. Survivals concern mostly young plants (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: sun or bright light for most cultivated species, very free-draining soil; too much water, especially in winter or on a young plant, is the cause of failure growers cite most
  • Habit: from small species, such as Encephalartos humilis (“humble”), to tree-like species, with trunks up to 6.7 m (22 ft) in Encephalartos altensteinii (Palmer and Pitman, 1972)
  • Toxicity: seeds and other parts poisonous, as in all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

Characteristics of the genus Encephalartos

An Encephalartos is recognised by its leaflets, which have no midrib but parallel veins, and by its true cones, male and female, borne on separate plants. The World List of Cycads gives the following description:

  • Habit: dioecious, palm-like plants with a thick, cylindrical stem, above or below ground, often massive, clad in the bases of old leaves. Most species produce offsets at the base; branching above ground is uncommon.
  • Leaves: pinnate, arranged in a spiral and separated by cataphylls (leaves reduced to scales). The lowest leaflets are often reduced to spines. The petiole has no prickles; it is often swollen at the base, and in some species has a collar of a different colour. Young leaves are hairy.
  • Leaflets: simple, the margins often spiny, toothed or lobed, with many parallel veins that fork, no distinct midrib, not jointed.
  • Cones: the scales of male and female cones end in an enlarged part, often flattened or faceted, but never drawn out into an upturned spine. Each female scale carries two ovules, rarely three, facing the cone axis.
  • Seeds: almost round or oblong, covered by a fleshy coat (sarcotesta) that is red, yellow, orange or brown.

Encephalartos, Cycas or Stangeria?

Three cycad genera grow wild in Africa, and the simplest way to tell them apart is the leaflet. That of a Cycas has a single thick midrib; that of an Encephalartos, many parallel veins and no midrib. The female organs differ too: in Cycas the fertile leaves form an open rosette at the top of the stem, whereas an Encephalartos forms a true cone. The only African Cycas accepted by The World List of Cycads, Cycas thouarsii, lives in Kenya, Tanzania, the Comoros and Madagascar. The third genus, Stangeria, has a single species from the Eastern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal, whose leaflets carry a strong midrib and many side veins, like a fern frond (The World List of Cycads).

Taxonomy and classification

Encephalartos is an accepted genus in the family Zamiaceae. The World List of Cycads, the checklist of the IUCN Cycad Specialist Group, recognises 65 species and 6 infraspecific taxa in its version of 15 August 2026. The subspecies form three pairs: Encephalartos barteri subsp. barteri and subsp. allochrous, Encephalartos ferox subsp. ferox and subsp. emersus, and Encephalartos tegulaneus subsp. tegulaneus and subsp. powysii. In this checklist the genus is not divided into subgenera or sections. POWO could not be consulted directly.

From Encephalartos caffer to Encephalartos afer

The only name change affecting a species common in cultivation is recent. In July 2024 the Nomenclature Section of the 20th International Botanical Congress, meeting in Madrid, adopted a new Article 61.6 of the International Code of Nomenclature: epithets formed on the root “caf(f)r-”, derived from a term that has become offensive in southern Africa, are banned and must be corrected to “afr-” (SANBI, report on the 2025 release of the South African National Plant Checklist). IPNI and The World List of Cycads therefore write Encephalartos afer (Thunb.) Lehm., stating that the spelling of the epithet was corrected under Article 61.6 of the Madrid Code. The basionym is Cycas afra, described by Thunberg in 1775. For growers the plant does not change: Encephalartos caffer and Encephalartos afer refer to the same species, and Species+ still uses the old spelling.

Species described, then merged

More than half of today's species were described after 1950, many between 1988 and 2002. Several of these descriptions were later placed in synonymy. According to The World List of Cycads, Encephalartos imbricans Vorster (1995) is placed under Encephalartos equatorialis, Encephalartos successibus Vorster (1995) under Encephalartos whitelockii, Encephalartos venetus Vorster (1996) under Encephalartos nubimontanus, Encephalartos verrucosus (1988) under Encephalartos dolomiticus, Encephalartos graniticola (1988) under Encephalartos dyerianus, Encephalartos flavistrobilus (2006) under Encephalartos schaijesii and Encephalartos eximius I.Verd. (1954) under Encephalartos cycadifolius. Encephalartos kanga Pócs & Q.Luke (2008) and Encephalartos voiensis (1989) are synonyms of Encephalartos kisambo; for Encephalartos kanga, The World List of Cycads gives the reasons put forward by Haynes (2009): strong overlap of vegetative characters, no data on male cones and female cones described from a single plant. Conversely, Encephalartos middelburgensis, first described as a subspecies of Encephalartos eugene-maraisii by Lavranos and Goode (1988), is now a species. The studies behind the other synonymies have not been checked for this page.

Etymology and history of the genus

The name Encephalartos means “bread in the head”. It comes from the Greek en, “in”, kephalē, “head”, and artos, “bread”, in reference to the meal that local people made from the stem of some species (The World List of Cycads). The German botanist Johann Georg Christian Lehmann, director of the Hamburg botanic garden, published the genus in 1834, together with several Cape species, including Encephalartos altensteinii, Encephalartos friderici-guilielmi and Encephalartos lehmannii. Some of these species were already known under other genera: Encephalartos horridus, Encephalartos longifolius and Encephalartos cycadifolius had been described by Jacquin in 1801 as Zamia, and Encephalartos afer by Thunberg in 1775 as Cycas.

The epithets tell part of this story. Encephalartos friderici-guilielmi honours King Frederick William III of Prussia, whose palm house at Potsdam had no equal at the time, and Encephalartos altensteinii Baron Karl von Altenstein, a statesman at his court and a patron of science. Encephalartos lehmannii honours Lehmann himself, Encephalartos woodii John Medley Wood, curator of the Natal Botanic Gardens, and Encephalartos dyerianus the South African botanist Robert Allen Dyer, monographer of the South African cycads. Encephalartos eugene-maraisii is dedicated to the writer and naturalist Eugène Marais, who had pointed the plant out to his niece, the botanist Inez Verdoorn, author of its description in 1945. Encephalartos inopinus, “unexpected”, was discovered on a farm called Onverwagt, “unexpected” in Dutch (Haynes, 2022, cited by The World List of Cycads).

The genus also has a long history in British cultivation. According to Guinness World Records, the Encephalartos altensteinii in the Palm House at Kew was brought from South Africa and planted in 1775, which makes it the oldest pot plant in the world; a 2025 study describes it as the living type specimen of the species (Deloso et al., 2025). Kew also received one of the stems of the single wild Encephalartos woodii: in 1899 according to Guinness World Records.

Common names

“Bread tree” is the best-known English name associated with the genus, but most common names apply to single species and come from southern Africa or from the nursery trade:

  • Afrikaans: broodboom, “bread tree”, for Encephalartos altensteinii (Palmer and Pitman, 1972, cited by the Gymnosperm Database); the word echoes the etymology of the genus
  • Zulu: uJobane for Encephalartos altensteinii (same source)
  • Taita (Kenya): kisambo, a local name that became the epithet of Encephalartos kisambo (The World List of Cycads)
  • English: Wood's cycad (Encephalartos woodii), Eastern Cape blue cycad (Encephalartos horridus), Drakensberg cycad (Encephalartos ghellinckii), Winterberg cycad (Encephalartos cycadifolius) according to the cycad encyclopaedia of the Palm & Cycad Societies of Florida; Karoo cycad (Encephalartos lehmannii) according to Palmer and Pitman; woolly cycad (Encephalartos heenanii), Escarpment cycad (Encephalartos brevifoliolatus) and Blue cycad (Encephalartos nubimontanus) according to SANBI documents

These names are not always unambiguous: on the PlantZAfrica pages of SANBI, for instance, the Xhosa name “umguza” is given for Encephalartos altensteinii, Encephalartos natalensis and Encephalartos ghellinckii, and “umphanga” for the first two.

Biogeography and ecology

The genus Encephalartos is exclusively African, and its centre of diversity is South Africa. According to the distributions in The World List of Cycads, the 65 species are spread by country as follows (a species can occur in several countries):

  • South Africa: 37 species, 29 of them endemic; 14 in the Eastern Cape, 13 in KwaZulu-Natal, 9 in Limpopo, 7 in Mpumalanga and 1 in Gauteng
  • Mozambique: 12 species, 3 of them endemic (in Manica and Nampula provinces)
  • Eswatini: 10 species, 1 of them endemic
  • Democratic Republic of the Congo: 7 species, 2 of them endemic
  • Tanzania: 5 species; Kenya: 4; Uganda: 4, 3 of them endemic
  • Zimbabwe: 3 species; Angola, South Sudan and Zambia: 2 each
  • Malawi, Central African Republic, Benin, Ghana, Nigeria and Togo: 1 species each

The northernmost species live in South Sudan: Encephalartos septentrionalis, “the northern one”, described in 1871 by Schweinfurth, and Encephalartos mackenziei. None is known from Madagascar. In the west, a single species, Encephalartos barteri, occurs from Benin to Nigeria.

Habitats are very varied. Encephalartos arenarius owes its name to the ancient dune ridges of the Eastern Cape where it grows, Encephalartos dolomiticus to the dolomite soils of Limpopo and Encephalartos equatorialis to its locality, on the northern shore of Lake Victoria, close to the equator. Type localities range from sea level (Encephalartos ferox subsp. emersus, on low rises above the floodplains of Inhambane) to more than 2,000 m (Encephalartos tegulaneus, collected at about 7,000 ft on Mount Lololokwe, in Kenya). According to SANBI (PlantZAfrica), Encephalartos ghellinckii grows in grassland up to about 2,400 m in the Drakensberg, where winters bring severe frost and snow and grass fires are frequent; fire plays an important part in the production of cones and new leaves. Fire scars from savanna burns are, according to SANBI, characteristic of plants of wild origin.

Five species extinct in the wild

Five species of Encephalartos are classed as Extinct in the Wild (EW) by The World List of Cycads, which cites for each an IUCN Red List assessment published in 2022 (Bösenberg, 2022). SANBI documents cite the same assessment for Encephalartos brevifoliolatus and Encephalartos nubimontanus; the Red List could not be consulted directly. They are:

  • Encephalartos woodii (KwaZulu-Natal): only one plant was ever found, a male, spotted in 1895 by John Medley Wood in the Ngoye forest, in Zululand. It was an isolated plant with four stems and a few offsets at the base. The offsets were removed and grown in South Africa and abroad, two large stems were planted in the Durban botanic garden in 1907, and the last stem was removed from Ngoye around 1916 (Palmer and Pitman, 1972). All cultivated plants are therefore offsets of this male individual, and no female plant is known despite many expeditions.
  • Encephalartos heenanii (Mpumalanga and Eswatini): 115 plants (326 stems) counted in 1995, about 24 plants (45 stems) in 2006 and a single plant seen from a helicopter in 2013, in a provincial nature reserve; plants have also disappeared from the few known sites in Eswatini (SANBI, 2015).
  • Encephalartos brevifoliolatus (Limpopo): described in 1996 from five to seven scattered plants, all male, and extinct in the wild shortly after its description; listed as EW on the Red List since 2006. A single cultivated plant is said to have produced a female cone (SANBI, 2023).
  • Encephalartos nubimontanus (Limpopo, Wolkberg region): 66 plants seen in the 1990s, eight in 2000, a few stems in 2003–2004 and none in 2010–2011; all known subpopulations were removed by collectors (SANBI, 2023).
  • Encephalartos relictus (Eswatini): described in 2001 from a collection made in March 1971 in the Lebombo Mountains near Siteki; the name means “abandoned” (The World List of Cycads). Details of the number and sex of the known plants have not been checked for this page.

Other species are close to the same situation. The World List of Cycads classes Encephalartos chimanimaniensis as Endangered (EN), not extinct, contrary to what some websites claim; Encephalartos dolomiticus, Encephalartos cupidus, Encephalartos latifrons, Encephalartos hirsutus and Encephalartos middelburgensis are Critically Endangered (CR).

Regulation and conservation

Every species of Encephalartos is listed in CITES Appendix I, the highest level of protection. According to Species+, the official database for CITES and the European Union, the genus has been listed since 4 February 1977. Unlike the cycads in Appendix II, the listing carries no annotation: seeds, pollen and seedlings are covered just like plants. International commercial trade in wild-collected plants is prohibited. Artificially propagated plants of Appendix I species are treated as Appendix II specimens (Article VII of the Convention), which allows their export from registered nurseries, according to SANBI documents. One other cycad genus is listed in Appendix I in its entirety: Ceratozamia.

The United Kingdom, the United States and Australia apply CITES through their own national legislation and permit systems; check with the national CITES authority before importing or exporting any plant or seed. For readers in the European Union, the genus has been listed in Annex A of Regulation (EC) No 338/97 since 1 June 1997; the list in force is set by Commission Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026. Beyond the permit, the EU follows the opinions of its Scientific Review Group on imports of artificially propagated plants from South Africa. According to Species+, consulted on 1 October 2026:

  • positive opinion, up to a caudex diameter of 15 cm: Encephalartos altensteinii, Encephalartos ferox, Encephalartos friderici-guilielmi, Encephalartos ghellinckii, Encephalartos lanatus, Encephalartos lehmannii, Encephalartos longifolius, Encephalartos natalensis, Encephalartos paucidentatus, Encephalartos princeps, Encephalartos senticosus, Encephalartos transvenosus and Encephalartos trispinosus (opinion of 20 June 2011);
  • positive up to a 7 cm caudex and negative above for several species, including Encephalartos cycadifolius, Encephalartos gratus, Encephalartos hildebrandtii and Encephalartos manikensis (opinions of 2011 and 2013); positive only up to 7 cm for Encephalartos afer (under the name caffer), Encephalartos villosus and, since 2017, Encephalartos humilis;
  • negative opinion, whatever the size, since 7 February 2013, for many species, including Encephalartos horridus, Encephalartos laevifolius, Encephalartos woodii, Encephalartos latifrons and all species extinct in the wild.

Before importing, buying or selling an Encephalartos, check its status on Species+, ask the national CITES authority if necessary and insist on documents proving that the plant was propagated in cultivation. This general information does not replace the official texts.

On the IUCN Red List, according to the references cited by The World List of Cycads, 47 of the 65 species are threatened (19 CR, 8 EN, 20 VU), in addition to the 5 species extinct in the wild. Most assessments date from 2022; eleven species from East Africa and South Sudan have a 2010 assessment. The status of each species is given in the species list further down.

Poaching in South Africa

Yes, poaching is now the main threat to South Africa's Encephalartos, and it serves mainly the garden and collection market. According to SANBI, South Africa's scientific authority, targeted removal from the wild is the main threat to 36 of the country's 37 species, 70 % of which are threatened with extinction. Cycads have been protected by provincial ordinances since the 1970s, and collecting wild cycads has been banned nationwide since February 2007 (TOPS regulations, replaced in May 2012 by Government Notice No. 371). Poaching continues all the same (SANBI, 2015).

A report by the Global Initiative Against Transnational Organized Crime (2021) gives a measure of it:

  • in August 2014, over two rainy nights, thieves took 24 Encephalartos latifrons, a species with fewer than 100 plants left in the wild, from Kirstenbosch National Botanical Garden in Cape Town;
  • in Eastern Cape province, almost 1,000 cycads were uprooted in 27 thefts between 2011 and 2018, and the more than 630 cycads seized by the police between 2011 and 2016 all belonged to Red List species;
  • South African authorities rank this trade among priority wildlife crimes, alongside the poaching of rhinos, elephants and abalone;
  • the South African domestic market outweighs international trafficking, and the existence of a legal trade in cultivated plants serves as cover for laundering wild plants.

Uprooted plants survive poorly: according to SANBI, 30 to 50 % of cycads removed from the wild die in the following years. In one 2006 case, poachers tried to grind off the fire scars, typical of wild plants, on three Encephalartos heenanii. Cycad bark is also harvested illegally for traditional medicine (muthi), in quantities of several tonnes a year according to researchers cited by the Global Initiative, but this market does less damage than the horticultural trade. In the United States, a batch of poached Encephalartos hirsutus seized by the US Fish and Wildlife Service is kept at the University of California Botanical Garden in Berkeley (Deloso et al., 2025).

Growing Encephalartos

Encephalartos grow well in pots anywhere, and in the ground in the mildest areas, on three conditions: very free-draining soil, summer warmth and a winter without excess water. Growth is slow, from one to a few leaves a year, and large specimens remain expensive, so many enthusiasts start from young plants or seed.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Soil: largely mineral (pumice, volcanic gravel, grit, coarse sand), with a limited share of well-rotted compost. In the ground, plant on a mound or in a rock garden.
  • Watering: regular during the warm season, when the plant grows, letting the soil dry between waterings; almost none in winter. On the Fous de palmiers forum (France), the failures reported are due more often to water than to cold: rot after a wet winter in Brittany, the near-total loss of a young Encephalartos cycadifolius watered heavily in summer in Roussillon.
  • Light: full sun for the blue-leaved species, sun or partial shade for the green species. Plants coming out of shelter in spring must be accustomed gradually to direct sun to avoid scorch.
  • Propagation from seed: seeds must be fresh and fertilised. Cycads can produce normal-sized seeds without fertilisation, which will not germinate; be wary of seed sold without guarantees. Encephalartos seeds are themselves covered by CITES Appendix I.
  • Transplanting: growers advise against moving a well-established plant; a transplanted specimen can lose all its leaves and stay several years without growing.

Container cultivation

In most of Britain and other cool climates, a pot is the safe way to grow an Encephalartos. Plants spend the summer outdoors and the winter in a frost-free glasshouse, a conservatory or a bright room. Deep pots suit the taproot of young plants; use the same very mineral mix as in the ground, and keep it almost dry in winter. A pot does not protect from cold: in Ravenna, two potted plants sheltered from rain died at −7 °C (19.4 °F) (see Cold hardiness).

Cold hardiness

The hardiness of an Encephalartos depends as much on the age of the plant and the wetness of the soil as on temperature. Survivals in cold areas often concern young plants with an underground caudex, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil and, in the wild, by snow. Adult specimens with an above-ground trunk do not have this protection: a result obtained on a young plant does not apply to an adult plant. The reports below concern mainly young or medium-sized plants.

In their native range, as some growers on the Fous de palmiers forum point out, most Encephalartos experience dry winters and hot, rainy summers, the opposite of a Mediterranean climate. Species of the high-altitude grasslands of South Africa, such as Encephalartos ghellinckii in the Drakensberg, endure frost, snow and savanna fires (SANBI, PlantZAfrica). No cold-cultivation data are documented for the species of East and Central Africa, and no dated, quantified report from the United States or Australia is documented for the species discussed here.

Experience in Europe

Most dated outdoor reports come from France, whose south-east has a climate comparable to coastal California or southern Australia, and whose Atlantic coast is the closest available guide for south-west England; they remain French experience, with no exact temperature equivalent for any other place. On the Mediterranean coast and in the south-west, Encephalartos friderici-guilielmi is the species most often cited as hardy: growers report survival without damage at −10 °C (14 °F) in the ground. Encephalartos lehmannii comes through Roussillon winters in the ground without protection, with leaf damage at about −7 °C (19.4 °F). Encephalartos horridus has been followed only in a sheltered position, down to −4 °C (24.8 °F). On the Atlantic coast, Encephalartos friderici-guilielmi survived the February 2012 cold spell in Nantes, under a cloche; in southern Brittany, at Concarneau, results are more mixed and failures come mainly from rot. No reports are documented for inland or mountain areas. On the French Riviera, photos posted in 2010 on the Fous de palmiers forum show Encephalartos horridus, Encephalartos lehmannii and Encephalartos longifolius planted in the botanic garden of the Villa Thuret, at Antibes.

For Encephalartos ghellinckii and Encephalartos laevifolius, no dated and quantified report is documented. For Encephalartos cycadifolius, the only documented report concerns a failure due to excess water.

Reports published on forums. These are amateur observations: they are indicative and do not amount to a general rule. The threads on the Fous de palmiers (France) and Tropicamente (Italy) forums were re-read in full in October 2026. Only complete reports and, among those missing an element, the most useful and credible ones are included.

Encephalartos friderici-guilielmi:

  • A grower in Nantes (Loire-Atlantique, western France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his Encephalartos friderici-guilielmi, in the ground since 2004 in very free-draining soil, survived −9.5 °C (14.9 °F) in February 2012, the minimum recorded in the garden during a fortnight-long cold spell with three or four days without thaw, under a cloche: no trace of damage. He had already reported in 2007 that the plant, then young, had withstood −8 °C (17.6 °F) without protection (messages of September 2007, February and March 2012).
  • A grower in the Béarn (Pyrénées-Atlantiques, south-west France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his Encephalartos friderici-guilielmi, a small plant with a caudex of about 4 cm planted out in spring 2009, survived repeated frosts down to −10 °C (14 °F) in February 2012, with no protection at all and no defoliation, then two very wet winters (messages of February 2012 and April 2014).
  • A grower on the Côte Bleue (Bouches-du-Rhône, near Marseille) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his Encephalartos friderici-guilielmi, in the ground for its first winter, came through the long, snowy winter of 2009–2010 in good shape, with a minimum of −7 °C (19.4 °F) measured under shelter (message of February 2010).
  • A grower in Concarneau (Finistère, Brittany) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his Encephalartos friderici-guilielmi, planted out in spring 2011, had its three leaves scorched in February 2012 by −5 °C (23 °F) for a few hours; these leaves had formed before planting. He mentions no protection (message of April 2012).
  • A grower in Ravenna (Emilia-Romagna, Italy; zone 8a/8b according to him) reports on the Tropicamente forum that his potted Encephalartos friderici-guilielmi, under a terrace, against a wall and sheltered from rain, did not survive the December 2009 spell: −7 °C (19.4 °F) under the terrace (−10 °C, 14 °F, in the open), with hoar frost, snow and a daytime high of −3 °C (26.6 °F). The leaves were partly dry in January 2010; by June he had little hope left, and in 2013 he confirmed the loss of the plant (messages from December 2009 to September 2013).

Encephalartos lehmannii:

  • A grower in Roussillon (Pyrénées-Orientales, France; zone 9b according to him) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his Encephalartos lehmannii, in the ground for about twelve years and unprotected, survived several cold spells in winter 2009–2010, including −7 °C (19.4 °F) with snow: leaves only slightly reddened, then six or seven new leaves the following summer. In February 2012, with only the caudex protected by pine bark, it came through a dozen days of frost down to −5 °C (23 °F) with a very violent north-westerly wind, foliage intact. A few years earlier a frost of −7 °C had almost completely defoliated it and made it produce a second head (messages from March 2010 to February 2012).
  • A grower in Ravenna (Emilia-Romagna, Italy) reports on the Tropicamente forum that his potted Encephalartos lehmannii, in an unheated cold glasshouse, sheltered from wind and snow but with minimums little different from outside according to him, had all its leaves burnt by the December 2009 spell at −7 °C (19.4 °F), then did not survive (messages from December 2009 to August 2012).
  • A grower in Concarneau (Finistère, Brittany) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his trials with Encephalartos lehmannii and Encephalartos natalensis failed in winter 2007–2008, when he recorded −4 °C (24.8 °F): the plants had rotted by the end of the winter. He does not say whether they were in pots or in the ground (message of January 2009).

Encephalartos horridus:

  • The Roussillon grower reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his Encephalartos horridus, in the ground in a patio where the absolute minimum did not drop below −4 °C (24.8 °F) when the garden reached −7 °C (19.4 °F), has never had leaf damage in about twelve years; he mentions no protection. In 2026 he estimated that it withstands “intact around −3 °C, at most −4 °C” in dry weather. He cites no dated episode (messages of January 2011 and August 2026).
  • A grower in Ravenna (Emilia-Romagna, Italy) reports on the Tropicamente forum that his potted Encephalartos horridus, in the same cold glasshouse, had dry leaf tips after the −7 °C (19.4 °F) of December 2009.

Encephalartos cycadifolius:

  • The Roussillon grower reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his Encephalartos cycadifolius, a seedling brought from California in 1994, lost all its leaves but one after being watered heavily in his absence during summer 2009. The following winter, with −7 °C (19.4 °F) and snow, the last leaf stayed intact and the caudex firm. The plant was in the ground (messages from December 2009 to January 2011).

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In short: in Britain Encephalartos are glasshouse plants, with one documented open-air exception in the Isles of Scilly; in coastal California, South Florida and Australian gardens such as Melbourne, many species grow outdoors. A species listed by a garden in the BGCI PlantSearch database (consulted in October 2026) shows that it has been declared there at some point, not that a living plant is there today.

  • United Kingdom: at Kew, the Encephalartos altensteinii of 1775 is displayed in the Palm House (Guinness World Records). The Cambridge University Botanic Garden catalogue lists Encephalartos gratus (accession of 1958, wild origin), Encephalartos laurentianus (1970, wild origin), Encephalartos lehmannii (1959), Encephalartos natalensis (2013) and Encephalartos princeps (2018); a 2010 study at the Royal Botanic Garden Edinburgh described a collection made up mostly of southern African species. In the open air, Tresco Abbey Garden (Isles of Scilly), which describes its climate as mild and practically frost-free, showed tall plants captioned Encephalartos natalensis in its July 2026 garden diary. No other Encephalartos growing in the open ground in Britain is documented in a verifiable source.
  • United States: at Ganna Walska Lotusland (Montecito, California), three Encephalartos woodii dominate the pool of the cycad garden, among species such as Encephalartos horridus, Encephalartos trispinosus, Encephalartos lehmannii, Encephalartos heenanii, Encephalartos latifrons, Encephalartos ferox, Encephalartos lanatus and Encephalartos friderici-guilielmi; in March 2011 hand pollination there produced seeds of Encephalartos heenanii, the “first time in the United States” according to the collector quoted in the autumn 2012 newsletter. At Berkeley, a 2015 Plant Collections Network profile describes the San Francisco Bay climate as “ideal for many Encephalartos”. Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden's Garden Explorer has 35 Encephalartos entries, including Encephalartos woodii, and Fairchild gave an Encephalartos woodii to Naples Botanical Garden (Florida) in October 2022. In St. Louis, the Missouri Botanical Garden still grows an Encephalartos horridus donated in 1918 by a local collector (Deloso et al., 2025).
  • Australia: at Melbourne, the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria census places four Encephalartos woodii in three outdoor beds and thirteen Encephalartos altensteinii in twelve beds, alongside some twenty other species outdoors, from Encephalartos aemulans to Encephalartos whitelockii. The Royal Botanic Garden Sydney has 31 Encephalartos entries in its Garden Explorer (updated 1 October 2026), including two hybrids Encephalartos natalensis × woodii; Adelaide declares 29 species of Encephalartos in BGCI PlantSearch.

Toxicity and precautions

Yes, Encephalartos are poisonous, their seeds in particular. A study published in 1944 in the Onderstepoort Journal of Veterinary Science and Animal Industry was the first to demonstrate the toxicity of Encephalartos horridus (Van der Walt, 1944). Like other cycads, they contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; the seeds are the most dangerous part. In animals as in people these toxins attack the liver, the digestive system and the nervous system; the risk is well documented in other cycads, especially for dogs that chew the seeds. Traditional preparations of meal from the stem must not be copied. Wear gloves to handle the seeds, and keep plants and seeds away from children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Frequently asked questions

What does the name Encephalartos mean?

“Bread in the head”. The name combines the Greek words en (in), kephalē (head) and artos (bread), in reference to the meal some African peoples made from the stem of certain species. The Afrikaans name “broodboom”, bread tree, carries the same idea. This meal is not edible without preparation, because the plant is poisonous.

How many species are there in the genus Encephalartos?

Sixty-five, according to The World List of Cycads (version of 15 August 2026), plus six subspecies. Thirty-seven live in South Africa. Higher figures sometimes found online often add species and subspecies together, or include names that are now synonyms.

Which Encephalartos is the most cold-tolerant?

Encephalartos friderici-guilielmi, according to growers' reports. In France, some growers report survival without damage down to −10 °C (14 °F) in the ground in February 2012, in the Béarn without protection and in Nantes under a cloche. Encephalartos lehmannii comes next, with leaf damage at about −7 °C (19.4 °F) in Roussillon. In Ravenna, Italy, both species in pots and under shelter died after −7 °C in December 2009. These results concern young or medium-sized plants; a wet winter can do more harm than dry cold.

Can Encephalartos horridus stay outdoors in winter?

Only in the mildest, best-sheltered gardens. The only documented in-ground report, from France, describes a plant intact for about twelve years in a Roussillon patio, where the temperature never drops below −4 °C (24.8 °F). In Ravenna, a potted plant in a cold glasshouse had dry leaf tips after −7 °C (19.4 °F). Elsewhere, including most of the UK, grow it in a pot, in a mix that dries quickly, and bring it into a frost-free place.

Why are there only male plants of Encephalartos woodii?

Because only one plant was ever found in the wild, and it was a male. Discovered in 1895 in the Ngoye forest in KwaZulu-Natal, it was propagated from offsets before the last stem was removed around 1916. All cultivated plants are therefore clones of that male, and no female has been found despite many searches. Botanic gardens such as Kew, Lotusland and Melbourne grow it; hybrids Encephalartos natalensis × woodii also exist in collections and should not be confused with the species.

Do I need a CITES permit to buy an Encephalartos?

Yes, for any international trade, seeds included. The genus is in CITES Appendix I (Annex A of the EU Regulation), without the seed exemption that applies to Appendix II cycads; the UK, the US and Australia apply the same CITES listing through their own permit systems. In the European Union, imports from South Africa also depend on species-by-species opinions, negative for example for Encephalartos horridus. Buy only cultivated plants, with their documents.

Are Encephalartos caffer and Encephalartos afer the same plant?

Yes. Following the adoption of Article 61.6 of the Code of Nomenclature at the Madrid congress in 2024, the epithet caffer is replaced by afer. IPNI and The World List of Cycads now write Encephalartos afer, but the name Encephalartos caffer remains common in catalogues and in Species+.

Are Encephalartos poisonous?

Yes, the seeds above all. Their toxicity has been demonstrated since 1944 for Encephalartos horridus, and cycad toxins attack the liver and the nervous system. Keep seeds and plants away from children and pets, and call a poison centre or a vet if any part is swallowed.
