Zamia integrifolia, the coontie, is a small cycad with an almost always underground stem, native to Florida and south-eastern Georgia, the Bahamas, the Cayman Islands and Cuba, where it grows mainly in pinelands on sandy or limestone soils. It is the only cycad native to the continental United States and the most cold-tolerant of the cultivated species of the genus Zamia. Nurseries long sold it as Zamia floridana or Zamia pumila, names still found on labels. At the Jardin zoologique tropical in La Londe-les-Maures, on the French Riviera, a Zamia integrifolia survived −7 °C (19.4 °F) in February 2012, with damage to its leaves.

Key facts about Zamia integrifolia

  • Accepted name: Zamia integrifolia L.f. (1789), a name proposed for conservation, accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and by GBIF; POWO could not be consulted directly
  • Former names and synonyms: Zamia floridana A.DC., Zamia umbrosa Small, Zamia media Jacq., Zamia tenuis Willd., Zamia dentata Voigt, Palmifolium integrifolium (L.f.) Kuntze; often sold as Zamia pumila, which is another species
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: coontie, Florida arrowroot, Seminole bread
  • Natural range: United States (Florida, Georgia), Bahamas, Cayman Islands, Cuba (The World List of Cycads)
  • IUCN status: Near Threatened (NT), criterion A2ad, assessment published in 2022; the two varieties are not evaluated
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (the whole genus; seeds exempt under annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union; collecting from the wild is prohibited in Florida
  • Documented hardiness: USDA zones 8b to 11 and a winter minimum of about 15 °F (−9.4 °C) according to the University of Florida, 8a to 11b according to NC State Extension; on the French Riviera, survival at −7 °C (19.4 °F) with leaf damage; in Roussillon, defoliated at −7 °C under snow, then regrew (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: part shade to sun; well-drained sandy, loamy, acid or alkaline soils; high drought tolerance and good salt tolerance (University of Florida)
  • Habit and adult size: rounded clump 60 to 120 cm (2 to 4 ft) tall and 90 to 150 cm (3 to 5 ft) wide, usually on a single, largely underground stem; linear leaflets 10 to 20 cm (4 to 8 in) long (University of Florida)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Montgomery Botanical Center (Florida), 112 accessions; Royal Botanic Garden Edinburgh and Royal Botanic Garden Sydney list it in their online catalogues; Kew, Glasgow, some 35 American and five Australian gardens declare it to BGCI
  • Toxicity: all parts, especially the seeds, are poisonous (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Zamia integrifolia

The coontie forms a low, rounded clump of dark green, feather-like leaves rising from a squat stem that is usually underground or barely at soil level. The University of Florida (UF/IFAS) fact sheet describes a plant 2 to 4 ft (60 to 120 cm) tall and 3 to 5 ft (90 to 150 cm) wide, most often with a single stem, slow-growing and fine-textured.

  • Leaves: pinnate, spirally arranged, evergreen.
  • Leaflets: linear, 4 to 8 in (10 to 20 cm) long, with parallel veins and a margin rolled under and finely toothed.
  • Cones: the plant is dioecious; the elongated cones are 3 to 6 in (8 to 15 cm) long according to the same fact sheet, and the female cones release conspicuous red seeds when ripe.
  • Stem and roots: the underground stem, rich in starch, protected the plant from pineland fires; like all cycads, it forms coralloid roots that house nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria.

The species is very variable: some plants have narrow leaflets, others broader ones, and variety umbrosa is the plant of shady places.

Hybrids

No recognised natural hybrid has been documented. The coontie belongs to the Zamia pumila complex, a group of closely related species from the Caribbean and Florida that descend from a common ancestor (Salas-Leiva and colleagues, 2017) and have a complicated genetic history.

Possible confusion

  • Zamia pumila: the type species of the genus, from Cuba, the Dominican Republic and Puerto Rico. Its name was long applied to the Florida coontie and still often is in nurseries; the University of Florida fact sheet points out that the use is erroneous. A plant labelled “Zamia pumila” in the trade may therefore be a coontie.
  • Zamia floridana: not another species but a synonym of Zamia integrifolia, still used by nurseries and growers; the University of Florida fact sheet is published under this name.
  • Zamia angustifolia, from the Bahamas (Eleuthera) and eastern Cuba, and Zamia lucayana, from the Bahamas: two other accepted species of the Zamia pumila complex, the first with narrow leaflets; provenance helps to tell them apart. Contrary to what is sometimes written, the Cuban populations of Zamia angustifolia are not included in Zamia integrifolia.
  • Zamia furfuracea, the cardboard palm of garden centres: broad, thick leaflets covered in a scurfy down, very different from the narrow, smooth leaflets of the coontie. NC State Extension also lists Cycas revoluta and Zamioculcas zamiifolia among the plants it is often confused with.

To identify a plant, look at the number and width of the leaflets, and above all find out where it came from.

Taxonomy

Zamia integrifolia was published in 1789 by the younger Linnaeus in William Aiton’s Hortus Kewensis (volume 3, page 478); the type is a Florida plant cultivated at Kew (W. Aiton s.n., holotype at the Natural History Museum in London, BM). The name has been proposed for conservation with a conserved type (Stevenson and Reveal, 2011), a proposal recommended by the Nomenclature Committee for Vascular Plants (Applequist, 2012), according to The World List of Cycads.

In practice, Zamia floridana A.DC. (1868) and Zamia umbrosa Small (1921), names widely used in the twentieth century, are synonyms. The World List of Cycads also places in synonymy Zamia media Jacq. (1798), Zamia tenuis Willd. (1806), Zamia dentata Voigt (1828) and their combinations in the genus Palmifolium.

Varieties

Two varieties are accepted by The World List of Cycads since 2016: Zamia integrifolia var. integrifolia and Zamia integrifolia var. umbrosa (Small) D.B.Ward, named after the shady places where it grows. A genetic study of 25 populations from the Florida peninsula, using 26 microsatellite markers, came to the same conclusion: the populations of north-eastern Florida that correspond to var. umbrosa are genetically distinct and cohesive, and the authors recognise only these two varieties in Florida (Griffith and colleagues, 2022). The sources disagree here: NC State Extension still lists five varieties (integrifolia, broomei, floridana, sylvicola and umbrosa), distinguished by their leaflets, habitats and ranges.

The Zamia pumila complex

The coontie is part of the Zamia pumila complex, a group of Caribbean and Florida Zamia that descend from a common ancestor (Salas-Leiva and colleagues, 2017). The World List of Cycads now recognises eight species in it, including Zamia integrifolia, Zamia pumila, Zamia angustifolia and Zamia lucayana. The same 2022 study found that the genetic diversity of Florida’s Zamia is reduced compared with closely related Caribbean populations, in line with the history of commercial exploitation and the recent geological history of the Florida peninsula.

Etymology and history

The epithet integrifolia, from the Latin integer, “entire”, and folium, “leaf”, apparently refers to leaves without prickles or teeth (Whitelock, 2002, as quoted by Haynes, 2022). According to the University of Florida extension service, the English name coontie comes from a Seminole expression, “conti hateka”, meaning “white root” or “white bread”.

The coontie’s history is bound up with its starch. According to the University of Florida fact sheet (Culbert, 2010), sixteenth-century Spanish writings report that the Timucua and the Calusa removed the toxin from the stem by grinding and washing it, then made bread from the starch. The Seminoles adopted the technique, which settlers in the Fort Lauderdale area learned from them around 1825. In the 1880s mills were operating in Miami, and during the First World War one of them processed up to 18 tons of coontie a day for the army. This massive harvest greatly reduced wild coontie. The genetic study of 2022 goes further back: the pattern of genetic and geographical diversity matches the exchange networks of Native peoples of the Woodland period (1000 BC to AD 1000), not natural dispersal, which suggests that people helped to spread the plant (Griffith and colleagues, 2022).

Common names

In American English the plant is called coontie (from the Seminole, see above) and also “Seminole bread”; “Florida arrowroot”, a name NC State Extension still gives for the plant, was the trade name of the starch extracted from the stem, recorded around 1911. Jungle Music, a Californian nursery, also cites the spellings “coonti” and “koonti”. In French no name is attested.

In the wild

The World List of Cycads gives the species for the United States (Florida and Georgia), the Bahamas, the Cayman Islands and Cuba. It is the only cycad that reaches the continental United States. A University of Florida fact sheet of 2010 states, on the contrary, that none of the native Florida species is known in the West Indies: the two sources disagree, and this page follows The World List of Cycads, the reference for distribution.

In Florida the coontie grows in pine-dominated habitats, on well-drained sandy or loamy soils. It feeds the caterpillars of a small black and red butterfly, the Atala (Eumaeus atala), whose only native larval host plant it is according to the Florida Museum of Natural History: the caterpillars store the plant’s toxins, which makes them distasteful to predators. The Atala almost disappeared from Florida when the coontie was harvested for its starch; thought to be extinct, it was rediscovered in 1979 on a barrier island in Miami-Dade County, and today it lives mainly in gardens, nurseries and botanic gardens where cycads are grown, and is almost absent from its historic pine rockland habitat.

The coontie is pollinated by two native beetles, the weevil Rhopalotria slossoni and Pharaxonotha floridana, which live and feed in the male cones (Simon and colleagues, 2023). Norstog and colleagues (1992), writing of the Florida plant under the name Zamia pumila, already named the same weevil and a beetle then called Pharaxonotha zamiae, and described plants and insects as dependent on each other.

Conservation status and regulation

Zamia integrifolia is listed as Near Threatened (NT) on the IUCN Red List, under criterion A2ad, in the assessment by J.D. Bösenberg published in 2022 and cited by The World List of Cycads; GBIF gives the same category (the Red List itself could not be consulted). The two varieties are not evaluated. In Florida, collecting from the wild is prohibited: the species is on the state list of commercially exploited plants, and plants sold for gardens come from nurseries (Culbert, 2010). Griffith and colleagues (2022) describe a species brought close to extirpation about a hundred years ago and make management recommendations based on their genetic data.

Like all Zamia except Zamia restrepoi, Zamia integrifolia falls under CITES Appendix II, where the genus has been listed since 4 February 1977. The listing carries annotation #4 (version adopted at CoP20, in force on 5 March 2026): seeds, pollen, seedlings and tissue cultures obtained in vitro and transported in sterile containers, and cut flowers of artificially propagated plants, are not covered by CITES. International trade in the plants themselves, on the other hand, requires an export permit. In the European Union the species falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, whose current annexes are set by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026.

This general information does not replace the official texts: check the regulations in force before buying or exchanging plants (databases consulted in October 2026), ask the seller for proof of origin and prefer nursery-raised plants.

Growing Zamia integrifolia

This is the Zamia best suited to an outdoor trial in a mild climate, but it is not a hardy plant. In its home region the University of Florida rates it an outstanding landscape plant, for borders, mass planting, specimen use or containers, and notes that it attracts butterflies and can be grown indoors.

  • Light: according to the University of Florida, it grows best with some shade but tolerates full sun. A plant raised under glass or in shade scorches if moved suddenly into full sun: a grower in southern Vendée (France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that a nursery plant set out in full sun in March 2017 was “completely scorched” within three weeks. Acclimatise it over a few weeks before planting.
  • Soil: always well drained; the University of Florida says it tolerates sandy, loamy, acid or alkaline soils. The French nursery Cycadales.eu, for the Caribbean group as a whole, advises a mineral, free-draining compost containing at least some lime, these species tolerating acid conditions rather poorly.
  • Watering: once established it is very drought-tolerant and should be watered in moderation, if at all; after planting, on the other hand, it needs plenty of water (University of Florida). Too much water causes disease, especially in winter.
  • Coastal gardens: salt tolerance is rated good by the University of Florida, and NC State Extension says it tolerates salt spray.
  • Planting: on the Fous de palmiers forum, growers advise planting the stem deep, so that the growing point survives in the soil even if the leaves are frozen.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

In the south-eastern United States it is a common landscape plant: the University of Florida gives planting all year round in zones 8 to 11, and NC State Extension, in North Carolina, places it in zones 8a to 11b, in the coastal region of the state. Outside the United States, documented results in the ground come from the Mediterranean coast of France (see Cold hardiness). This Mediterranean experience may be of use to readers in comparable climates, such as coastal California or southern Australia, where no published outdoor result has been found; in the UK no outdoor trial has been reported.

Container cultivation

In a pot the coontie lives for a long time thanks to its slow growth. NC State Extension advises that, north of its native range, it can be grown as a house or conservatory plant in a generous pot and stood outside in dappled shade in summer. For the Caribbean group, Cycadales.eu recommends winter temperatures of 1 to 10 °C (33.8 to 50 °F), rising to 15 to 20 °C (59 to 68 °F) by day, with a compost kept fairly dry but not parched, and summer temperatures of 15 to 20 °C (59 to 68 °F) at night and 25 to 30 °C (77 to 86 °F) by day for at least four to six months of the year; without such a warm season, the nursery warns, these tropical plants decline. An unheated shelter is not enough in a cold winter, as the cases from Chimay and Ravenna described under Cold hardiness show.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In short: one of the most widely held Zamia in botanic gardens, under glass in Britain, in the ground in Florida.

  • United States: Montgomery Botanical Center, in Coral Gables (Florida), lists 112 accessions in its online catalogue, most of them from 2005 and 2006 according to the garden’s numbering (consulted on 24 October 2026). Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden lists it in its online Garden Explorer (inventory of 19 October 2026). About 35 American gardens declare it to BGCI, in Florida (among them Marie Selby, Pinecrest Gardens, Miami Beach Botanical Garden and Key West), California (the Huntington, Lotusland, San Diego Botanic Garden, San Diego Zoo, UC Berkeley), Hawaii and the north-east (New York Botanical Garden, Brooklyn, the Arnold Arboretum, Phipps Conservatory); Naples Botanical Garden and the United States Botanic Garden in Washington declare it under separate entries. The sources consulted do not say which of these plants grow outdoors.
  • United Kingdom: the Royal Botanic Garden Edinburgh lists it in its online Garden Explorer (updated 18 October 2026); Kew, Glasgow Botanic Gardens and the Harris Garden of the University of Reading declare it to BGCI. These are glasshouse collections.
  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney lists it in its Garden Explorer (inventory of 19 October 2026); Brisbane Botanic Gardens Mount Coot-tha, Noosa Botanic Gardens, Cairns Botanic Gardens and the System Garden of the University of Melbourne declare it to BGCI.

A BGCI declaration shows that a garden held the plant at some point, not that it is alive today. The British nursery Trebrown gives it a USDA zone 9a rating, its own commercial classification.

Propagation

The coontie is propagated from seed and sometimes by dividing clumps or offsets; NC State Extension notes that pieces of the fleshy underground stem can be used to start new plants. The sarcotesta, the fleshy coat of the seed, contains germination inhibitors and must be removed, wearing gloves; germination is slow, and a nursery plant takes about five years to reach a saleable size (Culbert, 2010). The University of Florida FPS-617 fact sheet judges propagation from seed “usually difficult” and growth very slow. To obtain seed, a male and a female plant are needed, and in cultivation hand pollination.

Two growers’ observations complete these data. A grower in Ravenna (Emilia-Romagna, Italy) reports on the Tropicamente forum, in May 2009, that his seeds germinated easily indoors in winter at 20 °C (68 °F). A grower whose profile gives Belley (Ain) and Montpellier (Hérault) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that, in his glasshouse, temperatures of 45 to 50 °C (113 to 122 °F) in summer 2009 caused the ovules in the cones of Zamia integrifolia to die.

Pests and diseases

According to the University of Florida, the main problems are scale insects, in particular Florida red scale (Chrysomphalus aonidum), which can be fatal if not controlled, mealybugs and the sooty mould that follows them; no disease is of major concern. In Florida, Atala caterpillars strip the foliage for about two weeks, then the plant grows new leaves; NC State Extension adds that the plants usually recover well. Outside its native climate, the main risk is stem rot in cold, waterlogged soil in winter.

Cold hardiness

Zamia integrifolia is the most cold-tolerant of the cultivated Zamia, but it is not hardy: according to a grower in Roussillon, the foliage is damaged below about −5 °C (23 °F), and the underground stem, if it stays sound, can regrow leaves after defoliation. Cycadales.eu says much the same: leaf damage from −5 °C, with survival at much lower temperatures possible after defoliation.

Mediterranean and European experience

The documented outdoor results all come from the Mediterranean coast of France:

  • At the Jardin zoologique tropical in La Londe-les-Maures (Var, France), a Zamia integrifolia survived −7 °C (19.4 °F) in February 2012, with damage to its leaves.
  • A grower in Roussillon (Pyrénées-Orientales, France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his plant, in the ground for about ten years, has its foliage damaged below −5 °C (23 °F) but did not lose it at −5 °C in winter 2010–2011; in another winter, not dated in his messages, it lost all its leaves at −7 °C (19.4 °F) under snow, then regrew them at the end of the following spring. Another plant, in a pot under the eaves, withstood −4 °C (24.8 °F) (message of March 2012). He advises waiting until spring before cutting off frozen leaves.

Two failures show the limits under cover in colder climates:

  • A grower in Ravenna (Emilia-Romagna, Italy) reports on the Tropicamente forum that his plant, kept in an unheated cold glasshouse, died straight after the −7 °C (19.4 °F) frost of winter 2009–2010.
  • A grower in Chimay (Hainaut province, Belgium) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his plant, wintered in a pot in a garden shed, had 95 % of its foliage scorched at −6 °C (21.2 °F) after several days without thaw (February 2010), then died in March 2010.

No result was found for Corsica, the French Atlantic coast, inland or mountain areas. In the ground, the coontie is therefore a reasonable trial only where frosts are rare and brief, in very free-draining soil and with the stem well buried; elsewhere, grow it in a pot and give it shelter in winter.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

In the United States, the University of Florida places the coontie in USDA zones 8b to 11 and states that it should withstand a winter minimum of 15 °F (−9.4 °C); NC State Extension gives zones 8a to 11b. None of the sources consulted compares the hardiness of the different Florida populations. No dated outdoor result was found for the UK or Australia.

Toxicity and precautions

Yes, the coontie is poisonous, especially its seeds. Stem and seeds contain cycasin, the toxin the peoples of Florida removed by long washing before eating the starch; Zamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, harmful to the liver and the nervous system. For this species NC State Extension lists cycasin, BMAA and an unidentified neurotoxin (in cattle), names the sap, seeds and stems as the poisonous parts and describes vomiting (sometimes bloody), bloody diarrhoea, jaundice, liver failure and death, adding that one or two seeds can be fatal. The ASPCA lists the closely related Zamia furfuracea as toxic to dogs, cats and horses. The red seeds attract children and animals: pick them up, wear gloves to clean them and never try to prepare the starch. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet straight away.

Traditional uses

The coontie was an important source of starch in Florida, first for the Timucua, the Calusa and then the Seminoles, later for a real industry in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries (see Etymology and history). According to the University of Florida fact sheet, the starch content of the plant varied from 8 % in summer to 20 % in winter. Today the coontie is a widely used ornamental in Florida gardens, not least to attract the Atala butterfly.

Frequently asked questions

Are Zamia integrifolia, Zamia floridana and Zamia pumila the same plant?

Partly. Zamia floridana is a synonym of Zamia integrifolia, the Florida coontie. Zamia pumila, on the other hand, is a separate species from the Greater Antilles, but its name is still often given to the coontie in the trade.

How cold-hardy is the coontie?

To a point. American extension services place it in USDA zones 8b to 11 (University of Florida) or 8a to 11b (NC State). In practice the foliage is damaged below about −5 °C (23 °F); on the French Riviera a plant survived −7 °C (19.4 °F) with leaf damage, and in Roussillon a plant defoliated at −7 °C regrew, while in Ravenna a plant in an unheated glasshouse died at −7 °C.

Can I grow a coontie outside in the UK?

No outdoor trial has been reported. It is safer to grow it in a pot, stood outside in summer and brought into a frost-free, bright place in winter, as NC State Extension advises north of its native range.

Why did its leaves turn brown after planting?

Often because of sunburn. A plant raised under glass scorches if moved suddenly into full sun: acclimatise it gradually.

Is the coontie poisonous to dogs and cats?

Yes. All parts, especially the seeds, are poisonous; one or two seeds can be fatal, according to NC State Extension. Keep them out of reach of children, dogs and cats.

What is the Atala butterfly?

A small Florida butterfly whose caterpillar lives on the coontie and stores its toxins. It almost disappeared with the exploitation of the coontie and now lives mainly in gardens where the plant is grown.

Is it easy to propagate?

Slowly. Sow the seed after removing the flesh; germination is slow and it takes about five years to raise a plant of saleable size. Pieces of the underground stem can also be used.
