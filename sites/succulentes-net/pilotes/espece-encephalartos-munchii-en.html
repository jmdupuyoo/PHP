Encephalartos munchii is a cycad of central Mozambique, known from a single site in the world: Mount Zembe, a granite inselberg in Manica province, south-west of Chimoio. This species of the genus Encephalartos forms clumps of trunks about 1 m tall, crowned with dull glaucous green leaves whose leaflets overlap in a V, and bears jade-green cones. Described in 1969 by R. Allen Dyer and Inez Verdoorn, who separated it from the Encephalartos manikensis complex, it is Critically Endangered: the 2003 IUCN action plan counted only 17 plants in the wild. In cultivation it is a fast-growing, frost-tender cycad for tropical and subtropical gardens, and the two reports from Europe are failures in light frost.

Key facts about Encephalartos munchii

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos munchii R.A.Dyer & I.Verd. (1969), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and GBIF
  • Former names and synonyms: no synonym; the Mount Zembe plants were previously included in a broadly defined Encephalartos manikensis, and the name “munchii” had been written on herbarium sheets of at least three different species (Dyer and Verdoorn, 1969)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Munch's cycad (Species+)
  • Natural range: Mozambique, Manica province, Mount Zembe (Macate district), the only known site
  • IUCN status: Critically Endangered (CR B1ab(iii,v)+2ab(iii,v)), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I since 1977, like the whole genus; Annex A in the European Union; Alliance for Zero Extinction site, with no legal protection in Mozambique
  • Documented hardiness: no measured value published; two unfavourable cultivation reports (Agen, France, −4 °C (24.8 °F); Apulia, Italy, −1 °C (30.2 °F)), see Cold hardiness
  • Light and soil: in the wild, scrub along streams and among granite rocks and boulders near the summit of Mount Zembe; in southern California, full or partial sun on the coast and inland, filtered light in desert areas, and good drainage (Jungle Music)
  • Habit and adult size: stems branching from the base, up to about 1 m tall and 30 cm across; leaves about 1 m; male cones up to about 65 cm (Dyer and Verdoorn, 1969)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Phipps Conservatory (Pittsburgh), Fairchild, the Huntington, Lotusland, the Los Angeles County Arboretum and five other gardens in the United States; Royal Botanic Garden Sydney, Brisbane, Adelaide and the University of Melbourne in Australia (BGCI); no British garden declares it
  • Toxicity: not studied for the species; seeds toxic in other Encephalartos (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos munchii

Look for dull glaucous green, fairly thin leaflets, crowded and raised in a V on either side of the rachis, and above all long, loose male cones whose spreading scales show the axis. The original description by Dyer and Verdoorn (1969), read in full, gives the following characters:

  • Stems: stout, branching from the base, up to about 1 m tall and 30 cm across, covered with alternating series of leaf bases and whitish woolly scale leaves
  • Leaves: about 1 m or slightly more, linear to narrowly oblong; rachis almost straight and erect, spreading then recurving with age; bare base 15 to 20 cm, more or less spiny; basal swelling covered with thick off-white wool
  • Leaflets: dull glaucous green, fairly thin, turning yellowish brown with age, inserted about 1 cm apart, crowded and overlapping, especially in the upper half, pointing towards the tip in a V; the median ones linear-oblong, about 15 cm by 2 cm, bearing on both margins, mainly in the lower half, a few to several spines, some longer than 5 mm; the lower ones reduced to prickles
  • Male cones: jade green, up to six at a time, subcylindrical, up to about 65 cm by 9 cm, on a smooth stalk up to 20 cm long; scales spreading and showing the axis
  • Female cones: jade green, subcylindrical or barrel-shaped, up to about 50 cm by 20 cm, on a stalk of about 10 cm surrounded by narrow woolly scale leaves about 15 cm long; on each scale, the upper face extends into a broad central lobe as long as the two lateral lobes
  • Seeds: scarlet, 4 to 4.5 cm by 2.5 to 2.75 cm

Dyer and Verdoorn stress that in this group leaf characters vary a great deal and do not allow a reliable diagnosis: species are separated mainly on the male cones. A young plant without cones cannot be identified with certainty. Jungle Music, a nursery in southern California, describes a leaf colour that varies from olive green to bright blue in cultivated plants, a blue-green male cone and a green female cone with a bluish tinge, and many basal suckers; it calls munchii the only member of the complex that can be blue.

Hybrids

No natural or garden hybrid of Encephalartos munchii is documented. In collections, cross-pollination with other Encephalartos remains possible: to obtain pure seed, female cones must be isolated and the origin of the pollen controlled.

Possible confusion

Encephalartos munchii belongs to the complex of Encephalartos manikensis, a group of cycads from Mozambique and Zimbabwe long treated as a single species (by Melville in 1957 and by Lewis in Flora Zambesiaca in 1960). In 1969 Dyer and Verdoorn separated four species from it: Encephalartos pterogonus, Encephalartos concinnus, Encephalartos chimanimaniensis and Encephalartos munchii. According to them, Encephalartos munchii differs by three characters:

  • dark green, glaucous leaflets, overlapping and raised in a V on the rachis, less rigid than in the related species, some turning brown after maturity;
  • long, loose male cones whose scales show the axis, whereas they are compact in Encephalartos manikensis and Encephalartos pterogonus;
  • on the scales of the female cone, a broad central lobe extending the upper face, as long as the two lateral lobes.

The loose male cones bring Encephalartos munchii close to Encephalartos chimanimaniensis, but the scales of the latter are thicker and end in a recurved swelling shaped like a duck's bill, whereas those of Encephalartos munchii are thin and spread almost at right angles (key of Dyer and Verdoorn).

Taxonomy

Encephalartos munchii is an accepted name in The World List of Cycads and GBIF. R. Allen Dyer and Inez Verdoorn published it in 1969 in Kirkia (volume 7, pp. 147–158; description pp. 155–157, figures 16 to 20), in the paper “Encephalartos manikensis and its near allies”. Their work was based on plants grown in the large rock garden of R.C. Munch at Mona farm near Rusape (then Rhodesia, now Zimbabwe), and on herbarium sheets: the authors state that they saw no plant in the wild.

The type is Munch's collection 452, from “Zembe Mountain south of Vila Pery” (Vila Pery is the former name of Chimoio). The sources differ on the herbarium holding the holotype: the original description places it in Salisbury, now Harare (SRGH), with a duplicate in Pretoria (PRE), whereas The World List of Cycads gives the holotype in Pretoria and the isotype in Harare. The difference is unresolved.

The species has never changed name, but the name itself has a history: before 1969, “munchii” had been written on herbarium sheets corresponding to at least three different plants, and Dyer and Verdoorn asked that it be used only in the sense they gave it. The complex itself bears the trace of an earlier placement: Encephalartos manikensis was first described in 1938 as a variety of Encephalartos gratus.

Etymology and history

The epithet munchii honours Raymond C. Munch (1901–1985), of Rusape, a cycad collector and observer of the Encephalartos manikensis complex in Zimbabwe and Mozambique (Haynes, 2022, cited by The World List of Cycads). It was at his initiative that Dyer and Verdoorn took up the study of the group again in 1966. The collector H. Basil Christian, who had bequeathed his garden “Ewanrigg” to the Rhodesian government, had already planned to give Munch's name to the form most distant from typical Encephalartos manikensis; the authors respected this wish.

Munch had found only a limited and fairly uniform colony on Mount Zembe. The original description also records the first known coning: in Munch's garden, an 18-year-old plant produced its first cones, six male cones, in 1967; the longest measured 66 cm when it reached the Botanical Research Institute in Pretoria on 15 February 1967, and 58 cm once dry. GBIF also lists four herbarium specimens collected in Mozambique in 1973 by Manuel Fidalgo de Carvalho (Coimbra and Missouri herbaria), three of them labelled “Vila Pery, Monte Zembe”.

Common names

Species+ gives the English name Munch's cycad, also used by the Phipps Conservatory in Pittsburgh. No Portuguese name or name in a local language is attested in a verifiable source.

In the wild

Encephalartos munchii is known only from Mount Zembe, in Macate district, Manica province, 22 km south-west of Chimoio. According to the Tropical Important Plant Area fact sheet prepared by the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew (Osborne and Darbyshire), Mount Zembe is a granite inselberg about 6 km by 2 km, rising to 1,200 m above a plain at about 600 m. The species is largely confined to the summit, in scrub along streams and among rocks and boulders, where it is rated “frequent”. The site combines rocks with thin soil, grassland on the flats and small pockets of moist forest in the gullies. Another plant found only here, Aloe decurva, is also Critically Endangered.

No published data describe the pollination or phenology of the species in the wild. An Italian collector published photographs of plants in habitat on the Tropicamente forum in 2014, placing them between 900 and 1,200 m, some twenty kilometres from Chimoio. Jungle Music speaks of rocky granite grassland slopes mixed with evergreen forest and scrub, a hot, wet summer and a cool, dry winter, and mentions populations “described in Zimbabwe”; no botanical source confirms the species outside Mount Zembe.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos munchii is Critically Endangered (CR). The World List of Cycads cites the IUCN Red List assessment published in 2022 (J.D. Bösenberg), under criteria B1ab(iii,v)+2ab(iii,v): a tiny extent of occurrence and area of occupancy, a single location, and a continuing decline in habitat quality and in the number of mature individuals. GBIF gives the same category; an earlier assessment, by J.S. Donaldson, dates from 2010.

The IUCN action plan for cycads (Donaldson, 2003) already listed the species as Critically Endangered, under criteria A2d; B1ab(ii,iv,v); C1+2a(i); D, with a population estimated at only 17 plants, a strong decline, overcollecting and reproductive failure as threats, and no population in a reserve. The species was then held in at least one general collection and one gene bank, but the plan counted it among those whose gene bank needed strengthening.

Kew's fact sheet on Mount Zembe reviews the threats: the site is not protected, but it is listed as an Alliance for Zero Extinction site because of Encephalartos munchii; increasing fire frequency is the main threat, especially to young plants; stone quarrying at the foot of the mountain could spread; and collecting remains a risk. A reintroduction programme began in 2003, with 1,000 to 1,300 seedlings planted by the Plantas de Moçambique project (Capela, 2006, cited by Kew); no follow-up has been published, but a survey was planned as part of a University of Kent project.

Regulation, according to Species+ (consulted through its public API on 26 October 2026):

  • CITES: Appendix I since 4 February 1977, like the whole genus;
  • European Union: Annex A, current list in Regulation (EU) 2026/1383; negative opinion of the EU Scientific Review Group on artificially propagated plants from South Africa, current since 7 February 2013;
  • Mozambique: past suspensions of trade in Zamiaceae, no longer in force: CITES suspended all exports from 2012 to 2019 (lifted by Notification 2019/027), and the EU, by decisions taken from 2008 to 2017, imports of wild specimens.

These notes do not replace the official texts: check Species+ and EUR-Lex before any purchase or import, and ask for documents proving that a plant was propagated in cultivation.

Growing Encephalartos munchii

Encephalartos munchii is rare in cultivation, and no botanical source gives growing rules for the species. Its habitat, rocks and granite boulders along streams, at altitude in the tropics, suggests a free-draining substrate and good light. The only practical advice comes from a specialist nursery, Jungle Music, in southern California.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

Jungle Music describes Encephalartos munchii as a fast-growing species that can push two flushes of leaves in a season in ideal conditions, suited to full or partial sun on the coast and inland, and to partial sun or filtered light in desert areas; like many Central African species, it is said to tolerate water but to need good drainage. The plant does not grow tall but becomes wide, with a full crown of spreading leaves, and the nursery advises giving it room. These indications come from a nursery in a frost-free or almost frost-free climate: outdoor planting is only realistic where frost is absent (see Cold hardiness).

Container cultivation

In Europe, the species is grown in pots by collectors. A collector in the Salento (Apulia, southern Italy) showed new leaves on his Encephalartos munchii on the Tropicamente forum in October 2009. A deep pot, a gritty, free-draining mix, generous watering in the growing season and a bright, frost-free place in winter follow from the habitat and from the reports below.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

BGCI PlantSearch lists 23 institutions holding the species. In the United States they are the Phipps Conservatory and Botanical Gardens in Pittsburgh, which presents its coning Encephalartos munchii on its Facebook page as one of its oldest and rarest plants; Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden in Florida; the Huntington, Ganna Walska Lotusland, the Los Angeles County Arboretum, the San Diego Botanic Garden and the two gardens of San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance in California; and the Honolulu Botanical Gardens and the University of Hawaii at Hilo. In Australia, the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney lists the species in its online catalogue, and BGCI adds the Brisbane Botanic Gardens Mount Coot-tha, the Botanic Garden of Adelaide and the System Garden of the University of Melbourne. No British garden declares the species, and the Montgomery Botanical Center has no accession. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not a living plant on display today. In the trade, Jungle Music classes it as a fairly expensive cycad, usually available in small sizes. In Italy, its presence at the Florence botanic garden (Giardino dei Semplici) is attested only by the English Wikipedia, in an undated list of the garden's plants.

Propagation

Like other Encephalartos, Encephalartos munchii is propagated from seed, which requires plants of both sexes and, in collections, hand pollination; the plant, branching from the base, also produces suckers that can be separated. No protocol specific to the species has been published. The original description gives an idea of how long the first coning takes: a cultivated plant produced its first cones only at 18 years. The seedlings of the 2003 reintroduction programme show that seed propagation is practised in Mozambique. Seeds, like plants, are covered by CITES Appendix I.

Pests and diseases

No disease or pest specific to Encephalartos munchii has been described. As with other Encephalartos, the main risk in cultivation is rot of the roots and trunk in a substrate that stays wet, especially in cool weather.

Cold hardiness

Encephalartos munchii is frost-tender: the only two cultivation reports are failures, at −4 °C (24.8 °F) and even at −1 °C (30.2 °F). No measured value has been published for the species.

Mediterranean and European experience

On the French forum Fous de palmiers, a grower in Agen (Lot-et-Garonne, south-west France) reports in January 2011 that, in the same frost at −4 °C, his Encephalartos altensteinii was completely defoliated and then recovered, whereas his Encephalartos lebomboensis, Encephalartos munchii and Encephalartos natalensis died. He gives neither the date of the frost nor the growing method, but writes in the same message that he has almost no experience of planting out apart from Cycas revoluta, Dioon edule and Encephalartos friderici-guilielmi: the plant was therefore very probably in a pot, which is a deduction. A collector in the Salento reports on the Tropicamente forum, in December 2009, that a single night at −1 °C, between two days close to 20 °C (68 °F), caused “quite a lot of damage” to several Encephalartos in active growth, including Encephalartos munchii. In December 2008 the same grower placed Encephalartos munchii, with Encephalartos concinnus, Encephalartos chimanimaniensis and Encephalartos pterogonus, among the species that in his experience have “no chance” of surviving a frost, even as adults. The Jardin zoologique tropical at La Londe-les-Maures has published no observation on this species. For Mediterranean growers, and for those in comparable climates such as coastal California or southern Australia, these reports point to a pot kept frost-free in winter.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No measured value has been published for these countries. Most of the gardens that declare the species are in frost-free or almost frost-free climates (Florida, Hawaii, coastal southern California, Brisbane), and Phipps grows its plant in a conservatory; whether the plants in Sydney, Adelaide and Melbourne grow outdoors is not stated, and Jungle Music gives no temperature. No USDA zone is documented for the species in a reliable source. Survivals in cold areas observed in other Encephalartos often concern young plants with an underground caudex, protected by the soil; they do not apply to this species with an aerial trunk.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data have been published for Encephalartos munchii. The risk is nevertheless documented in the genus: the toxicity of Encephalartos horridus was demonstrated as early as 1944 (Van der Walt), and Encephalartos contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; these substances affect the liver, the digestive tract and the nervous system, and the seeds are the most dangerous part. The ASPCA reports vomiting, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses that have eaten cycads. Treat this species as poisonous: wear gloves to handle seeds and keep plants and seeds out of reach of children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use of Encephalartos munchii is documented.

Frequently asked questions

Where does Encephalartos munchii grow in the wild?

Only on Mount Zembe, a granite inselberg in Manica province, Mozambique, 22 km south-west of Chimoio. The plants grow mainly near the summit, which rises to 1,200 m, among rocks and along streams.

How many plants are left in the wild?

Nobody knows precisely. The 2003 IUCN action plan put the population at 17 plants; 1,000 to 1,300 seedlings were planted on the site in 2003, but their fate has not been published.

How can it be told from Encephalartos manikensis?

Mainly by the male cones, long and loose with scales that show the axis, whereas they are compact in Encephalartos manikensis. The glaucous, thin leaflets raised in a V also help, but leaf characters vary too much to be enough.

Is Encephalartos munchii blue?

Sometimes. The original description calls the leaflets dull glaucous green; Jungle Music reports that cultivated plants range from olive green to blue, especially in sun, and calls it the only member of the manikensis complex that can be blue.

Where does the name come from?

From Raymond C. Munch (1901–1985), a collector at Rusape, Zimbabwe, who grew the Mount Zembe plants and prompted botanists to study the Encephalartos manikensis group.

Is Encephalartos munchii frost-hardy?

No. A grower in Agen lost his plant at −4 °C (24.8 °F), and an Italian collector reports damage at −1 °C (30.2 °F). In frost-prone climates it must be grown in a pot and kept frost-free.

Can it be bought legally?

Yes, but only plants propagated in cultivation and accompanied by CITES and, in Europe, EU documents. The species is in CITES Appendix I and EU Annex A, and the EU maintains a negative opinion on artificially propagated plants from South Africa.
