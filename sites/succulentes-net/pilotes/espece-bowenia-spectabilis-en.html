Bowenia spectabilis, the zamia fern, is a cycad with no visible trunk from north-eastern Queensland, Australia, where it grows in and around rainforest on the coast and ranges, mainly between Cardwell and Cooktown, with outlying populations on Cape York Peninsula. It is the type species of the genus Bowenia, the only living cycads whose leaves are divided twice, and was described in 1863 by Joseph Dalton Hooker from a young plant sent to Kew. Its large, glossy fronds usually have leaflets with smooth margins, which separates it from the other species of the genus. It is classed as Least Concern by the IUCN.

Key facts about Bowenia spectabilis

  • Accepted name: Bowenia spectabilis Hook. ex Hook.f. (1863), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15), the World Checklist of Vascular Plants and the Australian Plant Census
  • Former names and synonyms: no former name in use; homotypic synonym Bowenia spectabilis var. spectabilis (W.Bull, 1878); the Australian Plant Census places the informal phrase names Bowenia sp. Tinaroo, Bowenia sp. (Tinaroo) and Bowenia sp. (Mt. Haig) in its synonymy
  • Family: Zamiaceae according to The World List of Cycads and the World Checklist of Vascular Plants; Stangeriaceae in CITES nomenclature (Species+) and in the Flora of Australia of 1998
  • Common names: zamia fern
  • Natural range: north-eastern Queensland, Australia, from Cape Melville and the McIlwraith Range (Cape York Peninsula) to the Cardwell area
  • IUCN status: Least Concern (LC), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated 1 April 2021 and published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union; “special least concern” in Queensland (Nature Conservation (Animals/Plants) Regulation 2020); not listed under the federal EPBC Act
  • Documented hardiness: no measured value; frost tolerance described as “light” by a seed nursery (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: semi-shade to shade; a moist, free-draining soil
  • Habit and adult size: underground stem up to 10 cm across, with 1 to 5 short branches; 1 to 7 leaves per crown, 1 to 2 m long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: declared to BGCI by 3 gardens in the United Kingdom, 13 in the United States and 11 in Australia (see In cultivation)
  • Toxicity: associated with field cases of poisoning in cattle; treat the whole plant, and above all the seeds, as toxic (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Bowenia spectabilis

It is recognised by its large, erect, dark green and glossy fronds rising straight from the ground, each divided into several branches (pinnae) carrying broad leaflets with smooth margins, and by its small underground stem with few crowns. The Flora of Australia (1998) and the Queensland government’s WildNet profile give the following characters.

  • Stem: underground, elongate, up to 10 cm across, with a long tuberous taproot; it divides into 1 to 5 short, slender branches that carry the leaves and cones. Chamberlain (1912) describes it as somewhat carrot-shaped, with one or two, sometimes four or five, branches at the top; Hooker (1863) noted the large lenticels on its surface.
  • Leaves: 1 to 7 per crown, erect, 1 to 2 m long and up to 1 m wide, on a petiole up to 1 m long; 4 to 10 spreading pinnae. Chamberlain saw leaves of nearly 2 m on plants in shaded places along streams, against seldom more than 1 m in the open.
  • Leaflets: 7 to 30 per pinna, obliquely lanceolate, narrowed into the axis at the base, 7 to 15 cm long and 1.5 to 4 cm wide, without a midrib; margins entire or with a few coarse tears, rarely regularly and shortly toothed. The Palm and Cycad Societies of Australia (PACSOA) give 6 to 10 cm by 2 to 3 cm.
  • Male cones: stalked, egg-shaped, up to 5 cm long and 2.5 cm across (Flora of Australia), 3 to 6 cm by 2 to 3 cm (WildNet), brownish according to the Australian Native Plants Society.
  • Female cones: stalkless, egg-shaped to globular, up to 10 cm long and 10 cm across (Flora of Australia), 10 to 12 cm by 7 to 10 cm (WildNet); a cone holds 22 to 90 seeds, depending on the size of the plant (Whitelock, 2002, cited by Asmussen, 2011).
  • Seeds: up to 32 mm long and 18 mm wide (Flora of Australia), with a pale pinkish to purplish fleshy coat (WildNet).

Plants are male or female. When the leaves were left for three days on a veranda in the tropical sun at Babinda, Chamberlain noted that they still looked almost as fresh as when cut.

Hybrids

No hybrid, natural or garden-raised, is documented in the botanical literature. The two species of the genus grow several hundred kilometres apart and each has its own pollinating weevil.

Possible confusion

The main risk of confusion is with the other Bowenia; the plant is also often taken for a fern.

  • Bowenia serrulata, of the Byfield area in central-eastern Queensland: leaflets sharply and regularly toothed, a larger stem (up to 25 cm across) with 5 to 20 crowns. Toothed leaflets are not an absolute criterion, since individuals with toothed leaflets occur occasionally in most populations of Bowenia spectabilis (Flora of Australia), and in all of them according to Hill and Osborne (2001, cited by Hsiao and Oberprieler, 2022). The number of crowns and, for a cultivated plant, its origin are the best clues.
  • Stangeria, of South Africa, the other fern-like cycad: Hooker (1863) already noted that its leaves are simply pinnate, whereas those of Bowenia are divided twice.
  • Ferns: the cones are enough to identify a Bowenia as a cycad.

Taxonomy

Bowenia spectabilis Hook. ex Hook.f. is accepted by The World List of Cycads, by the World Checklist of Vascular Plants (Kew, read on GBIF) and by the Australian Plant Census. Joseph Dalton Hooker described the genus and the species in 1863 in Curtis’s Botanical Magazine, from a young living plant, dried leaves and a male cone sent to Kew; the female cone was then unknown. Plate 5398 serves as the type (lectotype, according to The World List of Cycads), and the species is the type of the genus. Its name has not changed since; only the family of the genus varies with the source (see the genus page). The name Bowenia spectabilis var. spectabilis, a synonym of the species, dates from the same 1878 publication by William Bull as the variety serrulata, now Bowenia serrulata.

The chromosome study by Kokubugata and colleagues (2000) found 2n = 18 in both species, but eight chromosomes with a median centromere in Bowenia spectabilis against ten in Bowenia serrulata. G.W. Wilson’s thesis (2004) confirmed two species, separated for millions of years by the dry Burdekin Gap, and concluded that Bowenia spectabilis is morphologically variable.

The Tinaroo and Kuranda populations

Around Kuranda and Tinaroo, on the Atherton Tableland, grow plants whose leaflets are toothed and whose stem is larger and more branched, characters that seemed intermediate between the two species (Hsiao and Oberprieler, 2022). ‘Tinaroo’ is a provisional locality name for the Tinaroo population, which some botanists have suspected of being an undescribed species (see Bowenia sp. ‘Tinaroo’): David Jones (1993, 2002) treated it as a distinct, undescribed species, and Norstog and Nicholls (1997) as a variety of Bowenia spectabilis. Wilson (2004) concluded that the shape of the leaflets and stem varies with the environment, especially temperature, and that the Kuranda and Tinaroo plants are probably ecotypes of Bowenia spectabilis; the karyotypes studied by Kokubugata and colleagues (2000, 2001) point the same way.

The weevils give a further argument: Hsiao and Oberprieler (2022) found that those of Kuranda, Tinaroo and Cooktown all belong to Miltotranes prosternalis, the pollinator of Bowenia spectabilis, and conclude that these plants are also Bowenia spectabilis. The Australian Plant Census treats the phrase names Bowenia sp. Tinaroo and Bowenia sp. (Tinaroo) as synonyms of the species, the Flora of Australia cites a specimen from near Tinaroo Dam under Bowenia spectabilis, and McKenzie (2006) includes the Tinaroo Dam population in it.

A third species in the McIlwraith Range?

The isolated population of the McIlwraith Range, on Cape York Peninsula, is separated from the populations further south by the drier lowlands of the Normanby (or Laura) Gap. Wilson (2004) regarded it only provisionally as Bowenia spectabilis, and no genetic analysis has been made of it. Its pollinating weevil is a distinct species, described in 2022 as Miltotranes wilsoni; since the ranges of the weevils match those of their host plants, Hsiao and Oberprieler suggest that this population may be a third, undescribed species of Bowenia. By October 2026, The World List of Cycads still accepted only two species.

Etymology and history

The epithet comes from the Latin spectabilis, “visually striking” or “remarkable”, referring to the striking appearance of the plant (Haynes, 2022, cited by The World List of Cycads). The genus name honours Sir George F. Bowen (1821–1899), first Governor of Queensland, from 1859 to 1868.

Hooker’s original description tells the story of its discovery. The botanist Allan Cunningham had collected a portion of a frond on the Endeavour River in 1819 and provisionally referred it to the arum family. Nothing more was known of the plant until Walter Hill, head of the Brisbane Botanic Garden, found it again at Rockingham Bay and sent a young living plant, with dried leaves and a male cone, to Kew in 1863. The genus was named, at Hill’s wish as well as Hooker’s, after the Governor of Queensland, in recognition of his encouragement of botany. Hooker compared the plant with Stangeria, which also has fern-like leaves, while noting that the leaf of Bowenia is divided twice. In the early twentieth century Chamberlain studied it in the field at Babinda, near Cairns, and towards Innisfail, where it was said to be fairly abundant; he was also told that it was abundant at Cooktown.

Common names

The English name is “zamia fern”, used by the Flora of Australia, the Australian Plant Census, the Australian Native Plants Society and PACSOA. It contains the word “fern”, although the plant is a cycad. No Aboriginal name for the plant is recorded in the sources that document its traditional use.

In the wild

Bowenia spectabilis lives in north-eastern Queensland. The Flora of Australia places it on the coast and ranges from Cardwell to Cooktown, with an outlying occurrence in the McIlwraith Range; WildNet from the McIlwraith Range to near Cardwell. According to Hsiao and Oberprieler (2022), its main range lies in the Wet Tropics bioregion, from Cape Melville in the north to Cardwell in the south, with seemingly isolated populations in Starcke National Park, north of Cooktown, and on the Atherton Tableland at Kuranda and Tinaroo.

It grows in open situations in and around rainforest, in vine forest and coastal forest, on creek banks and in sheltered places among rocks and boulders, from flat ground to steep hillsides, up to 700 m on the Atherton Tableland (WildNet); the Australian Native Plants Society also cites wet sclerophyll forest. WildNet records it on clay and alluvial soils, and particularly on sandy soils derived from metamorphic rocks and granite. Chamberlain found it most abundant in open places and clearings. The LLIFLE encyclopaedia, without citing its source, gives a population of 10,000 to 20,000 plants, considered stable, and states that the species does not grow above 800 m and seems limited by mean minimum temperature.

Pollination depends on a weevil, Miltotranes prosternalis, which breeds in the male cones, mainly from October to December, and also visits and pollinates the female cones: the plant provides food and a breeding site in return for pollination (Hsiao and Oberprieler, 2020, 2022). Female cones are rare. In three populations surveyed between 1994 and 1999, no more than 5% of the plants produced a female cone, few intact cones are found because foraging animals break them open, and the seeds ripen between April and June (Wilson, 2002, and Whitelock, 2002, cited by Asmussen, 2011). Its cones are therefore infrequent and contain fewer seeds than those of Lepidozamia hopei or Cycas media, which grow in the same region.

Conservation status and regulation

Bowenia spectabilis is not considered threatened. It is listed as Least Concern (LC) on the IUCN Red List, in an assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated 1 April 2021 and published in 2022, which replaced the 2010 assessment by K.D. Hill, also LC (The World List of Cycads, GBIF and Crossref, checked on 31 October 2026). In Queensland, WildNet lists it as “special least concern” under the Nature Conservation (Animals/Plants) Regulation 2020, as a restricted plant under the state’s wildlife management regulation, and among the species whose locality records are kept confidential. It is not listed under the federal Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act 1999 (EPBC). Hsiao and Oberprieler (2022) point out that the McIlwraith Range population and its weevil may need closer attention.

Like all Bowenia, it has been listed in CITES Appendix II since 4 February 1977, with annotation #4 in the version in force since 5 March 2026: live plants need an export permit, while seeds, pollen and seedlings or tissue cultures obtained in vitro in sterile containers are exempt. In the European Union it falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026 (Species+, checked on 31 October 2026). Check the regulations in force, and the customs and plant-health rules, before any purchase abroad, and buy seed-grown plants of known origin.

Growing Bowenia spectabilis

Bowenia spectabilis is grown like a plant of the tropical forest floor: warm, in shade or filtered light, in a soil that never dries out completely. The Australian Native Plants Society says it has proved hardy in cultivation in tropical and subtropical areas, in moist, semi-shaded or shaded positions, may also suit frost-free temperate climates, and is very slow-growing. PACSOA notes that it is less commonly grown than Bowenia serrulata.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Climate: Rare Palm Seeds says it can be grown in tropical and subtropical climates, where it prefers warm, humid conditions out of the sun. In the ground, it is only an option in frost-free climates.
  • Light: filtered sun (PACSOA); light shade in all climates, deep shade for young plants and in hot, dry climates (Rare Palm Seeds).
  • Soil: moist but free-draining (PACSOA); in the wild it grows particularly on sandy soils derived from granite or metamorphic rocks (WildNet). Standing water and cold, wet soil rot the underground stem.
  • Water and wind: high water needs and low wind tolerance (Rare Palm Seeds), with high humidity (PACSOA).

Container cultivation

PACSOA describes it as a very good pot plant and a fine feature plant for a shady spot. Outside frost-free climates, grow it in a deep pot with a humus-rich, free-draining mix kept slightly moist, in shade or bright indirect light, and overwinter it frost-free in a heated glasshouse, a conservatory or a bright room without direct sun. At the Jardin zoologique tropical in La Londe-les-Maures (Var, south-eastern France), a Bowenia sp. ‘Tinaroo’ has been grown in a pot since 2024 and develops slowly.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

BGCI PlantSearch lists 42 gardens declaring the species, plus two others under a variant of its author citation (checked on 31 October 2026); an entry shows that the plant has been declared at some point, not that it grows there today.

  • United Kingdom: the Royal Botanic Garden Edinburgh, the University of Bristol Botanic Garden and Treborth Botanic Garden (Bangor); no outdoor cultivation is documented in Britain.
  • United States: Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida), whose online catalogue lists four accessions, Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden, which lists it in its Garden Explorer, Marie Selby Botanical Gardens, San Diego Botanic Garden, the arboreta of California State University Fullerton and the University of California Santa Cruz, Fort Worth Botanic Garden, Lauritzen Gardens (Omaha), Lincoln Park Conservatory (Chicago), the Botanic Garden of Smith College (Massachusetts) and three gardens in Hawaii.
  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney and the Australian Botanic Garden Mount Annan, which list it in their online Garden Explorers, the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria, whose census placed it in outdoor beds at Melbourne in October 2026, the Australian National Botanic Gardens, Cairns, Cooktown, Mackay, the North Coast Regional Botanic Garden (Coffs Harbour), James Cook University (Townsville), St Kilda Botanical Gardens and the System Garden of the University of Melbourne.

Propagation

Propagation is from seed. According to the Australian Native Plants Society, the seed germinates without pretreatment, but germination may take up to 12 months; the Australian Tropical Rainforest Plants key gives 89 to 243 days. PACSOA advises pressing the seed halfway into the mix and notes that large plants can also be divided. Clean the seeds with gloves. Plants are male or female, and the pollinating weevil is absent outside Queensland: fertile seed in cultivation requires plants of both sexes and hand-pollination.

Pests and diseases

No pest specific to the species is documented in cultivation. For the Bowenia he grew in Brisbane, Leonard Butt found a dark, hard-shelled scale insect to be the only trouble, and advised controlling the ants that tend it. Cold, waterlogged soil causes rot of the underground stem, and direct sun scorches the thin leaflets. In the wild, foraging animals often break open the female cones.

Cold hardiness

No measured cold-hardiness value has been published for Bowenia spectabilis, either by a garden or by a grower. Rare Palm Seeds rates its frost tolerance as “light”, and the Australian Native Plants Society reports it hardy in tropical and subtropical areas, possibly also in frost-free temperate climates: these are general assessments, without temperatures.

Mediterranean and European experience

No trial in the open ground has been reported in Europe. On the French Fous de palmiers forum and the Italian forum Tropicamente, the species only appears in collection lists, seed exchanges and sale offers, with no winter result. At the Jardin zoologique tropical in La Londe-les-Maures, a Bowenia sp. ‘Tinaroo’ has been grown in a pot since 2024, where it develops slowly; in comparable Mediterranean climates, such as coastal California or southern Australia, this is the only documented approach.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No dated cold report for the species has been published on the English-language forums that can be read. No outdoor cultivation is documented in Britain. In Australia, Leonard Butt notes that Bowenia do very well in cultivation in tropical areas, and the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria census lists the species in outdoor beds at Melbourne, but no source gives the temperatures these plants have withstood.

Toxicity and precautions

Yes, Bowenia spectabilis is poisonous. Like other cycads, Bowenia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived. McKenzie (2006) notes that the species has been associated with field cases of poisoning in cattle, and the Australian Tropical Rainforest Plants key, after Everist (1974), states that eating the foliage is suspected of causing the locomotor disorder of cattle known as “zamia staggers”. McKenzie describes this syndrome, studied in cattle fed Bowenia serrulata or Macrozamia, as irreversible, with no treatment: a stiff, goose-stepping gait of the hind legs, knuckling of the hind fetlocks, then paralysis of the hindquarters. According to analyses by Ladd and colleagues (1993), cited by Asmussen (2011), the mature kernels of Bowenia contain 4.83 to 5.04% macrozamin by fresh weight, the highest value measured in any cycad.

Wear gloves to clean the seeds, keep plants and seeds out of reach of children, dogs, cats and livestock, and contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay in case of ingestion.

Traditional uses

Unlike most Australian cycads, whose seeds were eaten after detoxification, Bowenia spectabilis was mainly used for its underground stem. Asmussen’s (2011) review of Aboriginal cycad use gathers the historical accounts: F.M. Bailey (1883, 1906) wrote that the “yam-like root was used largely for food” after cooking, Horsfall (1987) that it required roasting and leaching, and Roth (1901) that it was eaten in some places but not at Cooktown; the rhizomes were harvested in the dry season, from May to October (Harris, 1989). A description from the Cairns Botanic Gardens adds that the seeds were roasted, crushed, soaked in water for 24 hours and roasted again before eating. Asmussen links the preference for the rhizome, which contains 17.2 to 29.1% carbohydrate (Miller and colleagues, 1993), to the rarity of female cones and to the very high toxicity of the seeds. These practices relied on traditional knowledge: the plant is not safe to eat.

Frequently asked questions

Is Bowenia spectabilis a fern?

No. It is a cycad, a seed plant that reproduces by cones, not spores. Its twice-divided fronds and underground stem give it the look of a fern, hence its English name “zamia fern”.

How do I tell it from Bowenia serrulata?

Mostly by the leaflets and the stem. Bowenia spectabilis usually has smooth-edged leaflets and a small stem with one to five crowns; Bowenia serrulata has regularly toothed leaflets and a larger stem with five to twenty crowns. Since some Bowenia spectabilis have toothed leaflets, the origin of the plant (north-eastern Queensland or the Byfield area) remains a useful clue.

Is Bowenia ‘Tinaroo’ a separate species?

Not formally. ‘Tinaroo’ is a provisional locality name for the Tinaroo population, which David Jones (1993) treated as an undescribed species and Norstog and Nicholls (1997) as a variety; the chromosomes and the pollinating weevils of the Tinaroo and Kuranda plants link them to Bowenia spectabilis, and the Australian Plant Census treats the name as a synonym. No description has been published.

Can it be grown outdoors in the UK?

Not on present evidence. No outdoor cultivation is documented in Britain, and its frost tolerance is described as light. Grow it in a pot, in shade, and overwinter it frost-free in a glasshouse, a conservatory or indoors.

Does it tolerate sun?

Only filtered sun. It is a forest plant whose thin leaflets scorch in direct sun; light shade suits adult plants, and deeper shade young plants and hot, dry climates. In the wild, Chamberlain nevertheless found it most abundant in open places and clearings within the forest.

Why does it produce so few seeds?

Because female cones are rare. In three wild populations, no more than 5% of the plants produced a female cone over several years, and foraging animals often break the cones open. In cultivation, seed is only obtained by hand-pollinating a female plant with pollen from a male.

Is it poisonous to animals?

Yes. It has been associated with poisoning in cattle in Australia, and the seeds of Bowenia are among the richest of all cycads in toxic glycosides. Keep it out of reach of dogs and cats, and see a vet at the slightest doubt.
