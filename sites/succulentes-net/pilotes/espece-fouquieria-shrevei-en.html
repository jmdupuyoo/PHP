Fouquieria shrevei is a small, white-flowered tree of the Chihuahuan Desert in northern Mexico, confined to gypsum soils in western Coahuila and the neighbouring part of Durango. It belongs to the genus Fouquieria, the ocotillos, and was described in 1939 by the American botanist Ivan Murray Johnston, who named it after his collecting companion, the desert ecologist Forrest Shreve. Its very broad, pale-edged leaves and its short, crowded clusters of white flowers set it apart from every other species of the genus. The IUCN Red List assesses it as Endangered.

Key facts about Fouquieria shrevei

  • Accepted name: Fouquieria shrevei I.M.Johnst., accepted by the World Checklist of Vascular Plants, the Kew checklist behind POWO (through GBIF); published in the Journal of the Arnold Arboretum 20: 238 (1939), under the spelling “Fouquiera shrevei”
  • Former names and synonyms: none listed by the World Checklist of Vascular Plants
  • Family: Fouquieriaceae, order Ericales
  • Common names: no English or Spanish common name attested in the sources consulted (see Common names)
  • Natural range: gypsum flats, knolls and slopes of western Coahuila and adjacent Durango, Mexico, in the Chihuahuan Desert, at about 800 to 1,800 m according to herbarium labels
  • IUCN status: Endangered (EN), assessment by Fuentes and Martínez Salas published in 2018
  • CITES and other protection: not listed by CITES (the species is absent from Species+, which lists three other Fouquieria); therefore not in the annexes of the EU wildlife trade regulations
  • Documented hardiness: not documented (see Cold hardiness)
  • Exposure and substrate: in the wild, open and very arid gypsum ground; the species is restricted to gypsum soils
  • Habit and size: a small tree 2 to 3 m tall, with a short trunk and numerous stiffly ascending, little-branched stems (Johnston 1939)
  • Toxicity: not documented (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Fouquieria shrevei

The plant is recognised by its leaves, which are much broader than in any other ocotillo and edged with a pale, papery margin, and by its white flowers packed into very short clusters in the leaf axils. The original description by Johnston (1939), drawn from the type collection, gives the following characters:

  • Habit: a small tree (“arbuscula”) 2 to 3 m tall, with a short trunk bearing numerous stiffly ascending, almost unbranched stems. A later herbarium label by James Henrickson (1964) describes the same form: a short basal trunk with several ascending to erect, straight branches.
  • Spines: 2 to 3 cm long on older growth, often longer than the leaves in their axils, flattish above and concave beneath; young spines quite often still carry a leaf blade.
  • Leaves: oblong-obovate to orbicular-elliptic or very broadly obovate, 1.5 to 3 cm long and 1 to 2.5 cm wide, rounded at the tip, narrowed abruptly into a stalk 1 to 2 mm long, with a pale scarious border about 0.3 mm wide.
  • Inflorescences: short, dense racemes 1 to 2 cm long, with 3 to 12 flowers, flattened, clearly shorter than the subtending leaf, arising from the axils of the upper leaves.
  • Flowers: sepals ovate to rounded, about 5 mm long, pale with papery margins; corolla white, with a bell-shaped tube 6 to 7 mm long, hairy inside in a narrow zone, and recurved lobes 6 to 7 mm long; ten clearly protruding stamens, with filaments up to 10 mm long and pinkish anthers 2.5 to 3 mm long; style about 9 mm long, divided above into pinkish branches.
  • Fruit: a three-valved, elongate-ovoid capsule, the valves 10 to 13 mm long; the seeds were unknown to Johnston.

Johnston also noted that the species is “very much more resinous” than Fouquieria splendens: dying plants secrete abundant brown resin in the bark of the trunk, and surprising amounts of it can be picked up around the decayed trunk of a dead plant.

Hybrids

No hybrid involving Fouquieria shrevei is documented, although the species grows almost side by side with Fouquieria splendens at the edge of some gypsum flats (Johnston 1939).

Possible confusion

The only species likely to be met alongside it is the ocotillo, Fouquieria splendens, which Johnston found very common on rocky slopes and at the foot of the hills around the type locality, growing almost side by side with Fouquieria shrevei at the margins of the gypsum flat. He wrote that the two “could be distinguished at a glance”: Fouquieria shrevei is a smaller, sturdier plant with stiffer, darker branches, larger and proportionally much broader leaves, and white flowers in short axillary clusters, whereas Fouquieria splendens has red flowers in terminal panicles.

In 1939 the only other white-flowered species known to Johnston was Fouquieria purpusii of Oaxaca, a caudiciform plant from a very different region, and he compared the size and proportions of the corolla with those of Fouquieria fasciculata. Neither of these grows in the Chihuahuan Desert, so confusion is only possible in collections.

Taxonomy

The name has been stable since it was published: Fouquieria shrevei I.M.Johnst. is accepted by the World Checklist of Vascular Plants, which lists no synonym and no infraspecific taxon. Johnston published it in 1939 in his article “New phanerogams from Mexico” in the Journal of the Arnold Arboretum (vol. 20, pp. 238–240), spelling the genus “Fouquiera”, as IPNI records; the standard spelling Fouquieria is used today.

Johnston described it as “a very distinct and well-marked species” that differs from all its congeners in its very broad leaves with scarious margins and its very short, congested axillary racemes. The type, Johnston 7815, was collected on 20 September 1938 on a gypsum flat 26 km south of Laguna del Rey, on the road to Mohovano, in Coahuila, and is kept at the Gray Herbarium of Harvard University (GH).

Molecular studies place the diversification of the genus Fouquieria in the Miocene and Pliocene, with vicariance linked to the mountain building of the Neogene and the early development of the regional deserts; most pairs of sister species live in different desert centres, which suggests that isolation by distance has been the main driver of speciation in the genus (De-Nova et al. 2018).

Etymology and history

The epithet shrevei honours Forrest Shreve (1878–1950), of the Desert Laboratory in Tucson, Arizona. Johnston explained that Shreve had been his collecting companion and that his publications showed “a sustained interest” in the genus, with many observations on the ecology, distribution and physiology of these desert trees. Shreve’s own collection from the trip, Shreve 8836, was made on 21 September 1938 on sterile gypsum soil near Mohovano and is kept as a topotype at Rancho Santa Ana Botanic Garden (RSA).

The plant had in fact been collected long before. Johnston identified as this species an old specimen in the Gray Herbarium gathered by Palmer (no. 2007) in May 1880 at “San Lorenzo de Laguna, Coahuila”, a locality he believed to lie at the northern foot of the hills east of Torreón. He also expected the species to be widely distributed on the gypsum soils of the very arid region around Laguna del Rey, which later collections have confirmed.

Common names

No common name is attested for Fouquieria shrevei in English or Spanish in the sources consulted: neither the World Checklist of Vascular Plants nor iNaturalist records one. The general Spanish name ocotillo belongs to Fouquieria splendens and should not be transferred to this species without a source.

In the wild

Fouquieria shrevei grows only on gypsum soils of northern Mexico, in the Chihuahuan Desert. Johnston found it “confined to gypsum soils” at the type locality, with Petalonyx crenatus and Selinocarpus purpusianus, and saw several colonies on the rolling country towards Laguna del Rey. The World Checklist of Vascular Plants gives its range as western Coahuila; herbarium specimens published on GBIF also include several collections from Durango.

These specimens, collected between 1938 and 2017, come from the gypsum country around Laguna del Rey, the valleys of El Hundido and Buenavista south-west of Cuatro Ciénegas, the Sierra de las Delicias and the Sierra del Venado in Coahuila, and from the Sierra de Tlahualilo and the Mapimí Biosphere Reserve in Durango. Their labels describe gypsum flats, knolls, dunes and steep gypsum slopes, between about 800 and 1,800 m, with most collections between 800 and 1,250 m. The companion plants they mention include Larrea, Acacia neovernicosa, Petalonyx, Selinocarpus, Tiquilia, Nama, Hesperaloe funifera and Agave lechuguilla, with Fouquieria splendens nearby in places.

This patchy habitat shapes the species’ genetics. In a study of five populations, Aguirre-Liguori, Scheinvar and Eguiarte (2014) found high total genetic diversity but very strong differentiation between populations (FST = 0.651), most chloroplast haplotypes being unique to a single population. They concluded that genetic drift has been intense and gene flow low between populations that follow the island-like pattern of gypsum deposits in the North American deserts.

Conservation status and regulation

Fouquieria shrevei is assessed as Endangered (EN) on the IUCN Red List, in an assessment by A.C.D. Fuentes and E. Martínez Salas published in 2018 (category consulted through GBIF on 31 October 2026). The restriction to scattered gypsum outcrops and the low gene flow between populations documented by Aguirre-Liguori et al. (2014) explain why each population matters.

The species is not listed by CITES: Species+ (consulted on 31 October 2026) lists only Fouquieria columnaris, Fouquieria fasciculata and Fouquieria purpusii in the genus. It is therefore not covered by the EU wildlife trade regulations either. Part of its range lies within the Mapimí Biosphere Reserve. Its national status should be checked in Mexico’s current list of protected species (NOM-059-SEMARNAT). Plants should only be acquired as seed-raised stock of legal origin, and the regulations in force should always be checked before any import.

Growing Fouquieria shrevei

Very little has been published on its cultivation, and the species is rare in collections. A herbarium voucher made in 2004 from a plant grown at the Desert Botanical Garden in Phoenix, Arizona, shows that it is kept in at least one botanical garden of the American Southwest. In the absence of documented trials, the best guide is its natural habitat: open gypsum ground in one of the driest parts of the Chihuahuan Desert.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

No result of outdoor cultivation of Fouquieria shrevei has been documented, whether in arid climates such as the American Southwest or in Mediterranean-type climates. Its wild conditions point to full sun and a very free-draining mineral soil; how it responds to winter wet, which harms many desert plants, is not documented for this species.

Container cultivation

No container cultivation regime specific to this species has been published, and in particular no trial has been documented on whether gypsum must be added to the substrate. Growers can only rely on the general habitat data given above.

Propagation

No propagation method specific to Fouquieria shrevei has been documented. The seeds were unknown when the species was described (Johnston 1939), and no published germination data were found. Given the conservation status of the species, seed of documented cultivated origin is the only responsible source of plants.

Pests and diseases

No pest or disease specific to Fouquieria shrevei is documented.

Cold hardiness

The cold hardiness of Fouquieria shrevei is not documented: no measured minimum temperature, no USDA zone from an identified source and no dated cultivation report were found. Hardiness figures for Fouquieria splendens, a much more widespread species, should not be transferred to it.

Toxicity and precautions

No data on toxicity have been published for Fouquieria shrevei, and none was found for the genus; this does not mean that the plant is harmless. The stems carry stiff spines 2 to 3 cm long, which call for care when handling. In case of ingestion by a person or a pet, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet.

Traditional uses

No traditional use specific to Fouquieria shrevei is documented.

Frequently asked questions

Does Fouquieria shrevei have red flowers like the ocotillo?

No. Its corolla is white, with pinkish anthers on ten protruding stamens (Johnston 1939). The flowers are borne in short, dense clusters in the leaf axils, not in the terminal panicles of red flowers that characterise Fouquieria splendens.

How can it be told apart from Fouquieria splendens?

Easily, according to its describer. Fouquieria shrevei is smaller and sturdier, with stiffer and darker branches, leaves 1 to 2.5 cm wide with a pale papery margin, white flowers and much more resin in the bark. It also grows only on gypsum, whereas Fouquieria splendens occupies the surrounding rocky slopes.

Does it only grow on gypsum?

Yes, in the wild. Johnston (1939) described it as confined to gypsum soils, and the herbarium labels of later collections describe gypsum flats, knolls, dunes and slopes. Aguirre-Liguori et al. (2014) describe it as a species confined to the gypsum soils of northern Mexico.

Where does it grow?

In northern Mexico only. Specimens come from western Coahuila, around Laguna del Rey and south-west of Cuatro Ciénegas, and from adjacent Durango, including the Mapimí Biosphere Reserve, at about 800 to 1,800 m.

Is it endangered or protected?

It is endangered but not CITES-listed. The IUCN Red List classifies it as Endangered (assessment published in 2018), while Species+ does not list it under CITES, unlike three other Fouquieria. Its status under Mexican law should be checked in the current NOM-059-SEMARNAT list.

Is it hardy?

It is not known. No minimum temperature or cultivation result has been documented for this species, so any hardiness figure found online should be treated with caution unless its source is given.

Who was it named after?

Forrest Shreve (1878–1950), of the Desert Laboratory in Tucson, Arizona. He accompanied Ivan Murray Johnston on the 1938 trip during which the type was collected, and Johnston dedicated the species to him for his long work on the genus.
