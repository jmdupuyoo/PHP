Cycas orientis is a cycad of the eucalypt savannas of eastern Arnhem Land, in Australia’s Northern Territory, where it is abundant on deep sands beneath the trees. This species of the genus Cycas was described in 1994 by the Australian botanist Ken Hill, who named it after its easterly range. It is recognised by its very glossy, light to mid-green leaves, its flat leaflets and its fairly long leaf stalks. Abundant, and growing almost entirely on Aboriginal land, it is listed as Least Concern by the IUCN.

How to recognise Cycas orientis

Cycas orientis stands out among the cycads of the Northern Territory by its light to mid-green, very glossy leaves, flat in section, by flat-edged leaflets whose midrib is more prominent beneath than above, and by its relatively long petioles (Hill 1994; Flora of Australia).

  • Trunk: slender, up to 4 m, rarely 7 m, 8 to 14 cm in diameter.
  • Leaves: 55 cm to 1.25 m long, with 130 to 180 leaflets set almost flat, at 160 to 180° on the rachis; petiole usually hairless and unarmed, 17 to 35 cm long, rarely up to 52 cm.
  • Median leaflets: 6 to 16 cm by 4.5 to 7 mm, hairless and two-toned.
  • Young growth: dense white hairs with a few orange ones; cataphylls (the scale leaves that protect the growing point) hard and pungent, 4 to 8 cm long, with a thick orange down.
  • Male cones: ovoid, about 20 cm by 8 cm; each microsporophyll ends in a fine, bent, upturned spine about 1 cm long.
  • Megasporophylls (the fertile leaves of female plants): 14 to 24 cm long, with grey and orange down, bearing only 1 to 4 ovules; narrowly triangular blade 4 to 9 cm long, regularly toothed, ending in a very variable spine 14 to 63 mm long.
  • Seeds: flattened-ovoid, orange and slightly bloomed, 25 to 36 mm long.

Hybrids

Where their ranges meet, Cycas orientis forms intermediate populations with Cycas arnhemica, the neighbouring cycad of central Arnhem Land (Hill 1994, 1996). Hill interprets these very variable populations as being of hybrid origin. No garden hybrid is documented.

Possible confusion

The closest resemblance is with Cycas armstrongii, from the Darwin area. According to Hill (1994), the leaflet midrib of Cycas armstrongii is equally prominent on both surfaces, whereas in Cycas orientis it stands out more beneath; Cycas armstrongii is also a smaller plant, with slenderer trunks and shorter leaves and petioles.

Cycas arnhemica, its western neighbour, belongs to another group: its cataphylls are soft and its leaflets have recurved or rolled margins, while those of Cycas orientis are flat and its cataphylls pungent (Hill 1996). The intermediate plants of the contact zone remain hard to name.

Taxonomy

Cycas orientis is a name accepted by The World List of Cycads and by GBIF; POWO could not be consulted. Ken Hill described it in 1994, together with Cycas arnhemica and Cycas canalis, in a paper on three new species from the Northern Territory (Telopea 5(4): 693–701).

In his 1996 revision of the Australian cycads, Hill placed it in section Cycas, subsection Endemicae, series Armstrongianosae, the alliance of Cycas armstrongii: species with flat leaflets, hard and pungent cataphylls and no hypodermis beneath the leaflet epidermis, living on sandy lateritic soils of flat country from central Cape York to the north of Western Australia, with one representative in New Guinea, Cycas papuana.

De Laubenfels and Adema (1998), with their very broad species concept, placed Cycas orientis in the synonymy of Cycas angulata. The Flora of Australia and The World List of Cycads keep it as a distinct species, so the name has not changed since 1994.

Etymology and history

The epithet is the Latin genitive orientis, “of the east”, referring to the species’ occurrence in the east of Arnhem Land (Hill 1994; Haynes 2022).

The type was collected by Ken Hill and L. Stanberg on 26 August 1991, 48.9 km east of Badalngarrmirri Creek; the holotype is in Sydney (NSW), with isotypes in Canberra (CANB) and Darwin (DNA). Hill studied fifteen collections, including several made by J.R. Maconochie in 1972 around Caledon Bay, Lake Evella and the airstrip of the BHP mining company. No history of its introduction into cultivation is documented.

Common names

No common name is attested for this species, in English or in any other language. No name in an Aboriginal language of Arnhem Land was found in the sources consulted.

In the wild

Cycas orientis is endemic to eastern Arnhem Land, in the Northern Territory, where it is abundant and widespread. It grows mainly in savanna woodland dominated by Eucalyptus tetrodonta and Eucalyptus miniata, on deep white to yellow sands over laterite, but can be found in most habitats that are not swampy (Hill 1994, 1996; Flora of Australia). The localities cited range from the Lake Evella and Buckingham River area to Caledon Bay and the surroundings of Nhulunbuy (Gove).

The climate is tropical monsoonal, with a hot wet season followed by a hot dry season, as the Palm and Cycad Societies of Australia (PACSOA) point out. Hill (1996) notes that overly frequent fires inhibit reproduction, a management problem still to be solved on these lands. Pollination and phenology are not documented.

Conservation status and regulation

The IUCN lists Cycas orientis as Least Concern (LC), in the assessment by J.D. Bösenberg published in version 2022-1 of the Red List, which replaces an earlier assessment by H. Ottley (2010); the Red List data relayed by GBIF confirm the LC category (consulted on 24 October 2026). Hill (1996) considered the species abundant and not threatened: although no reserve shelters it, its sheer abundance protects it in the medium term. Almost all populations lie on Aboriginal land. The online Flora of Australia gives no federal or territory conservation listing for it. The Red List itself could not be consulted directly.

Like every species in the genus except Cycas beddomei, Cycas orientis is listed in CITES Appendix II, where the family Cycadaceae has been included since 4 February 1977. The listing carries annotation #4 (CoP20 version, in force since 5 March 2026): seeds, pollen, seedlings or tissue cultures obtained in vitro and transported in sterile containers, and cut flowers of artificially propagated plants, are not subject to CITES, whereas international trade in the plants themselves requires an export permit. Australia, the United Kingdom and the United States apply CITES through their own national legislation; in the European Union the species is in Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, whose annexes in force are set by Commission Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026. Species+, the official CITES database, names the species and confirms this status.

This general information does not replace the official texts: check the rules in force before buying or exchanging plants, ask the seller for proof of provenance and prefer plants raised from seed in cultivation.

Growing Cycas orientis

In short: no institution has published growing advice for Cycas orientis, and no grower’s report was found. The PACSOA stresses very good drainage, in keeping with the monsoon climate of its habitat.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

Outdoors, it can only be considered in a tropical climate with no cold season. Its habitat, deep, free-draining sands under a light eucalypt canopy, points to full sun or light shade and a sandy, very free-draining soil, but this is a deduction from the wild, not a tested recommendation. No outdoor trial has been published in a Mediterranean, subtropical or temperate climate.

Container cultivation

In the United Kingdom and other temperate regions, it is a plant for a heated glasshouse or conservatory. In a 2021 note on growing Australian Cycas in a European climate, the French nurseryman Simon Lavaud (Cycadales.eu) classes “all the Northern Territory species” except Cycas angulata as “very difficult”; he does not name Cycas orientis, which falls into that group by its origin. He adds that the Northern Territory cycads are reputed to be the hardest of all, temperamental even in the subtropical climate of Queensland’s Sunshine Coast, whereas they thrive at Nong Nooch Tropical Garden in Thailand, where it is warm almost all year round. His general advice for the Australian Cycas is a strongly mineral substrate, full sun or the brightest place in the glasshouse, and generous watering in hot weather, reduced below 20 °C (68 °F) and stopped when nights fall below 10 °C (50 °F), because watering in cool weather carries a very high risk of root rot. These are a grower’s recommendations for the group, not results for this species. The Jardin zoologique tropical at La Londe-les-Maures, in the south of France, has published no observation on Cycas orientis.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In short: no record. The species does not appear in the BGCI PlantSearch database for any garden in the United Kingdom, the United States or Australia (consulted on 24 October 2026; the only garden listed is in China), nor in the online catalogues of Montgomery Botanical Center, Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden, the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney, the Australian Botanic Garden Mount Annan or the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria. No first-hand report from a private grower in these countries could be read.

Propagation

Like all Cycas, Cycas orientis is dioecious: a male and a female plant are needed for fertile seed. No germination data specific to the species have been published. Seeds are exempt from CITES under annotation #4; ask for seed from cultivated plants, not collected in the wild.

Pests and diseases

No pest or disease specific to the species is documented. In the wild, the main pressure reported is fire when it returns too often (Hill 1996). In cultivation, scale insects, including Aulacaspis yasumatsui, can attack any Cycas, and root rot in a cold, wet substrate is the failure Simon Lavaud warns about for the Australian Cycas in Europe.

Cold hardiness

No cold-tolerance data exist for Cycas orientis. Eastern Arnhem Land has a tropical monsoon climate, with two hot seasons, one wet and one dry (PACSOA), and none of the sources consulted says whether the plant can take frost.

Mediterranean and European experience

No cultivation result has been published in Europe, in the open ground or in a pot, and no dated frost episode concerns the species. Even in the mildest Mediterranean climates, which are comparable to coastal California or southern Australia, nothing supports growing it outdoors.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No cold reading and no dated growing report were found, either on forums or from botanic gardens. Treat it as frost-tender; no USDA zone can be given without a documented trial.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity analysis specific to Cycas orientis has been found. The risk is, however, well documented in the genus: Cycas tissues, and the seeds above all, contain cycasin, which is converted during digestion into methylazoxymethanol, toxic to the liver and nervous system, along with a neurotoxic amino acid, BMAA. The ASPCA lists the sago palm (Cycas revoluta) as poisonous to dogs, cats and horses. Until proven otherwise, treat the plant as poisonous: keep seeds and leaves out of reach of children and pets, and wear gloves to clean the seeds. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet straight away.

Frequently asked questions

Where does Cycas orientis grow?

In eastern Arnhem Land, in Australia’s Northern Territory, mainly in eucalypt savanna on deep sands, from the Lake Evella area to Caledon Bay and Nhulunbuy.

What does orientis mean?

“Of the east” in Latin: the name recalls that the species occupies the eastern part of Arnhem Land.

How can it be told from Cycas armstrongii?

By the leaflets. In Cycas orientis the midrib is more prominent beneath than above, whereas it is equally prominent on both surfaces in Cycas armstrongii, which is also a smaller plant.

Does it hybridise with Cycas arnhemica?

Yes, according to Ken Hill: where the two species meet, there are intermediate populations that are probably of hybrid origin.

Is it endangered?

No. The IUCN lists it as Least Concern: it is abundant and widespread, although no reserve shelters it and overly frequent fires hamper its reproduction.

Can it be grown outdoors in the UK?

No, almost certainly not. No cold data exist, its native climate is hot all year round, and Simon Lavaud ranks the Northern Territory cycads among the hardest to grow in Europe, even under glass.

Is it poisonous?

It must be assumed so. No toxicity analysis specific to the species exists, but the Cycas studied contain dangerous toxins, especially in the seeds.
