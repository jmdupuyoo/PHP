Zamia montana is a Zamia from Colombia, known mainly from the department of Antioquia on the Western Cordillera, where it grows in premontane and montane rainforest between 1,750 and 2,080 m: it is the highest-growing cycad of the New World tropics. This species of the genus Zamia, described in 1875 and for a time placed in the genus Aulacophyllum (Aulacophyllum montanum), has a stem that can become tree-like and broad leaflets grooved by their veins. Once feared extinct, it was found again in 2014 in a few forest fragments; the IUCN lists it as Critically Endangered.

  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales

  • IUCN status: Critically Endangered (CR), criteria B1ab(iii,v), assessment published in 2022

How to recognise Zamia montana

Three characters together identify it: obovate leaflets with an acuminate tip, marked by clearly visible veins, a few scattered prickles on the petiole, and a tree-like habit. Unlike the other South American species with veined leaflets, its veins form grooves on the upper surface but are only slightly raised beneath, whereas in the others they are clearly raised (Calonje and colleagues, 2015). The 2015 revision, based on plants observed in the wild, gives the following description:

  • Stem: tree-like, up to 150 cm tall and 20 cm in diameter, solitary or sometimes branched, upright or reclining.
  • Leaves: 1 to 10, up to 3.07 m long; petiole up to 1.70 m, with a few prickles no more than 2 mm long; rachis unarmed or with a few prickles in its lower third, carrying up to 30 opposite or nearly opposite leaflets.
  • Leaflets: oblanceolate, with a narrowly wedge-shaped base and an acuminate tip, leathery, with margins strongly rolled under except at the tip, entire but sometimes irregular and slightly wavy at the end; median leaflets measure 19.5 to 41 cm by 5.9 to 10.5 cm and are spaced 6 to 10 cm apart.
  • Male cones: 1 to 3 per stem, up to 13.5 cm long and 3.5 cm wide, covered in beige-yellow down, on a stalk 2.5 to 3.5 cm long.
  • Female cones: one per stem, upright, cylindrical, up to 33 cm long and 16 cm wide, covered in beige-yellow down when young (the colour at maturity has not been observed), with a blunt sterile tip no more than 5 cm long.
  • Seeds and first leaves of seedlings: unknown.

Hybrids

No hybrid, natural or horticultural, has been documented.

Possible confusion

The species with which it was longest confused is Zamia oligodonta, from the department of Risaralda: from 2009 to 2015 the two were united under the name Zamia montana. According to the revision by Calonje and colleagues (2015), Zamia oligodonta has an almost always underground stem, no more than three leaves at a time, narrowly elliptic to elliptic, wavy leaflets with a long-acuminate or tail-like tip, and veins clearly raised beneath. Zamia montana, by contrast, has a tree-like trunk, up to ten leaves and obovate leaflets with veins only slightly raised beneath. The irregular, almost coarsely toothed margin at the tip of the leaflets is found in both species and does not separate them.

The authors also compare the two species with the other South American Zamia with veined leaflets; among them, Zamia wallisii also grows in Antioquia.

Taxonomy

The World List of Cycads and GBIF accept the name Zamia montana; POWO, the site’s nomenclatural authority, could not be consulted directly. The species was described in 1875 by the German botanist Alexander Braun in the Monatsberichte der Königlichen Preussischen Akademie der Wissenschaften zu Berlin (pages 376 to 377). The original material, collected by Gustav Wallis and grown by James Veitch’s nursery, was probably destroyed with the Berlin herbarium, bombed during the Second World War; a drawing of a leaflet made by August Eichler in 1881 survives at Kew. In 2001 Dennis Stevenson therefore designated as neotype a collection made by James L. Zarucchi and colleagues on 24 September 1987 (no. 5724), at 1,750 m, at kilometre 17 of the Nutibara–Murrí road, in the municipality of Frontino (Antioquia); the neotype is kept in New York (NY), with duplicates in Bogotá (COL), Medellín (HUA) and at the Missouri Botanical Garden (MO) (Calonje and colleagues, 2015).

Aulacophyllum and “Zamia kalbreyeri”

In 1876 Eduard Regel moved the species into the genus Aulacophyllum, which he had created for Zamia with strongly veined leaflets whose leaves seemed to emerge all together. The group nevertheless included species without veined leaflets, such as Aulacophyllum ortgiesii, now a synonym of Zamia chigua, and the distinction between simultaneous and successive leaf production does not hold. Bentham and Hooker returned Aulacophyllum to Zamia in 1880, and all authors have followed them since. The name “Zamia kalbreyeri”, attributed to U. Dammer on a specimen in the Berlin herbarium and cited by Schuster in 1932, was never validly published and has no taxonomic standing (Calonje and colleagues, 2015); Species+ nevertheless still lists it as a synonym.

Zamia oligodonta separated again (2015)

In 2009 A.J. Lindström placed Zamia oligodonta, described in 2003, in the synonymy of Zamia montana. Fieldwork carried out in 2014 by Michael Calonje, Gustavo Morales, Cristina López-Gallego and Francisco Javier Roldán showed that the two species are clearly distinct, and their revision, published in Phytotaxa in 2015, separated them again. The World List of Cycads follows this revision; the CITES database, on the other hand, still treated Zamia oligodonta as a synonym of Zamia montana in 2026.

Etymology and history

The epithet comes from the Latin montanus, “of the mountains”, and refers to the premontane to montane habitat of the species in Antioquia (The World List of Cycads, after Haynes, 2022). Calonje and colleagues (2015) stress that it is the highest-growing cycad of the New World tropics.

The history of its collection can be read in the specimens cited by the 2015 revision. The original material was collected by Gustav Wallis; Wilhelm Kalbreyer collected the species at Paramillo (Antioquia) on 26 July 1880, and a specimen collected by Shuttleworth dates from 1882. Among the specimens examined, the next ones date from 1986 to 1991, all from the municipality of Frontino, between 1,750 and 2,080 m. In 2004 Stevenson considered it possibly extinct, until the searches of 2014 found living populations; in the meantime, several collecting sites of the late 1980s and early 1990s had been completely cleared. The “rediscovery of 1983” sometimes mentioned appears in none of the sources consulted.

Common names

No vernacular name is attested, in English or in Spanish.

In the wild

According to the 2015 revision, Zamia montana is endemic to the department of Antioquia, where it grows in premontane and lower montane rainforest on the Western Cordillera, between 1,750 and 2,080 m. The World List of Cycads now gives Antioquia and Risaralda; the sources consulted do not explain what data the mention of Risaralda, the department where Zamia oligodonta grows, is based on.

The climate, estimated by modelling (WorldClim), is cool and wet: 2,400 to 2,500 mm of rain a year, with two peaks (October–November and April–May) and drier months from December to March and in July–August; the mean annual temperature is 19.2 °C (66.6 °F), with values ranging from 11.9 to 25.7 °C (53.4 to 78.3 °F). The soils are rich in organic matter, high in aluminium and poor in calcium, magnesium and potassium, with a pH of 4.24 to 5.86 (Calonje and colleagues, 2015).

Very little is known about its reproduction: immature or nearly ripe male cones were collected in mid-April, and an immature female cone, almost at full size, was seen in July 2014. Caterpillars of a butterfly of the genus Eumaeus were seen eating the young leaves; the pollinators have not yet been observed.

Conservation status and regulation

Zamia montana is listed as Critically Endangered (CR) by the IUCN, under criteria B1ab(iii,v), in an assessment by Cristina López-Gallego published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads); GBIF gives the same category (the Red List itself could not be consulted). The IUCN cycad action plan (Donaldson, 2003) already classed it as CR, with fewer than 100 plants known, a steep decline due to habitat destruction and no population in a protected area; it noted that deforestation over the previous fifteen years had reduced its populations to a critical level and that the species had also suffered intensive collecting.

The 2015 revision describes a dramatic situation. The species was known from a single, highly fragmented population, made up of four subpopulations in forest fragments separated by pasture: about 100 adults in all, over an area of about 3 km² and an area of occupancy of less than 0.2 km². Some of these fragments were due to be cleared by local people within the year; in freshly felled forest the authors found plants cut down a few days earlier. Of about 80 plants recorded, only four were intact, a single one bore a cone, and no regeneration was seen. The main threats are clearance for pasture and crops such as lulo (Solanum quitoense). The species is not known from any protected area. The authors recommended keeping it as Critically Endangered (criteria A3ac, B1ab(i-v)+2ab(i-v)), as did the Colombian red list of cycads (Galeano and colleagues, 2005, as cited by the revision), and called for protected areas and an ex situ conservation programme; the exact localities were not published, to limit illegal collecting.

Like all species of the genus except Zamia restrepoi, Zamia montana falls under CITES Appendix II, where Zamia have been listed since 4 February 1977. International trade in the plants is therefore allowed, but requires an export permit from the country of origin. The listing carries annotation #4: seeds, pollen, seedlings and tissue cultures obtained in vitro and transported in sterile containers are not covered by CITES. In the European Union the species falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, whose current list is set by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026.

Species+ has a page for the species, which confirms this status (consulted on 11 October 2026); IUCN status taken from The World List of Cycads. This information does not replace the official texts: check the regulations in force before buying or exchanging plants, and ask the seller for proof of origin.

Growing Zamia montana

None of the sources consulted documents the cultivation of Zamia montana: no published growing guide, no grower’s report, no commercial offer. The 2015 revision called for an ex situ conservation programme, a sign that the species was then missing from collections.

The French specialist nursery Cycadales.eu (2023) places Zamia montana among the cloud-forest Zamia of Colombia and Panama, which it rates as “very difficult” and best left to experienced growers. For this group, for lack of data, it suggests as a starting point a compost that always drains well, neutral to acid, water that is also neutral to acid, and temperatures between 20 and 30 °C (68 and 86 °F), avoiding any extreme, too cool or too hot. These are indications for a group of species, not a trial on this one.

Its natural habitat, a cool, very wet mountain rainforest on acid, humus-rich soils, is very different from that of the lowland Zamia; no trial allows more precise advice to be drawn from it for the moment.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

No outdoor cultivation has been reported anywhere outside its native range. Its habitat suggests that only a cool, humid, frost-free tropical highland climate could suit it; this is a deduction, not a result.

Container cultivation

In the UK and other temperate climates, it could only be attempted under glass, in a humid house kept between about 20 and 30 °C, as Cycadales.eu suggests for the group, in an acid, free-draining compost rich in organic matter. No grower has reported such an attempt.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In short: no record of the species in British, American or Australian gardens.

  • United Kingdom, United States and Australia: no record in BGCI, in the online catalogue of Montgomery Botanical Center (no match on 24 October 2026) or in the inventory of British, American and Australian garden catalogues (Fairchild, Edinburgh, Cambridge, Sydney, Melbourne and others) compiled on 19 October 2026.
  • Elsewhere: the only declarations to BGCI come from Colombia, from the Jardín Botánico de Bogotá José Celestino Mutis and the Jardín Botánico de Medellín.

The British nursery Trebrown gives it a USDA zone 9b rating, its own commercial classification, without any other information and without any published observation behind it.

Propagation

Like all Zamia, the species is dioecious: propagation would be by sowing fertilised seed, and therefore by pollination between a male and a female plant. The seeds of Zamia montana have not yet been described, and there are no germination data.

Pests and diseases

In the wild, caterpillars of a butterfly of the genus Eumaeus eat the young leaves (Calonje and colleagues, 2015). No disease is documented, and there are no data from cultivation. For the Zamia of wet habitats as a whole, Cycadales.eu warns of root rot in a compost that is too wet in cool weather.

Cold hardiness

There are no cold-hardiness data for Zamia montana. Its habitat, the highest of all New World tropical cycads, is cool (mean annual temperature of 19.2 °C, or 66.6 °F, with modelled values between 11.9 and 25.7 °C, or 53.4 and 78.3 °F), but no source reports frost in its range or any cold trial in cultivation: any hardiness figure would be invented.

Mediterranean and European experience

No in-ground cultivation has been reported in France, and no dated frost episode concerns it. The Fous de palmiers forum contains no growing report: the only mention of the species (January 2013) expresses the hope of obtaining plants one day (search of 13 October 2026). The Italian Tropicamente forum has nothing on it either, and the Jardin zoologique tropical at La Londe-les-Maures, in the Var, has published no observation of this species.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No growing report was found in any of these countries, nor anywhere else, in the horticultural literature consulted.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity study specific to Zamia montana has been found. The risk is, however, documented in the genus and in other cycads: Zamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, and for Zamia furfuracea the ASPCA cites cycasin and BMAA, a neurotoxic amino acid, with vomiting, bloody diarrhoea and liver damage in dogs, cats and horses; the seeds are the most dangerous part. In the absence of evidence to the contrary, treat the plant as poisonous: keep seeds and leaves out of reach of children and animals, and wear gloves to clean seeds. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet straight away.

Traditional uses

No use is documented for this species.

Frequently asked questions

Is it a palm?

No. Despite its trunk and large leaves, Zamia montana is a cycad (Zamiaceae), a gymnosperm that reproduces by cones, with no close relationship to palms.

Where does it grow?

On the Western Cordillera of Colombia, in Antioquia (and in Risaralda according to The World List of Cycads), in mountain rainforest between 1,750 and 2,080 m: no other New World tropical cycad grows so high.

How can I tell it from Zamia oligodonta?

By its tree-like trunk, up to 1.5 m, its more numerous leaves (up to ten) and its obovate leaflets with veins only slightly raised beneath. Zamia oligodonta has an almost always underground stem, no more than three leaves and elliptic, wavy leaflets with raised veins.

Why was it thought to be extinct?

Because in 2004 Dennis Stevenson considered it possibly extinct: several of its collecting sites had been cleared. In 2014 the authors of the 2015 revision found about 100 adults in a few forest fragments.

Is it endangered?

Yes, severely. The IUCN lists it as Critically Endangered (2022), and in 2015 the species was known only from one fragmented population, threatened by clearance for pasture.

Can I grow it in the UK?

Nobody knows. There are no cultivation or cold-hardiness data; a specialist nursery classes it among the “very difficult” Zamia, to be kept between 20 and 30 °C (68 and 86 °F), which in the UK means a heated, humid glasshouse.

Is it poisonous?

It must be assumed so. No analysis exists for the species, but the cycads studied contain serious toxins, especially in their seeds.
