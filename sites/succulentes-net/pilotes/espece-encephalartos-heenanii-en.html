Encephalartos heenanii is a southern African cycad from the mountains on the border between Eswatini (the Piggs Peak district) and the South African province of Mpumalanga, where it grew on very steep grassy slopes between patches of indigenous forest. This species of the genus Encephalartos, whose young leaves and cones are wrapped in a thick brown wool, was described in 1972 by Robert Allen Dyer from plants brought to his attention by Denis Heenan; in English it is called the woolly cycad. It is now Extinct in the Wild: 115 plants were counted in 1995, a single one was seen from a helicopter in 2013, and the species survives only in gardens and private collections.

Key facts about Encephalartos heenanii

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos heenanii R.A.Dyer (1972), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15)
  • Former names and synonyms: none
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: woolly cycad (SANBI, Species+) and Heenan's cycad (Species+)
  • Natural range: Eswatini (Piggs Peak district) and South Africa (Mpumalanga, Barberton area); no longer known in the wild
  • IUCN status: Extinct in the Wild (EW), assessment by J. D. Bösenberg published in 2022; previously Critically Endangered (CR)
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I (whole genus, since 1977); Annex A in the European Union; negative EU opinion on artificially propagated plants from South Africa; specially protected in Mpumalanga, and wild collection banned throughout South Africa
  • Documented hardiness: no measured value; described as frost tolerant by SANBI's PlantZAfrica, in a habitat with hot summers and cold winters at about 1,500 m (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, steep slopes in short grassland and cliffs in deep valleys; in cultivation, light shade or full sun (PlantZAfrica), a well-drained soil (Jungle Music)
  • Habit and adult size: branched at the base, with stems up to about 2.25 m tall and 25 to 35 cm across, and leaves 1 to 1.3 m long (Dyer, 1972); larger figures in PlantZAfrica (see How to recognise)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Ganna Walska Lotusland (three adult plants and 21 seedlings in 2019), Montgomery Botanical Center (accession of 2017), the Huntington and the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley (BGCI); no British or Australian garden declares it
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; treat it as poisonous like all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos heenanii

Look for the long brown wool that covers the young leaves, the crown of the stem and the cones, and for male and female cones that are almost identical in size and shape. The original description by Dyer (1972), published in Bothalia and freely available in full, gives the following characters:

  • Habit: branched at the base; stems erect or ascending, sometimes lying down with age, up to about 2.25 m tall and 25 to 35 cm in diameter; scales at the apex very woolly, with long brown hairs
  • Leaves: covered with long brown hairs when young, then almost hairless except at the base; 100 to 130 cm long and 15 to 20 cm wide; rachis slightly twisted; petiole about 20 cm
  • Leaflets: somewhat spaced at the base of the leaf, closer above, sometimes slightly overlapping; margin entire, very rarely with one or two prickles in young plants; median leaflets 12 to 15 cm long and about 1.5 cm wide, rigid, with 20 to 30 veins raised on the underside; the lowest leaflets are not reduced to prickles, although one or two may be lobed and slightly spiny
  • Cones: solitary, usually borne to one side of the last crown of leaves, clothed in a shaggy brown wool about 1 cm long that almost hides the scales; male and female cones are nearly the same size and shape, but the male is much lighter
  • Male cone: subcylindrical, 27 to 30 cm long and 15 to 17 cm across, on a stalk 8 to 9 cm long
  • Female cone: broadly ovoid, 23 to 30 cm long and 17 to 18 cm across, on a stalk 5 to 6 cm long; still immature seeds 3.75 to 4 cm long

Dyer also reports a curious case: on one plant, Heenan saw an almost hairless cone next to a normally woolly one.

The PlantZAfrica account of the species (Zondi, 2022) describes plants in cultivation and gives somewhat different figures. It describes a single, unbranched aerial stem up to 3 m long and 30 cm in diameter, with basal suckers on mature plants, a crown of golden-brown wool and upright leaves whose incurved rachis gives the plant a “wine-glass” outline. It distinguishes, unofficially, a short-leaf form, with leaves 0.95 to 1.05 m long and median leaflets 12 to 15 cm by about 1.5 cm, and a long-leaf form, with leaves 1.25 to 1.76 m long and leaflets 20 to 30 cm by 2 cm. Young leaves look bluish because of their golden-brown felt; mature leaves are dark green above and paler beneath. According to the same account, both sexes produce one to three cones a season, light green but hidden by brown wool, which thins on mature male cones and persists on female cones; male cones shed pollen in winter (June to August) and female cones break up in summer (December to February); the ripe seeds are bright red, 55 mm long and 32 mm across. The figures it gives for the male cone (270–300 mm in diameter and 150–170 mm long) appear to have length and diameter swapped when compared with Dyer's. The San Diego County nursery Jungle Music describes a basket-shaped crown of leaves curving inwards, trunks of up to about 8 ft (2.4 m) in mature plants, orange-brown female cones and male cones ranging from light green to tan.

Hybrids

Dyer considered, when the plant was discovered, that it might be a natural hybrid between Encephalartos paucidentatus, abundant 1.5 km away, and Encephalartos laevifolius, which grows a little further off. He found no evidence for it, and the fresh cones collected in 1971 confirmed that the plant was a distinct species. PlantZAfrica states that Encephalartos heenanii hybridises with Encephalartos paucidentatus where the two share a habitat, and mentions anecdotal reports, cited from Grobbelaar (2002), that the long-leaf form might descend from a natural hybrid between Encephalartos paucidentatus and the short-leaf form; these reports are not supported by any published study. The 2017 Biodiversity Management Plan for South Africa's threatened Encephalartos lists the pair heenanii/paucidentatus among the closely related species whose taxonomy DNA barcoding should help to resolve.

Possible confusion

The closest species is Encephalartos paucidentatus. According to Dyer's Latin diagnosis, Encephalartos heenanii differs in being more branched at the base, with smaller stems, shorter leaves, leaflets that are not recurved, cones densely covered in brown wool and male cone scales with a thicker, shorter swelling. In Encephalartos paucidentatus the cones, yellowish and barely woolly, also differ markedly between the sexes: the male cones, 40 to 50 cm long, are much slimmer than the female cones, whereas the two look alike in Encephalartos heenanii. PlantZAfrica adds Encephalartos transvenosus to the species it is confused with: the leaflets of Encephalartos transvenosus lack the prominent longitudinal ridges on the underside, while those of Encephalartos paucidentatus are toothed, its leaves are straight or curve downwards and its basal leaflets are reduced to several spines. The leaves of the short-leaf form of Encephalartos heenanii are also much shorter than those of Encephalartos paucidentatus.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos heenanii is an accepted name with no synonym (The World List of Cycads). Robert Allen Dyer published it in 1972 in Bothalia (10(4): 539–546), under the title “A new species of Encephalartos from Swaziland”. The holotype (D. Heenan s.n., PRE 30904), kept at the National Herbarium in Pretoria, was collected in September 1969 in the Piggs Peak district of Swaziland, now Eswatini, “on krantzes”, that is on cliffs; The World List of Cycads records isotypes at Munich (M), Pretoria (PRE) and Stockholm (S). Male and female cones were collected in June 1971. No change of name or rank has been proposed since.

Etymology and history

The epithet honours Denis Heenan (1927–2003), of Swaziland, who recognised the plant as a distinct species and brought it to the attention of Dyer (Haynes, 2022, cited by The World List of Cycads). According to Dyer, Heenan alerted him in August 1969; no fresh cones were then available, and the remains of old cones had been badly damaged by insects. The field observations of Denis Heenan and his son David, who is photographed beside a plant in the publication, made the description possible.

The species entered collections long before it vanished from the wild. According to the South African National Biodiversity Institute (SANBI), no permit to collect it in the wild was ever issued, but plants may have been removed before the provincial laws of the 1970s, seedlings were available from the Hartebeesthoek nursery between 1975 and 1998, and some plants were reportedly rescued from gold-mining areas. International trade began in 1988: 216 specimens were exported from South Africa up to the end of 2011, 93 % of them after 1995, during the very period in which the wild population collapsed.

Common names

In English the species is called the woolly cycad, the name used by SANBI and PlantZAfrica for the thick wool below the crown of leaves, and Heenan's cycad; Species+ gives both. No vernacular name in Afrikaans or siSwati is given by these sources.

In the wild

Encephalartos heenanii grew, according to SANBI, on very steep slopes in short grassland, in deep valleys between patches of indigenous forest; Dyer adds “on cliffs”. It was known from the Piggs Peak district in Eswatini and from a provincial nature reserve in Mpumalanga. PlantZAfrica places it in the Barberton area, in open grassy places, on steep slopes and at forest margins, at about 1,500 m. The climate there is hot in summer and cold in winter, with about 1,200 mm of rain a year, falling mainly in summer. Jungle Music describes the habitat as hot and dry with little rainfall, which does not agree with these figures.

Fire seems to play a part in its life cycle. SANBI considers it possibly fire-adapted, like other grassland cycads, and PlantZAfrica writes that regular fires stimulate coning. In the region, fires are now suppressed because of pine plantations, among which the last plants grew; this was still considered a threat to them. PlantZAfrica also notes that the very woolly cones of species such as Encephalartos heenanii were one of the arguments against wind pollination: according to the same source, insects are now known to pollinate all ten cycad genera.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos heenanii is Extinct in the Wild (EW) according to the assessment by Bösenberg (2022) for the IUCN Red List, cited by The World List of Cycads. It was previously listed as Critically Endangered (CR, criteria B1ab(ii,iv,v)+2ab(ii,iv,v)), and PlantZAfrica still gives that category from the national Red List of South African plants (assessment by Donaldson, 2009). SANBI's non-detriment findings document (2012, updated 2015) traces the collapse:

  • in 1995, 115 plants, with 326 stems, were counted in the Mpumalanga reserve;
  • by 2006 only about 24 plants, with 45 stems, remained, all of the long-leaf form;
  • in 2013 a helicopter survey found a single plant;
  • plants had also disappeared from the few known sites in Eswatini.

The 2017 Biodiversity Management Plan (Government Gazette No. 40793) gives slightly different dates, with fewer than 24 plants recorded in 2009 and recent surveys that failed to find any plant; it expects any remaining wild plant to fail to reproduce. PlantZAfrica, whose figures come from the 2009 national assessment, still mentions fewer than 400 mature plants and a few scattered groups of about 20 in Eswatini; these older figures do not match the later surveys.

The cause is poaching for gardens and collections, even inside a protected area. The poachers responsible for removing at least 100 stems were arrested and jailed after an investigation lasting several years; in a case of 2006, thieves tried to grind away the fire scars on three plants, a sign of their wild origin. In 2011–2012, 43 % of the ranger posts in the reserve were vacant, and the provincial agency had no operating budget from 2011 to 2014.

Ex situ conservation is not secure either. The 2017 management plan states that no viable collection of the species exists in South Africa's national botanical gardens or other government nurseries, that the plants cone infrequently and that hand pollination and seedling survival are relatively poor. It therefore recommends recovery from plants held privately, through a confidential database of genetically pure plants in South Africa and abroad, and records that members of the Cycad Society of South Africa have offered to donate seed or seedlings for this purpose.

The species is specially protected under the Mpumalanga Nature Conservation Act (No. 10 of 1998), and the collection of wild cycads has been banned throughout South Africa since 2007 (Government Notice No. 371 of 2012 for the current text). Like the whole genus, it has been listed in CITES Appendix I since 4 February 1977 and, in the European Union, in Annex A of the wildlife trade regulations (current list: Regulation (EU) 2026/1383), according to Species+. Species+ also records a negative opinion of the EU Scientific Review Group, in force since 7 February 2013, on imports of artificially propagated plants from South Africa, whatever their size; a review covering the whole genus was under consultation in September 2026. Databases consulted on 25 October 2026: check the current rules and ask for proof of origin before any purchase.

Growing Encephalartos heenanii

The species now survives only in cultivation, and its future depends on collections. PlantZAfrica describes it as a slow grower that does best in light shade or full sun and is frost tolerant. Jungle Music recommends sun in coastal and inland southern California, part sun or filtered light in desert areas, and a well-drained soil; it notes that the plants cone very sporadically and produce few suckers in cultivation, so that every means of propagation is limited. Both sources stress that it does not transplant well: according to PlantZAfrica, mature plants may die when moved.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

The best documented example of cultivation is Ganna Walska Lotusland, in Montecito near Santa Barbara, California, where the species is grown in the cycad garden:

  • the garden holds three adult plants and works with the IUCN Cycad Specialist Group on an “insurance colony”, a back-up collection; two such colonies are recommended, one in the country of origin and one elsewhere;
  • at the end of 2010 a female produced a cone; it was hand-pollinated several times in early March 2011 with pollen of the same species supplied by the collector Loran Whitelock;
  • the seeds were harvested on 16 September 2011, a first for the United States according to Whitelock; sown on 27 January 2012, they began to germinate on 20 February;
  • in 2019 Lotusland was growing 21 plants raised from these seeds; a southern California collector keeps pollen for future pollinations, and the garden hopes to send seedlings back to South Africa.

According to Lotusland, the species cones very rarely, even in South Africa. In South Africa, the plants photographed for PlantZAfrica grow in the Lowveld National Botanical Garden at Mbombela. No planting in the ground is documented in Europe or Australia.

Container cultivation

No grower's report on container cultivation has been published. Given the value of the plants and their dislike of disturbance, a large, deep, free-draining container, moved as little as possible, is the prudent choice where the plant cannot go outdoors all year; under glass, the same well-drained conditions apply.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In the United States, BGCI PlantSearch lists six institutions that declare the species: Ganna Walska Lotusland, Montgomery Botanical Center in Coral Gables (one accession, of 2017 according to its number, in the garden's online catalogue), the Huntington in San Marino, the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley, the botanical garden of the University of Hawaii at Hilo and the US National Plant Germplasm System. A BGCI listing shows that a garden has declared the species at some point, not that a plant is alive today. No garden in Britain or Australia declares it to BGCI. In Italy, the Botanic Garden of Naples grew one plant, of Swaziland origin, in 1993–1994 (Gaudio and colleagues, 1997); its survival has not been confirmed.

Propagation

Propagation relies mainly on hand pollination and seed, as Lotusland has shown, and pollen can be stored by collectors to fertilise a female that cones years later. PlantZAfrica recommends sowing in river sand on a heated bench at 24 to 28 °C (75.2 to 82.4 °F); germination should start four to six weeks after sowing, but some seeds take longer, especially without bottom heat. Suckers are the other method. According to PlantZAfrica, a sucker can be removed when it reaches about 80 mm in diameter: clear the soil around it, cut it cleanly at the point of attachment with a clean saw, chisel or knife, and treat both wounds with a fungicide, sulphur or a tree seal. A sucker of at least 250 mm with its own roots can go straight into the ground; smaller or rootless ones should first be left to dry and callus for a few weeks in a cool, dry place before potting. SANBI notes that, in cycads, suckers left in the ground after the main plant has been dug up sometimes survive. Seeds, like plants, fall under CITES Appendix I.

Pests and diseases

In habitat, PlantZAfrica reports that plants are susceptible to the caterpillars of the leopard moth. Dyer noted in 1969 that the remains of old cones were badly damaged by insects. In cultivation no disease specific to the species is described; as with other Encephalartos, the main risk is root and stem rot in a cold, wet soil, and the reluctance of mature plants to be moved makes any root damage more serious.

Cold hardiness

No measured cold hardiness value exists for Encephalartos heenanii. PlantZAfrica describes it as frost tolerant and its habitat, at about 1,500 m, as cold in winter, but gives no temperature. As in other Encephalartos, survival in the cold often concerns young plants whose caudex is still underground, protected by the warmth stored in the soil and sometimes by snow; adult plants with an aerial stem do not enjoy that protection, and the tolerance of a seedling says nothing about that of a mature plant.

Mediterranean and European experience

No outdoor trial of the species is documented in France, Italy or elsewhere in Europe. On the French Fous de palmiers forum, a participant reported in 2011 that he had seen a South African collector plant a specimen with a 1 m stem in a cold area, without knowing whether it would survive the winter; this second-hand account gives no place or temperature.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

Lotusland, in a mild Mediterranean climate on the coast of southern California, grows the species outdoors without reporting cold damage or a minimum temperature. No other dated report with a temperature has been published. No botanical source gives a USDA zone for the species; the zone ratings found on some websites cite no measurement. In Britain it would be a plant for a cool glasshouse, but no British garden is known to grow it.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data have been published for Encephalartos heenanii. The risk is nevertheless documented in the genus: the toxicity of Encephalartos horridus was demonstrated as early as 1944 (Van der Walt), and Encephalartos, like other cycads, contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; these substances affect the liver, the digestive tract and the nervous system, and the seeds are the most dangerous part. The ASPCA reports vomiting, diarrhoea, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses that have eaten cycads.

Treat the plant as poisonous: wear gloves to handle seeds and keep plants and seeds away from children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use specific to Encephalartos heenanii is documented. PlantZAfrica describes the general beliefs attached to cycads in isiZulu culture, where they are planted at the gate of a homestead to protect it, without linking them to this species.

Frequently asked questions

Does Encephalartos heenanii still exist in the wild?

No. The IUCN has listed it as Extinct in the Wild since 2022. The last documented survey, by helicopter in 2013, found a single plant in a Mpumalanga reserve, and the sites in Eswatini were already empty.

How many plants were left, and when?

115 plants (326 stems) in 1995, about 24 (45 stems) in 2006 and only one in 2013, according to SANBI. The more dramatic figures found online, such as “272 stems in 1996” or “none left in 2019”, appear in no SANBI document.

Why did it disappear?

Because of poaching. The plants were dug up for collections, even inside a nature reserve, at the same time as legal trade in seedlings was growing. The absence of fire, caused by pine plantations, may also have harmed the last plants by reducing coning.

How can I recognise it?

By its brown wool. The young leaves, the crown and the cones are covered in long brown hairs, and the male and female cones are almost the same size and shape. The leaflets have entire margins, and the lowest ones are not reduced to spines.

Is it a hybrid of Encephalartos paucidentatus?

No, it is a distinct species. Dyer tested the idea in 1969–1971 and rejected it once fresh cones were collected. PlantZAfrica nevertheless reports natural hybrids with Encephalartos paucidentatus and anecdotal claims that the long-leaf form is of hybrid origin, which DNA studies planned in South Africa should clarify.

Is it frost-hardy?

Possibly, but nobody has measured it. PlantZAfrica calls it frost tolerant, and its mountain habitat has cold winters, but no temperature has been published. Given its value, protect it from hard frost and winter wet.

Can I buy one legally?

Only with great difficulty and with full documentation. The species is in CITES Appendix I and EU Annex A, and the EU has a negative opinion on imports of artificially propagated plants from South Africa. Any plant offered for sale must come with proof of cultivated origin.
