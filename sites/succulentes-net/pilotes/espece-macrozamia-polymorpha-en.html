Macrozamia polymorpha is a small cycad with no visible trunk from northern New South Wales, in eastern Australia, where it grows as scattered plants in dry woodland on deep sands around Coonabarabran and north to the southern Pilliga Scrub. This species of the genus Macrozamia owes its name to its leaflets “of many forms”: most are entire, but some plants carry a mixture of simple and forked leaflets. Long confused with Macrozamia heteromera, and known to enthusiasts by the nickname “Coonabarabran hybrid”, it was described only in 1998, by David Jones. It is listed as Least Concern by the IUCN.

Key facts about Macrozamia polymorpha

  • Accepted name: Macrozamia polymorpha D.L.Jones (1998), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and the Australian Plant Census
  • Former names and synonyms: no synonym in the strict sense; plants formerly included in Macrozamia heteromera; the Australian Plant Census lists the informal phrase name Macrozamia sp. ‘Southern Pilliga’
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales; section Parazamia
  • Common names: no established English common name; the enthusiasts’ nickname “Coonabarabran hybrid” predates the description
  • Natural range: northern New South Wales, Australia, around Coonabarabran and north to the southern Pilliga Scrub; east towards Gunnedah according to PACSOA
  • IUCN status: Least Concern (LC), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated 10 September 2020 and published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union; not listed under the federal EPBC Act or in New South Wales
  • Documented hardiness: no cultivation data; in the wild, about 76 frost days a year at Coonabarabran according to PACSOA, with a weekly July minimum around −3.6 °C (25.5 °F) (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, dry eucalypt and Callitris woodland on deep sands derived from sandstone; in cultivation, a very free-draining soil
  • Habit and adult size: underground stem 10 to 25 cm across; 2 to 7 leaves (up to about 10) 50 to 100 cm long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Royal Botanic Garden Sydney and Australian Botanic Garden Mount Annan (plants of wild origin), Montgomery Botanical Center (Florida); declared to BGCI by Adelaide Botanic Garden and two Californian gardens
  • Toxicity: no data for the species; the genus is very toxic to people and animals (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Macrozamia polymorpha

Within the Macrozamia heteromera group, it is recognised by its bright green, broader leaflets, which are usually entire, and by the always entire leaflets of its seedlings (Flora of Australia). The flora gives the following measurements.

  • Stem: underground, 10 to 25 cm across.
  • Leaves: 2 to 7 in the crown, 50 to 100 cm long, moderately to strongly keeled, mid-green, semi-glossy; petiole 12 to 22 cm long; axis not to moderately spirally twisted.
  • Leaflets: 70 to 110 per leaf, simple or occasionally forked, 12 to 26 cm long and 5 to 9 mm wide (segments 3 to 5 mm wide), the same colour on both faces or slightly paler beneath; the lowest leaflets are not reduced to spines.
  • Male cones: spindle-shaped, 16 to 25 cm long and 4.5 to 5.5 cm across.
  • Female cones: ovoid, 13 to 20 cm long and 7 to 9 cm across, with terminal spines 2 to 30 mm long.
  • Seeds: ovoid, 26 to 30 mm long and 22 to 25 mm wide, with red flesh.

The authors of the page of the Palm and Cycad Societies of Australia (PACSOA), who have studied the species in the field, describe obliquely erect, then spreading leaves with a strongly recurved tip, bright green, up to about ten per plant (usually three to five) and up to 1 m long. The leaflets leave the axis in an open V and have a creamy-yellow callus at the base. In their experience most plants carry mainly entire leaflets; others mix entire and divided leaflets, and these variations occur from plant to plant within one stand, sometimes between two neighbouring plants.

Hybrids

Yes. David Jones writes in Cycads of the World (second edition, quoted by PACSOA) that Macrozamia polymorpha hybridises with Macrozamia diplomera where the two species grow close together. PACSOA adds that plants around Coonabarabran clearly show examples of hybridisation, and that identification there is often difficult. No garden hybrid has been documented.

Possible confusion

It is most easily confused with the other small species of the region with forked leaflets.

  • Macrozamia heteromera: its closest relative according to PACSOA, from which it was separated; narrower, duller leaflets, mostly divided.
  • Macrozamia diplomera: more numerous, flat, stiff leaves, mostly forked leaflets and lowest leaflets reduced to spines; the two species cross.
  • Macrozamia glaucophylla and Macrozamia stenomera: leaflets mostly divided, glaucous in the first, very slender and divided two or three times in the second.

Taxonomy

Macrozamia polymorpha D.L.Jones is accepted by The World List of Cycads and the Australian Plant Census. It belongs to section Parazamia, that of the small species with an underground stem, and to the Macrozamia heteromera group.

Before 1998 these plants were included in Macrozamia heteromera, a species described by Charles Moore in 1884 and already known to be very variable. According to PACSOA, enthusiasts called them the “Coonabarabran hybrid”. In 1998 David Jones revised the complex and separated two species from it, Macrozamia glaucophylla and Macrozamia polymorpha; the description of the latter appeared that year in volume 48 of the Flora of Australia (p. 718). For the reader, this means that plants labelled Macrozamia heteromera before 1998, especially if they come from around Coonabarabran and have mostly entire leaflets, may belong to this species. The Australian Plant Census also records the informal name Macrozamia sp. ‘Southern Pilliga’ as a synonym. An article by B. and S. Ironmonger (2003) on the taxonomy, distribution and cultivation of the Macrozamia heteromera group is cited by The World List of Cycads.

Etymology and history

The epithet comes from the Greek poly-, “many”, and -morphos, “form”, and refers to the leaflets, sometimes divided and sometimes entire (The World List of Cycads, after Haynes, 2022).

The type was collected by D.L. Jones and B.E. Jones on 7 June 1991, about 4.2 km north along Dandry Road from the Newell Highway, in New South Wales (holotype in the Australian National Herbarium, Canberra, CANB; isotypes in Brisbane, Melbourne and Sydney). The flora’s representative specimens include collections made long before the description, among them one by L.A.S. Johnson in November 1951 at Tummallallee Creek, 48 km north-north-east of Coonabarabran. Nothing is documented about its introduction into cultivation.

Common names

No English common name is attested. The only name recorded is the nickname “Coonabarabran hybrid”, which PACSOA gives as the name used by enthusiasts before the species was described; it is not an established vernacular name, and it is misleading, since the species is not a hybrid. No Aboriginal name is recorded. Gardeners and botanists use the scientific name.

In the wild

The species is locally abundant around Coonabarabran and north to the southern Pilliga Scrub, in northern New South Wales (Flora of Australia). PACSOA places its range between Coonabarabran and Gunnedah and mentions the Rocky Glen area, about 35 km east of Coonabarabran.

It grows in dry sclerophyll woodland on sandy soils over sandstone (Flora of Australia), on dry, flat ground, as scattered single plants under a canopy of eucalypts and native pines (Callitris), according to PACSOA. For Coonabarabran (509 m), the same source gives 735 mm of rain a year, a third of it in summer, a minimum of −3.6 °C (25.5 °F) reached at least once a week in July, a maximum of 35.9 °C (96.6 °F) reached at least once a week in January, and frost on about 76 days a year. Female plants usually carry a single cone, sometimes two; males can carry up to three. No study of the pollination of the species has been published.

Conservation status and regulation

Macrozamia polymorpha is listed as Least Concern (LC) on the IUCN Red List. The assessment, by J.D. Bösenberg, is dated 10 September 2020 and was published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, GBIF and Crossref, checked on 26 October 2026). No listing under the federal Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act 1999 (EPBC) or in New South Wales appears in the Atlas of Living Australia (checked on 26 October 2026).

Like all Macrozamia, it has been listed in CITES Appendix II since 4 February 1977, with annotation #4 in the version adopted at CoP20, in force since 5 March 2026: live plants need an export permit, while seeds, pollen and seedlings grown in vitro in sterile containers are exempt. In the European Union it falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026 (Species+, checked on 26 October 2026). Check the regulations in force, and the customs and plant-health rules, before any purchase abroad.

In practice, buy seed-grown plants of known origin; seed collected where it grows with Macrozamia diplomera may give hybrids.

Growing Macrozamia polymorpha

No reliable source describes the cultivation of this species: the advice below rests on its habitat and on the general rules for the genus. It remains rare in collections; in February 2013, a grower on the French Fous de palmiers forum reported ordering seed from a specialist seller, without any follow-up.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Soil: very free-draining and sandy, like the deep sands of its habitat.
  • Light: in the wild, under a light canopy of eucalypts and Callitris; a bright position suits it. Its tolerance of full sun outside Australia is not documented.
  • Water: about 735 mm of rain a year in its region, slightly more in summer; water during growth, let the soil dry between waterings and avoid cold, waterlogged soil in winter.
  • Growth: no data for the species; small Macrozamia with an underground stem are generally very slow.

Container cultivation

No container culture has been described. Given its small size and the lack of cold data, a deep pot with a sandy, very free-draining mix is the most sensible choice outside its native climate; in a pot, however, the stem loses the protection the soil gives it in winter.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney grows a plant of wild provenance in a bed, from an accession numbered in 1992 (S1992-3817); the Australian Botanic Garden Mount Annan lists five accessions of wild provenance numbered in 2023 (including A2023-20691 and A2023-2445), according to their Garden Explorers. The Botanic Garden and State Herbarium of South Australia (Adelaide) also declares the species to BGCI.
  • United States: Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida) lists accessions numbered in 2000 (2000411) and 2023 (20230184 to 20230186) in its online catalogue; the Fullerton Arboretum (California State University, Fullerton) and the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley declare it to BGCI. A BGCI declaration says that a garden has held the plant, not that a living specimen is there today.
  • United Kingdom: no garden declares the species to BGCI (checked on 26 October 2026).

No grower in the English-speaking world has published a cultivation result.

Propagation

Propagation is by seed. Plants are male or female: both sexes are needed and, in cultivation, hand-pollination is required to obtain fertile seed. Seed collected in the wild near Macrozamia diplomera may give hybrids. The seedlings have entire leaflets, a character that helps to tell them from those of Macrozamia heteromera (Flora of Australia). No germination data specific to the species have been published.

Pests and diseases

No problem specific to the species is documented. For Macrozamia grown in Europe, growers’ forums mainly mention scale insects and rot in cold, wet soil (see the genus page).

Cold hardiness

No cultivation report exists. In the wild, the species endures frequent frosts, with its stem protected by the soil.

Mediterranean and European experience

No outdoor cultivation of the species has been reported in Europe, in the ground or in a pot. On the French Fous de palmiers forum, in March 2014, a grower reported the opinion of an Australian nurseryman specialising in cycads, who ranked Macrozamia polymorpha among the hardiest species of the genus, those from around Coonabarabran and the Pilliga, without giving temperatures. It is an opinion, not a trial.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No dated report exists on the English-language forums that can be read. The Bureau of Meteorology’s Coonabarabran (Showgrounds) station, at 520 m, records about 53 days a year at or below 0 °C (32 °F) in the air and a lowest temperature of −9.0 °C (15.8 °F), on 12 July 2002. These figures describe the region, where the plant withstands repeated frosts with an underground stem protected by the soil; they say nothing of what a plant in a pot or in wet soil would tolerate. In Britain and in the colder parts of the United States, a pot kept frost-free and dry in winter is the prudent choice.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data specific to Macrozamia polymorpha have been published, but the risk is well documented for the genus, including close species of the same region, and the plant should be treated as very toxic, especially its seeds and young leaves. Macrozamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a powerful poison of the liver; cattle that eat the young leaves can develop a locomotor disorder of the hindquarters, the “zamia staggers”, which McKenzie (2006) describes as irreversible, with no treatment (see the genus page).

Wear gloves to handle the seeds, keep them out of reach of children, dogs and livestock, and contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay in case of ingestion.

Traditional uses

No use specific to Macrozamia polymorpha is documented. Asmussen’s (2011) review of the eating of Macrozamia seeds by Aboriginal peoples does not mention this species (see the genus page).

Frequently asked questions

Is it a hybrid?

No. Despite the old nickname “Coonabarabran hybrid”, it is a species described in 1998. True natural hybrids do exist, however, with Macrozamia diplomera, where the two species grow close together.

Why do some leaves have forked leaflets?

Because the species is variable. Most plants have entire leaflets, but some mix entire and divided leaflets, hence the name; seedlings, on the other hand, always have entire leaflets.

How does it differ from Macrozamia heteromera?

By its leaflets. Those of Macrozamia polymorpha are bright green, broader and mostly entire; those of Macrozamia heteromera are narrower and mostly divided.

How hardy is it?

It has not been tested. No cultivation report exists; in its habitat it endures about 76 frost days a year according to PACSOA, and the Coonabarabran weather station has recorded −9.0 °C (15.8 °F). An Australian nurseryman, quoted on a French forum, ranks it among the hardiest Macrozamia, without figures.

Is it endangered?

No. The IUCN lists it as Least Concern (assessment published in 2022), and it remains locally abundant. Like the whole genus, it is nevertheless listed in CITES Appendix II.

Is it poisonous?

Probably, like all Macrozamia. No analysis specific to the species exists, but the seeds and young leaves of the genus are very toxic to people, pets and livestock.
