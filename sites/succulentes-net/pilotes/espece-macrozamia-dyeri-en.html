Macrozamia dyeri is a large cycad endemic to the south coast of Western Australia, where it grows in coastal heath and shrubland around Esperance, between the Munglinup River and Israelite Bay. This robust species of the genus Macrozamia forms a thick trunk above ground, up to 3 m tall according to the Flora of Australia, crowned by many grey-green leaves 2 m long or more. Ferdinand von Mueller described it in 1885 as Encephalartos dyeri, and it was transferred to Macrozamia in 1930. The Noongar people of the Esperance region call it “djeeri”, and a 2020 study showed that its present distribution follows the places where they lived.

Key facts about Macrozamia dyeri

  • Accepted name: Macrozamia dyeri (F.Muell.) C.A.Gardner (1930), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15), the Australian Plant Census and Florabase
  • Former names and synonyms: Encephalartos dyeri F.Muell. (1885, basionym); Macrozamia preissii subsp. dyeri (F.Muell.) J.Schust. (1932); included in Macrozamia riedlei by Gardner (1956) and Johnson (1959)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: zamia palm and djeeri, a Noongar name (Flora of Australia)
  • Natural range: Western Australia, south coast, from the Munglinup River to Israelite Bay (Esperance region)
  • IUCN status: Least Concern (LC), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated August 2020 and published in 2022; “not threatened” in Western Australia (Florabase)
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II, like the whole genus; Annex B in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: no dated cultivation report (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, open coastal heath and shrubland on deep calcareous beach sands; in cultivation, not documented
  • Habit and adult size: trunk above ground 0.4 to 3 m tall and 50 to 120 cm across; 70 to 150 leaves 1.8 to 2.4 m long; up to 6.5 m tall according to Jones (1993)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: listed at the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney; declared to BGCI by Mount Annan, Kings Park (Perth), Fairchild and Lotusland, and by the Millennium Seed Bank in England
  • Toxicity: not studied for the species; seeds and young leaves are toxic throughout the genus (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Macrozamia dyeri

It is recognised by its massive habit, with a trunk above ground, and by its long keeled leaves carrying relatively few but broad leaflets. The Flora of Australia (Hill, 1998) distinguishes it by its very robust habit with a visible trunk, its semi-glossy keeled leaves with relatively few broad leaflets, and its slender male cones whose scales end in only a short spine. Its measurements are:

  • Trunk: above ground, 0.4 to 3 m tall and 50 to 120 cm across;
  • Leaves: 70 to 150 in the crown, 180 to 240 cm long, moderately keeled (the two rows of leaflets form a V), grey-green, semi-glossy; petiole 26 to 63 cm;
  • Leaflets: 70 to 100 per leaf, undivided, 33 to 45.5 cm long and 12.5 to 17 mm wide, barely paler beneath, with flat margins and a sharp tip; the lowest leaflets are reduced to spines;
  • Male cones: spindle-shaped, 48 to 62 cm long and 10 to 14 cm wide, with terminal spines 2 to 22 mm long;
  • Female cones: narrowly ovoid, 45 to 50 cm long and 15 to 20 cm wide;
  • Seeds: ovoid, 43 to 55 mm long and 28 to 36 mm wide, with red flesh.

Florabase, the official flora of Western Australia, describes it more briefly: a tree-like cycad with a large, thick trunk above ground, dull, strongly keeled leaves with broad leaflets, and long, narrow cones. The two sources differ slightly on the appearance of the leaves (semi-glossy or dull) and on how strongly they are keeled.

Jones (1993), cited by the Gymnosperm Database, gives much larger dimensions for the biggest plants: up to 6.5 m tall and 100 cm across. These figures exceed the maximum given by the Flora of Australia and probably concern exceptional individuals.

Hybrids

No natural or garden hybrid has been reported. The species is geographically separated from the other Macrozamia of Western Australia, which grow further west.

Possible confusion

The risk of confusion concerns the two other Western Australian species, Macrozamia fraseri and Macrozamia riedlei. According to the Flora of Australia, the three form a group of closely related species with almost cylindrical petioles carrying a few leaflets reduced to spines. Macrozamia dyeri stands out by its broader leaflets (12.5 to 17 mm against 7.5 to 14 mm in Macrozamia fraseri), fewer in number (70 to 100 against 112 to 174), and by male cones that are narrower for their length. Florabase notes that the cones of Macrozamia fraseri are “large and broad”, those of Macrozamia dyeri “long and narrow”. Distribution usually settles the matter: Macrozamia dyeri is the only species of the south-east coast, around Esperance.

In European gardens, identifying plants sold as Macrozamia remains tricky. In March 2010, on the French Fous de palmiers forum, members hesitated between Macrozamia dyeri and other species for a potted plant, without reaching a conclusion.

Taxonomy

Macrozamia dyeri (F.Muell.) C.A.Gardner belongs to the family Zamiaceae. Ferdinand von Mueller described it in 1885 as Encephalartos dyeri, in a pharmaceutical journal, the Australasian Chemist and Druggist (vol. 8, p. 12). Several Australian Macrozamia were then placed in Encephalartos, a genus now restricted to African cycads.

In 1930, Charles Austin Gardner transferred it to Macrozamia in his Enumeratio Plantarum Australiae Occidentalis (p. 3). This work, a simple checklist of the flora of Western Australia, gives no description: it merely lists “M. Dyeri (F.v.M.) Gardner comb. nov.”, followed by the basionym in brackets, and treats the species as distinct from Macrozamia riedlei, under which Gardner placed Macrozamia fraseri. Two years later, J. Schuster treated it as a subspecies of Macrozamia preissii, a name that The World List of Cycads now places under Macrozamia riedlei.

The species was then sunk for a time. Lawrence Johnson (1959) records that Gardner, in a 1956 work with Bennetts, regarded all the Western Australian populations as a single species, Macrozamia riedlei, and that he himself followed Gardner, but with reservations: the only authentic material of Macrozamia dyeri he had seen was a pair of cuticle preparations from a leaflet of an apparent isotype from “Esperance Bay” kept at Kew, which showed stomata on both surfaces, and the published descriptions indicated leaflets considerably broader than any he had seen in Macrozamia riedlei. The Flora of Australia (Hill, 1998) and The World List of Cycads recognise it as a distinct species.

Etymology and history

The epithet honours Sir William Turner Thiselton-Dyer (1843–1928), English botanist and director of the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, from 1885 to 1905 (Haynes, 2022, as given by The World List of Cycads). Mueller, who corresponded with him, published the species in 1885, the year Thiselton-Dyer became director of Kew. The online Flora of Australia describes him as “assistant director of Kew Garden in 1930 when the species was described”: the date is wrong, since Thiselton-Dyer died in 1928 and the name dates from 1885.

The genus name joins the Greek makros, “large”, to the genus name Zamia.

Among the reference specimens of the Flora of Australia are collections near Condingup, on the road to Munglinup Beach, at Lake Monjingup and on the eastern slope of Mount Arid.

Common names

The Flora of Australia gives two names: “zamia palm”, an English name used in Western Australia for Macrozamia in general, and “djeeri”, a Noongar name, which the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney also uses in its Garden Explorer. The Atlas of Living Australia lists two further traditional names, “Djiridji” and “Kweneny”. Despite the resemblance, nothing indicates a link between the Noongar name and the epithet dyeri.

In the wild

Macrozamia dyeri is a locally abundant species of the south coast of Western Australia. According to the Flora of Australia, it ranges from the Munglinup River in the west to Israelite Bay in the east, and grows in coastal shrubland and heath on deep deposits of calcareous beach sand. Florabase places it in the Esperance Plains bioregion (Recherche subregion), in coastal heath without jarrah (Eucalyptus marginata), and only in the Shire of Esperance.

The climate of this coast is Mediterranean, with mainly winter rainfall; no climate record has been published for the species' habitat, and no minimum temperature.

Its relationship with the Noongar of Esperance has been studied by Alison Lullfitz and her co-authors (2020). According to the abstract of their article, the seeds of Macrozamia dyeri were a staple food of the Noongar, made safe and kept by leaching in soil or water, a technique dated archaeologically to at least 13,000 years ago. The populations lie close to surface water in the west of their range and to granite outcrops in the east, which matches the places the Noongar lived before colonisation; 75% of them are within 3.2 km of a registered Noongar site, and the arrangement of plants within populations follows the slope, aspect and landforms the Noongar favoured. The authors conclude that a mutualistic relationship with the Noongar shaped the plant's distribution, and that this history should inform its conservation.

Conservation status and regulation

Macrozamia dyeri is not threatened: the IUCN Red List classes it as Least Concern (LC), in the assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated August 2020 and published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, GBIF and Crossref, checked on 26 October 2026), and Florabase gives it as “not threatened” in Western Australia. The Atlas of Living Australia shows no listing for it under national or state threatened-species legislation.

Like all species of the genus, it is listed in CITES Appendix II and, in the European Union, in Annex B of the wildlife trade regulations (Species+, checked on 26 October 2026). International trade in plants and seeds requires permits. Buy only cultivated plants with their documents, and check the regulations in force before any purchase or import.

Growing Macrozamia dyeri

No published source describes the cultivation of Macrozamia dyeri in detail, and it is rare in collections outside Australia. Its natural habitat gives some pointers, with no guarantee of success.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Soil: deep, calcareous sands in the wild; a sandy, very free-draining soil is the basis. Judging from its habitat, this is not a plant that needs acid soil.
  • Light: open heath, without trees; a very bright position matches its habitat.
  • Water: winter rainfall and dry summers; no reliable source states its needs in cultivation. As with other Macrozamia, avoid cold combined with waterlogged soil.
  • Space: with 70 to 150 leaves over 2 m long and a trunk that can exceed 1 m across, it is a plant for a large garden.
  • Growth: not documented for the species.

Its Mediterranean-type native climate suggests gardens with dry summers, such as those of coastal California or southern Australia, but no outdoor result has been published, and the Jardin zoologique tropical in southern France has published no observation of this species.

Container cultivation

Young plants can be kept in a large, deep container filled with a sandy, very free-draining mix; an adult, with its massive trunk and leaves over 2 m long, soon outgrows any pot. No container-growing report has been published.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney lists the species in its Garden Explorer (updated on 1 October 2026), under the name “Djeeri”; the Australian Botanic Garden Mount Annan and Kings Park and Botanic Garden in Perth declare it to BGCI.
  • United States: Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden (Miami) and Lotusland (Santa Barbara) declare it to BGCI.
  • United Kingdom: the only declaration to BGCI comes from the Millennium Seed Bank at Wakehurst, a seed bank, which says nothing about a living plant.

Eight institutions in all declare it to BGCI, the others in China, the Czech Republic and Spain. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not necessarily a living plant on display today. The species does not appear in the online catalogue of the Montgomery Botanical Center or in the census of Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria. No English-language grower's report has been found.

Propagation

Propagation is by seed. The plants being male or female, both sexes and, in cultivation, hand pollination are needed to obtain fertile seed. No germination data specific to the species has been published, and no source mentions offsets.

Pests and diseases

No problem specific to the species is documented in reliable sources. In Macrozamia grown in Europe, growers' forums mainly mention scale insects and rot in cold, wet soil (see the genus page).

Cold hardiness

No dated cultivation report exists for Macrozamia dyeri, in Europe or elsewhere.

Mediterranean and European experience

No planting in the ground is documented in Europe. On the Fous de palmiers forum, a member wrote in March 2010 that the species is “given as zone 9b” and judged it probably much less hardy than Macrozamia communis, without basing this on any observation. This opinion is not a cultivation report, and no recorded cold spell concerns the species.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No cultivation result is documented elsewhere either, and no frost data has been published for its habitat. No source gives a USDA zone for the species other than the forum opinion above. Any hardiness figure would be a guess, so none is given here.

Toxicity and precautions

No analysis specific to Macrozamia dyeri has been published, but the risk is well documented in the genus and in its Western Australian relatives. Macrozamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a toxin of the liver; according to Ladd et al. (1993), cited by Asmussen (2011), both the flesh and the kernel of the related Macrozamia riedlei contain macrozamin. The young leaves cause irreversible paralysis of the hindquarters in cattle (see the genus page). Wear gloves to handle seeds, keep them out of reach of children, dogs and livestock, and contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay in case of ingestion.

Traditional uses

The seeds of Macrozamia dyeri were a staple food of the Noongar of the Esperance region, who detoxified and preserved them by leaching in soil or water, a technique dated to at least 13,000 years ago (Lullfitz et al., 2020). Elsewhere in the south-west, the Noongar prepared the flesh of Macrozamia seeds by soaking it and then burying it in pits (see the genus page). These methods rest on traditional knowledge: never eat cycad seeds.

Frequently asked questions

Where does Macrozamia dyeri grow?

Only on the south coast of Western Australia. Its range runs from the Munglinup River to Israelite Bay, around Esperance, in coastal heath on deep sand.

How big does it get?

Large, for a cycad: a trunk 0.4 to 3 m tall according to the Flora of Australia, with leaves 1.8 to 2.4 m long. Jones (1993) cites plants 6.5 m tall and 1 m across.

How can it be told from Macrozamia fraseri?

By its broader, fewer leaflets and its long, narrow male cones. Distribution also helps: Macrozamia dyeri lives around Esperance, Macrozamia fraseri to the north and around Perth.

Who is it named after?

Sir William Turner Thiselton-Dyer, director of the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, from 1885 to 1905. The name was given by Ferdinand von Mueller in 1885.

Is it hardy?

Nobody knows. No grower has published a result in cold weather, and the opinions circulating on forums rest on no measurement.

Why is it linked to Aboriginal history?

Because its seeds were a Noongar staple food for thousands of years. A 2020 study found that three-quarters of its populations lie within 3.2 km of a registered Noongar site, and concluded that people shaped its present distribution.

Is it endangered?

No. The IUCN classes it as Least Concern (2022) and Western Australia considers it not threatened. It remains protected by CITES (Appendix II).

Are its seeds poisonous?

Very probably. No analysis concerns this species, but the seeds of Macrozamia are toxic, including those of its close relative Macrozamia riedlei. Never eat them.
