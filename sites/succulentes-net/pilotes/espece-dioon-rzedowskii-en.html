Dioon rzedowskii is a large cycad endemic to northern Oaxaca, Mexico, where it grows in small groups on limestone cliffs and steep slopes in the valley of the Río Santo Domingo, around the village of San Bartolomé Ayautla. Its original description places it in semi-deciduous tropical forest, the 2020 revision of the genus in humid tropical forest. This species of the genus Dioon eventually forms a trunk 5 to 7 m tall and carries leaves nearly 2 m long, with leaflets that become smooth-edged in adult plants. Described in 1980 by Italian and Mexican botanists and named after the botanist Jerzy Rzedowski, it belongs, with Dioon spinulosum and Dioon mejiae, to the group of the largest species in the genus. Listed as Endangered by the IUCN, it remains rare in cultivation, and its cold tolerance has never been measured.

  • Natural range: Mexico, northern Oaxaca (valley of the Río Santo Domingo, around San Bartolomé Ayautla, and San Pedro Teutila on the opposite side of the canyon); 650 to 850 m according to the original description, 400 to 1,300 m according to the 2020 revision
  • IUCN status: Endangered (EN A2ac+4ac; B1ab(i,iii,iv,v)), assessment by Vovides, Chemnick and Gregory published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (annotation #4: seeds exempt); Annex B in the European Union; “in danger of extinction” (P) under the Mexican standard NOM-059-SEMARNAT-2010, category kept in its 2019 update
  • Documented hardiness: no measurement or dated report; a Californian nursery estimates the mid to upper 20s °F, about −4 to −2 °C (25 to 28 °F) (see Cold hardiness)

  • In the UK, US and Australia: Kew, the Royal Botanic Garden Edinburgh and RHS Wisley (BGCI); Montgomery Botanical Center (three accessions), Fairchild and some ten other American gardens; Royal Botanic Garden Sydney and Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria

How to recognise Dioon rzedowskii

Dioon rzedowskii is recognised by its large size, its long flat leaves bearing broad leaflets with smooth margins in adult plants, and its petiole without the spines that replace the lowest leaflets in its neighbour Dioon spinulosum. The original description by De Luca, Moretti, Sabato and Vázquez Torres (1980) gives:

  • Trunk: cylindrical, erect or reclining, 5 m or more tall and 25 to 40 cm in diameter; older plants often have reclining stems;
  • Cataphylls: densely woolly, 16 to 18 cm long and 2 cm wide at the base;
  • Leaves: flat, numerous, leathery, ascending, hairy when young and then hairless, 160 cm long or more; petiole semi-terete, 10 to 15 cm long and 3 to 4 cm wide at the base;
  • Leaflets: more than 80 on each side, nearly opposite, elongate-lanceolate but unequally tapering towards the tip, sharp-pointed, with slightly rolled margins, entire in adults, bent downwards near the tip; the lower leaflets more widely spaced, the upper ones more crowded; mid-leaf leaflets 14 to 19 cm long and 1.8 to 2.1 cm wide, with about 30 to 35 veins;
  • Female cone: ovoid-cylindrical, 60 to 75 cm long and 20 to 25 cm wide, blunt at the tip, with deltoid-ovate, densely woolly scales 10 by 5 cm; seeds ovoid-cylindrical, 5 to 6 cm by 3 cm;
  • Male cone: cylindrical, 30 to 50 cm long or more, with microsporophylls about 3 by 1.5 cm.

The authors add that young plants have spiny leaflets, and that the spines diminish with age and disappear completely in adult plants. For them, the characters unique to the species are the leaflets: more than 1 cm wide in the middle of the leaf, lanceolate but unequally tapering towards the tip, entire, and bent downwards at the tip.

The revision by Hernández-Tapia, Jiménez-Ramírez and Vovides (2020) gives close figures: plants 5 to 7 m tall; 10 to 20 leaves 160 to 200 cm long; 60 to 80 pairs of sickle-shaped leaflets, rarely overlapping, the median ones 13.2 to 19.1 cm long; male cones 30 to 35 by 8 to 12.5 cm on a short stalk; female cones on an elongated stalk; seeds with a yellow sarcotesta. It counts about a dozen veins per leaflet, against 30 to 35 in the original description, without explaining the difference. The margins of juvenile leaflets carry 1 to 16 small spines along their whole length. The most useful character is therefore the change in the leaflet margin, spiny in seedlings and smooth in adults: a young plant may look like other spiny species of the genus, which makes seedlings hard to identify without known provenance.

Hybrids

No natural or garden hybrid of Dioon rzedowskii is documented.

Possible confusion

The most likely confusion is with Dioon spinulosum, which grows in the lowland humid forests of the same region of northern Oaxaca. According to the 2020 revision, Dioon spinulosum keeps very spiny leaflets throughout its life and its basal leaflets are reduced to spines along the petiole, whereas Dioon rzedowskii loses its spines with age and lacks these basal spines. The authors of 1980 stress that the two ranges are contiguous but their habitats differ: Dioon spinulosum grows on the plains and foothills at 0 to 300 m, in the understorey of evergreen tropical forest, Dioon rzedowskii higher up, in more open places.

The third member of the group, Dioon mejiae, grows in Honduras, and provenance is usually enough to rule it out. The 1980 authors separate it by median leaflets that do not taper unequally and lower leaflets that are not markedly spaced. For a plant of unknown origin, only an adult leaf and, if possible, the cones allow a firm identification.

Taxonomy

Dioon rzedowskii is an accepted name without synonyms. Paolo De Luca, Aldo Moretti and Sergio Sabato, of the University of Naples, and Mario Vázquez Torres, of the Universidad Veracruzana, described it in 1980 in the journal Brittonia (32(2): 225–229). The type was collected by Vázquez Torres (number 2300) on 22 May 1979, below San Bartolomé Ayautla, in the district of Teotitlán (Oaxaca). The holotype is kept at the herbarium of the Universidad Veracruzana (written XALUV in the original description, XALU in The World List of Cycads), with isotypes at ENCB, FI, MEXU, NAP and NY.

In the 2020 revision, which follows the phylogeny of Dorsey and co-authors (2018), Dioon rzedowskii belongs to the Spinulosum clade with Dioon spinulosum and Dioon mejiae, the group of large, tall-trunked Dioon of humid forests. The other species of the genus fall into three clades (Edule, Western and Southern), the last containing most of the Oaxacan species. According to Dorsey and co-authors, these four clades and the living species date from the Pleistocene.

Etymology and history

The epithet honours Jerzy Rzedowski, a Polish-born botanist and specialist of the Mexican flora (The World List of Cycads, after Haynes, 2022); the authors of 1980 dedicated the species to him for his remarkable contribution to knowledge of the flora and vegetation of Mexico. Born in 1926, he died in Mexico City in 2023; his Vegetación de México (1978), the standard account of the country’s vegetation, is the one the protologue uses to describe the habitat. The name of the genus comes from the Greek dis, “two”, and ôon, “egg”, for the two seeds borne by each scale of the female cone.

The discovery is told in the account of the Naples expedition of 1979, published by Aldo Moretti in 2022. San Bartolomé Ayautla could then be reached only on foot, along some 24 km of dirt road, and the botanists were guided by a farmer who spoke only Mazatec. The population grew at the bottom of a deep canyon of the Río Santo Domingo, on a cliff so steep that the guide himself struggled to climb up to collect a few leaves and an enormous female cone. The plants did not seem to match any known species, and the new species was established after study in Naples. It is one of the six Dioon described between 1979 and 1984 by Paolo De Luca and Sergio Sabato, mostly with Mario Vázquez Torres. Its introduction into cultivation outside Mexico is not dated.

Common names

No English common name is attested; “Rzedowski’s dioon”, seen online, is not documented in any reference source. The original description states that the Mazatec people commonly call it tush-kju; the 2010 list of the Mexican standard NOM-059-SEMARNAT-2010 gives the same name, and the 2020 revision cites it after Bonta and Osborne (2007).

In the wild

Dioon rzedowskii lives in a small area of northern Oaxaca, in the basin of the Río Santo Domingo. The authors of 1980 observed it in small groups on limestone cliffs in the canyon, an hour’s walk from San Bartolomé Ayautla, in the low mountains of the Sierra de Oaxaca, between 650 and 850 m. They also record a second station, reported by Steven R. Hill near San Pedro Teutila, on the opposite side of the canyon, a short distance from the type locality. The herbarium specimens cited by the 2020 revision all come from San Bartolomé Ayautla, between 416 and 650 m, and the same revision gives a range of 400 to 1,300 m for the species.

The protologue places the habitat in the semi-deciduous tropical forest (bosque tropical subcaducifolio) of Rzedowski (1978), with a Beaucarnea, Luehea speciosa, Plumeria alba, Pseudobombax ellipticum and a Randia as the most frequent associated plants. The 2020 revision, by contrast, makes it one of the two species of the genus living in humid tropical forest in Oaxaca, in very wet regions, and Skelley, Tang and Pérez-Farrera (2022) place it in the humid forests of north-eastern Oaxaca, on steep limestone slopes, higher than Dioon spinulosum. The published sources do not explain the difference between these descriptions, and no climate figures for the habitat have been published in a verifiable source.

Like other Dioon, the species has separate male and female plants. No study of its pollination has been published; in other Dioon, the cones host specialised beetles of the genus Pharaxonotha, whose guts are filled with cycad pollen, and weevils, and Skelley and co-authors (2022) suggest that the beetles of Dioon spinulosum may also visit Dioon rzedowskii.

Conservation status and regulation

Dioon rzedowskii is listed as Endangered (EN) on the IUCN Red List, under criteria A2ac+4ac and B1ab(i,iii,iv,v): a past and projected decline of the population and a small, fragmented range. The assessment, by Andrew P. Vovides, Jeffrey Chemnick and Timothy J. Gregory, is dated 3 July 2020 and was published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, GBIF, Crossref).

Internationally, the species falls under CITES Appendix II through the listing of all cycads since 1977. Annotation #4, in its version of 5 March 2026, exempts seeds, pollen, in vitro cultures in sterile containers and cut flowers: live plants need CITES permits, seeds do not. In the European Union the species is in Annex B (Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, in force since 29 June 2026; Species+, record 18395). In Mexico, the NOM-059-SEMARNAT-2010 list of 2010 classes it as “in danger of extinction” (category P), the highest category for a species still present in the wild, and the 2019 update keeps that category. Only cultivated plants with the required CITES documents, or seed of known origin, are acceptable. Databases checked on 26 October 2026: check the regulations in force before any purchase or import.

Growing Dioon rzedowskii

No detailed cultivation report has been published for Dioon rzedowskii, and the advice below is indicative. The Californian nursery Jungle Music, in its guide to the best cycads for coastal Southern California, describes it as a slow to moderate grower reaching about 7 to 8 feet (2.1 to 2.4 m) in cultivation, moderately spined, and more tolerant of sun than Dioon spinulosum and Dioon mejiae; it also writes that the species carries leaflets down to the base of the petiole. The authors of 1980 noted that it seeks more open places than Dioon spinulosum, which agrees with that better sun tolerance.

Its habitat on limestone cliffs and slopes, in semi-deciduous or humid forest depending on the source, suggests a sharply drained soil and water during the growing season, but no source confirms this for the species. Aldo Moretti, who saw it in the wild in 1979, remarked that the position of the plants, on a sheer cliff, confirmed the need of Dioon to avoid waterlogging.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

Dated outdoor results are scarce. A grower on the Lazio coast (Italy) reports on the Tropicamente forum, at the end of December 2010, that he has planted a Dioon rzedowskii in the ground at the entrance of his garden, on very fine sand in the second row back from the sea, sheltered by houses and large pines, with a pine wood protecting it from the north wind; his cycads stand on a mound raised by almost 50 cm of very free-draining mix with a good share of humus. A few days earlier the minimum had fallen to −4.5 °C (24 °F) for a few hours, damaging figs, euphorbias and agaves, while his Macrozamia came through; the post does not say how the Dioon reacted. No observation of the species has been published for the Jardin zoologique tropical at La Londe-les-Maures, in the Var.

Container cultivation

On the same forum, a grower in Ravenna (Emilia-Romagna, Italy) showed the new leaves of his Dioon rzedowskii in July 2012, without saying whether it was in a pot or in the ground. In climates with frost, given the size of the plant, a large pot moved under cover in winter is the prudent option until its hardiness is better known.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

  • United Kingdom: the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, the Royal Botanic Garden Edinburgh and RHS Garden Wisley declare the species to BGCI PlantSearch; Edinburgh’s own online Garden Explorer did not list it in October 2026, and no outdoor cultivation is documented in Britain.
  • United States: the Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida) lists three accessions in its online catalogue, one from 2014 and one from 2020; the Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden (Miami) listed it in its online catalogue in October 2026. BGCI PlantSearch also lists Lotusland, the Huntington, San Diego Botanic Garden, the San Diego Zoo, Marie Selby Botanical Gardens, Naples Botanical Garden, the Boyce Thompson Arboretum (Arizona), the Como Park Zoo and Conservatory (Minnesota), the University of Hawaii at Hilo and the US National Plant Germplasm System. A BGCI listing is a declaration by the garden, not proof that the plant is alive today.
  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney lists the species in its online Garden Explorer and declares it to BGCI; Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria (Melbourne) also declares it to BGCI.

Propagation

No propagation data specific to Dioon rzedowskii have been published. As with other cycads, the normal route is sowing fresh seed from a pollination between male and female plants. Seeds are exempt from CITES permits (annotation #4), which makes them the simplest way to obtain the species legally. Germination time and success rate are not documented for the species.

Pests and diseases

No disease or pest specific to Dioon rzedowskii is documented. The beetles living in Dioon cones feed on pollen and are not described as pests. In cultivation, the general risks of cycads, such as root rot in waterlogged soil, probably apply, but they have not been described for this species.

Cold hardiness

The cold tolerance of Dioon rzedowskii is not known: no measurement and no dated report of a plant that has been through frost have been published. The species comes from tropical forests at low and middle elevations in southern Mexico, and nothing suggests that it can take hard frost.

Mediterranean and European experience

The only European report that links the species with a cold spell is the Lazio coast planting described above, where the minimum fell to −4.5 °C (24 °F) for a few hours in December 2010; its outcome for the Dioon is not known. The Ravenna grower, who had lost a potted Dioon edule after −7 °C (19 °F) in December 2009, does not say how he protects his Dioon rzedowskii. In France, the species appears on the Fous de palmiers forum only in a 2010 identification thread about a park plant in New Caledonia, unrelated to cold.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

  • Nursery claim: Jungle Music, in Southern California, writes that the species “is not tremendously hardy, but can tolerate into the mid to upper 20’s F”, i.e. about −4 to −2 °C (25 to 28 °F). This undated nursery estimate gives no place of measurement, plant age or growing conditions.
  • No dated report from a British, American or Australian grower has been found. The values of “−2 to −4 °C” (28 to 25 °F) and “USDA zone 9b” repeated by some websites have no other known source and are not used here.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity analysis has been published for Dioon rzedowskii, but it should be treated as toxic. Cycads contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a toxin affecting the liver and the nervous system. The risk is documented mainly in other cycads, for dogs that chew the seeds and for livestock eating the leaves. Wear gloves to handle the seeds and keep plants and seeds out of reach of children and animals. If any part has been eaten, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

According to the 2020 revision, Dioon rzedowskii has no known use other than as an ornamental. No food or ritual use specific to the species is documented, unlike some other Dioon whose seeds are eaten in Mexico and Honduras.

Frequently asked questions

Is Dioon rzedowskii hardy?

Not as far as is known. No measured result has been published, and the only estimate, from a Californian nursery, puts its limit at about −2 to −4 °C (28 to 25 °F). Outside frost-free climates, grow it in a pot moved under cover in winter.

How can it be told from Dioon spinulosum?

By the leaflet margins and the petiole. In adult Dioon rzedowskii the leaflets have smooth margins and the petiole carries no spines; in Dioon spinulosum the leaflets stay spiny all their life and the lowest ones are reduced to spines. Young plants of both species are spiny and hard to separate.

How big does it grow?

Large. In the wild the trunk reaches 5 to 7 m and 25 to 40 cm across, with leaves 1.6 to 2 m long. In cultivation, a Californian nursery speaks of about 2.1 to 2.4 m, which takes many decades given its slow growth.

Is it protected?

Yes. It is listed as Endangered by the IUCN, in CITES Appendix II, in Annex B in the European Union and as “in danger of extinction” in Mexico. Seeds are exempt from CITES permits, live plants are not.

Is it toxic?

Yes, as a precaution. There is no analysis for the species, but all cycads studied contain toxins dangerous to people and animals, especially in the seeds.

What does its name mean?

It is a tribute. The epithet rzedowskii honours Jerzy Rzedowski (1926–2023), a Polish-born botanist who devoted his career to the flora and vegetation of Mexico.
