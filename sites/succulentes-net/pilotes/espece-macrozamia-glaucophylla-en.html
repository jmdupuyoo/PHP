Macrozamia glaucophylla is a small cycad with no visible trunk from inland New South Wales, in eastern Australia, where it grows in dry woodland on deep sand, from around Gunnedah to the northern Pilliga forest, south of Narrabri. This species of the genus Macrozamia stands out by its blue or grey leaves, whose leaflets are usually forked. Long known as the “blue form” of Macrozamia heteromera, and described in 1884 as its variety glauca, it was only recognised as a species in 1998. It is very rare in cultivation, and the IUCN lists it as Least Concern.

Key facts about Macrozamia glaucophylla

  • Accepted name: Macrozamia glaucophylla D.L.Jones (1998), published in the Flora of Australia 48: 718–719, accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and the Australian Plant Census
  • Former names and synonyms: Macrozamia heteromera var. glauca C.Moore (1884); informal names Macrozamia sp. “Northern Pilliga” and “blue form” of Macrozamia heteromera (PACSOA)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: no English common name is attested
  • Natural range: Australia, inland New South Wales, from around Gunnedah to the northern Pilliga forest (the “Pilliga Scrub”), south of Narrabri
  • IUCN status: Least Concern (LC), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated September 2020 and published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union; no listing under Australian threatened-species law (Atlas of Living Australia)
  • Documented hardiness: no measured value and no cultivation report; ranked among the hardiest Macrozamia by an Australian specialist, according to a forum post (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, very open dry woodland of Callitris or eucalypts, on deep sand; in cultivation, not documented
  • Habit and adult size: underground stem 20 to 40 cm across; 2 to 8 leaves 60 to 120 cm long (up to about ten, and as many as 25 near creeks, according to the PACSOA)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: wild-collected plants of 1992 and 2022 at the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney; one accession of 2023 at the Montgomery Botanical Center (Florida); no British garden lists it
  • Toxicity: no data for the species; toxic throughout the genus (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Macrozamia glaucophylla

Among the small Macrozamia with forked leaflets, it is recognised by its longer leaves, which are always blue or grey (Flora of Australia, Hill, 1998). The measurements below come from the Flora unless stated otherwise.

  • Stem: underground, 20 to 40 cm across.
  • Leaves: 2 to 8 in the crown, 60 to 120 cm long, distinctly to moderately keeled, grey-green to blue, dull; axis slightly to moderately twisted. According to the Palm and Cycad Societies of Australia (PACSOA), the leaves, strongly recurved towards the tip, rise on average 50 to 70 cm above the ground (about 1.1 m at most); young leaves come up almost vertically, with their leaflets still furled, before opening, as in Lepidozamia peroffskyana.
  • Petiole: 12 to 25 cm, without spines: the lowest leaflets are not reduced to spines.
  • Leaflets: 90 to 120 per leaf, 16 to 25 cm long and 4 to 7 mm wide, usually divided once (segments 3 to 5 mm wide), sometimes undivided, the same colour or nearly so on both faces, with a tip that is not spiny. According to the PACSOA, a second division, giving three or even four segments, is not uncommon; the leaflets rise from the axis in a V, with a pinkish-red callus that turns creamy white with age.
  • Male cones: spindle-shaped, about 25 cm long and 5 to 6 cm wide; up to four per plant (PACSOA).
  • Female cones: ovoid, 18 to 23 cm long and 9 to 11 cm wide; often two per plant after a good year (PACSOA). According to the PACSOA, they are the largest female cones of any small Macrozamia (section Parazamia) in New South Wales.
  • Seeds: ovoid, 30 to 34 mm long and 24 to 26 mm wide; those from the north of the range are larger than the seeds of Macrozamia diplomera and Macrozamia heteromera (PACSOA).

The PACSOA describes a clear north–south variation: in the north, the plants are robust, with longer and more numerous leaves and crowded leaflets that give them a “bushy” look reminiscent of Macrozamia stenomera; in the south, they are smaller and tidier, and look more like Macrozamia heteromera. The authors call the cause of this variation puzzling.

Hybrids

No hybrid involving Macrozamia glaucophylla is documented. The hybrids that the PACSOA reports in the southern Pilliga involve two other species of the group, Macrozamia diplomera and Macrozamia polymorpha.

Possible confusion

Macrozamia glaucophylla belongs to a group of small species of inland New South Wales whose leaflets are often divided.

  • Macrozamia heteromera: smaller, with mid-green, shorter leaves. Plants from the south of the range of Macrozamia glaucophylla come close to it in size.
  • Macrozamia stenomera: leaflets divided several times, which makes identification easy according to the PACSOA, despite the bushy look shared by the two species in the northern Pilliga.
  • Macrozamia polymorpha: leaflets usually undivided; only a “very distant” relationship according to the PACSOA.
  • Young plants of other species: in 2013 and 2014, on the French Fous de palmiers forum, bluish plants first taken for Macrozamia glaucophylla were identified by a specialist as Macrozamia moorei, because their lowest leaflets were reduced to spines, which never happens in Macrozamia glaucophylla.

Taxonomy

Macrozamia glaucophylla D.L.Jones was published in 1998 in volume 48 of the Flora of Australia (pages 718–719). It is accepted by The World List of Cycads and the Australian Plant Census and belongs to the family Zamiaceae. The type (holotype in the Australian National Herbarium in Canberra, CANB; isotypes in Brisbane, Melbourne and Sydney) was collected on 9 June 1991 by D.L. and B.E. Jones (no. 7498) about 40 km south of Narrabri.

The change of name came from a revision of the Macrozamia heteromera complex. In 1884, Charles Moore described a variety glauca of Macrozamia heteromera; The World List of Cycads now treats it as a synonym of Macrozamia glaucophylla. In 1959, L.A.S. Johnson kept these plants within Macrozamia heteromera: he noted that in cultivation Moore's variety kept its rather broad, once-divided, glaucous leaflets, but that every gradation could be found in the wild and that the glaucous forms showed too little geographic or ecological cohesion to be treated as a subspecies. According to the PACSOA, David Jones revised the complex in 1998 and separated two species from it, Macrozamia glaucophylla and Macrozamia polymorpha. Enthusiasts had previously known the plant as the “blue form” of Macrozamia heteromera or under the provisional name Macrozamia sp. “Northern Pilliga”. For the reader, a blue plant bought as Macrozamia heteromera before 1998 has a good chance of being Macrozamia glaucophylla.

The PACSOA places the species in section Parazamia, that of the small species without spiny lowest leaflets. The Flora of Australia attaches it to the Macrozamia heteromera complex, defined by leaves with stomata on both faces and often divided leaflets. The phylogenomic study by Habib et al. (2022) concludes that the traditional division of the genus into sections does not reflect its evolutionary history.

Etymology and history

The name joins the Greek glauco-, “with a bluish waxy bloom”, and phyllon, “leaf”, referring to the bluish glaucous new leaves (Haynes, 2022, as given by The World List of Cycads). The genus name joins the Greek makros, “large”, to the genus name Zamia.

The PACSOA account, by Craig Thompson and Paul Kennedy, is a revision of an article by Paul Kennedy on Macrozamia heteromera published in Principes before the 1998 revision. The authors describe how hard it is to find the plants in the Pilliga, along sandy tracks where kangaroos, emus and feral pigs suddenly cross the road.

Common names

No common name is attested in English or in any other language: the scientific name is used. The phrase “blue form of Macrozamia heteromera”, still met with, is not a common name but an old usage among enthusiasts.

In the wild

Macrozamia glaucophylla lives in inland New South Wales, around Gunnedah and south to the northern Pilliga forest; it is locally abundant there, but in scattered populations, in dry woodland on deep sand (Flora of Australia, PlantNET). According to the PACSOA, it is confined to the northern part of the Pilliga State Forest, south of Narrabri. This forest covers some 380,000 ha between Coonabarabran and Narrabri and adjoins a nature reserve of 80,000 ha created in 1968.

The PACSOA describes single plants or, in places, small dense stands, under a sparse canopy of Callitris glaucophylla (white cypress pine) or Eucalyptus crebra (narrow-leaved ironbark), with Acacia. Coning is irregular, but in good years most plants in a stand cone together. Kangaroos eat the flesh of fallen seeds; possums carry them to the foot of a tree; teeth marks were attributed to feral pigs. Large fires sweep through the Pilliga: 350,000 ha in 1951, then more than 100,000 ha in 1982, 1997 and 2006.

For the climate, the PACSOA quotes Narrabri, at 212 m: 660 mm of rain a year over 64 days, a third of it in summer; winter lows of −0.8 °C (30.6 °F) and summer highs of 37.6 °C (99.7 °F), reached at least once a week in July and January; and 29 frost days a year.

Conservation status and regulation

Macrozamia glaucophylla is not threatened. The IUCN Red List classes it as Least Concern (LC), in the assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated 4 September 2020 and published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, GBIF and Crossref, checked on 26 October 2026). The Atlas of Living Australia, which compiles the national and state threatened-species lists, shows no listing for it (checked on 26 October 2026).

For international trade, like other Macrozamia, it falls under CITES Appendix II, with annotation #4, which exempts seeds among other items (listing in force on 5 March 2026), and under Annex B in the European Union (Species+, checked on 26 October 2026). Ask for a seed-raised plant and check the regulations in force before any purchase or import.

Growing Macrozamia glaucophylla

No published source describes the cultivation of Macrozamia glaucophylla, and no grower has published an account of it. The pointers below rest on its habitat and on experience with the genus.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Soil: sandy, deep and very free-draining, like the Pilliga sand.
  • Light: not documented in cultivation. In the wild, the plant grows under a sparse tree canopy (PACSOA): a bright position seems suitable.
  • Water: at the Jardin zoologique tropical in La Londe-les-Maures, in the Var (southern France), experience with the genus Macrozamia shows that watering once a week in summer, when there is no rain, is a minimum, and that frost on waterlogged soil is more dangerous than dry frost. This Mediterranean experience is useful for gardens with a similar climate, such as coastal California or southern Australia; it does not concern this species in particular.
  • Growth: very slow. According to a specialist nurseryman on the Fous de palmiers forum (2014), it takes at least 15 to 20 years to obtain an adult plant of these small Macrozamia.

Container cultivation

No container-growing report has been published. A deep pot suits the taproot of cycads, with a sandy, very free-draining mix; keeping the pot dry in winter limits the risk of rot.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney lists in its Garden Explorer (updated on 1 October 2026) a wild-collected plant accessioned in 1992 (S1992-3826), in bed 102i, and a second wild-collected accession of 2022. Mount Annan and the Australian National Botanic Gardens in Canberra also declare it to BGCI.
  • United States: the Montgomery Botanical Center, at Coral Gables in Florida, lists one accession of 2023 (20230190) in its online catalogue; the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley and Honolulu Botanical Gardens also declare it to BGCI.
  • United Kingdom: no garden declares it to BGCI, and no other source mentions it there.

Eight institutions in all declare it to BGCI, the other two in Spain (Jardí Botànic Marimurtra) and the Czech Republic. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not necessarily a living plant on display today. The species does not appear in the census of Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria. No English-language grower's report has been found.

Propagation

Propagation is by seed. A male plant, a female plant and, outside Australia, hand pollination are needed; coning is irregular. Clean the seeds wearing gloves, as the flesh is toxic. No germination data specific to the species has been published.

Pests and diseases

No pest or disease specific to the species is documented. Used to sand, the plant is mainly at risk of root rot in a mix that holds water.

Cold hardiness

The cold hardiness of Macrozamia glaucophylla is unknown: no value has been measured and no cultivation report is documented. No source gives a USDA zone for it.

Mediterranean and European experience

No trial is documented in Europe. In 2014, a member of the Fous de palmiers forum relayed the ranking of an Australian nurseryman specialising in cycads, who placed Macrozamia glaucophylla among the “hardiest” Macrozamia, those of the Coonabarabran area and the Pilliga; this ranking gives no temperature.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No result has been published. The climate of Narrabri (29 frost days a year, weekly lows of −0.8 °C (30.6 °F) in July) gives an idea of conditions in the range, without saying what the plant can withstand.

Toxicity and precautions

It should be treated as toxic, even though no data has been published for this species. Macrozamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a powerful toxin of the liver, and their leaves cause irreversible nervous damage in cattle (Healy, 1967). Keep the plant and its seeds out of reach of children, dogs and cats, wear gloves to clean seeds and, in case of ingestion, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No use specific to Macrozamia glaucophylla is documented.

Frequently asked questions

Is Macrozamia glaucophylla the “blue form” of Macrozamia heteromera?

Yes. What enthusiasts called the “blue form” of Macrozamia heteromera before 1998, or Macrozamia heteromera var. glauca, is now the species Macrozamia glaucophylla. It differs in its larger size, longer leaves and blue or grey colour.

Why are its leaflets forked?

It is a character it shares with a few related species of inland New South Wales. Its leaflets usually divide once, sometimes twice. No published source explains what this does for the plant.

Is it frost-hardy?

Nobody knows. It lives in a region with about thirty frosts a year, and an Australian specialist ranks it among the hardiest Macrozamia, but no trial has been published. Grow it in a pot first, kept dry in winter.

How can it be told from a young plant of another species?

Look at the base of the leaves. In Macrozamia glaucophylla, the lowest leaflets are never reduced to spines; in Macrozamia moorei or Macrozamia communis, they are. Forked leaflets are another good clue.

Is the species endangered?

No. The IUCN classes it as Least Concern (2022). Repeated fires are part of its Pilliga habitat, and, like all cycads, it is covered by CITES.

Is Macrozamia glaucophylla poisonous?

Yes, like all Macrozamia. The seeds and young leaves are the most dangerous parts. Keep it out of reach of children and animals, and handle the seeds with gloves.
