Cycas dharmrajii is a large cycad of the Andaman Islands, in India, described in 2017 by Lal Ji Singh, of the Botanical Survey of India, from plants in the north of the archipelago. This species of the genus Cycas is notable for a giant trunk swollen at the base that branches irregularly, and for its aerial roots. Once treated as a synonym of Cycas sainathii, itself now placed under Cycas zeylanica, it has been accepted again by The World List of Cycads since a 2020 revision; the IUCN Red List, however, still includes it in Cycas sainathii, listed as Vulnerable.

How to recognise Cycas dharmrajii

According to the abstract of the original description (Singh 2017), Cycas dharmrajii differs from all known cycads in a combination of characters:

  • a giant trunk, swollen at the base, which branches abnormally;
  • polymorphic, often branched aerial roots;
  • medium-sized leaves;
  • megasporophylls (the fertile leaves of female plants) bearing 10 to 28 pairs of well-marked, hook-shaped lateral structures, and 1 to 3 pairs of ovules;
  • two schizo-lysigenous mucilage canals in each leaflet, and characteristic pitting on the walls of the leaflet epidermal cells.

The detailed measurements (height, leaf length, cones, seeds) are given in the original article (paid access) and are not repeated here.

Hybrids

No hybrid is documented for this species.

Possible confusion

The World List of Cycads accepts three species in the Andaman Islands. The characters below come from their published descriptions:

  • Cycas zeylanica, a coastal cycad of Sri Lanka and the Andamans: trunk 2.3 to 3.1 m tall, barely swollen at the base; sterile blade of the megasporophylls lanceolate and obscurely toothed (6 to 12 small teeth no more than 2 mm long), according to Lindström and Hill (2007).
  • Cycas pschannae, the other large cycad of the archipelago: a tree up to 12 m tall, with a trunk not swollen at the base; sterile part of the megasporophylls triangular, with two unequal, horn-shaped lateral teeth at its base, according to its original description (Srivastava and Singh 2015).
  • Cycas dharmrajii: a giant trunk swollen at the base and unusually branched; megasporophylls with 10 to 28 pairs of hook-shaped lateral structures (abstract of Singh 2017).

The keys to the cycads of the archipelago published by Singh (2017) and by Singh and Misra (2020) are not reproduced here, and no further distinguishing character is given.

Taxonomy

Cycas dharmrajii is accepted by The World List of Cycads; the World Checklist of Vascular Plants (WCVP), on which POWO is based, also accepts it, for the Andaman Islands (consulted through GBIF on 31 October 2026). Lal Ji Singh, of the Andaman and Nicobar Regional Centre of the Botanical Survey of India, described it in January 2017 in the Nordic Journal of Botany (volume 35, pages 69 to 76), with a key to the Cycas of the archipelago.

Its status has changed over time. The World List of Cycads first treated it as a synonym of Cycas sainathii, another name described from the Andamans in 2014, which the list now places under Cycas zeylanica. It was reinstated as a species following the revision of the cycads of the Andaman and Nicobar Islands published in 2020 by Lal Ji Singh and Dharm Raj Misra, of the University of Allahabad, in the Indian Journal of Forestry (volume 43, pages 46 to 57). According to its abstract, that paper places Cycas andamanica under Cycas pschannae, Cycas darshii under Cycas sphaerica and Cycas sainathii under Cycas zeylanica, and gives a key to the species of the archipelago, but it says nothing about Cycas dharmrajii, so the arguments for its reinstatement cannot be set out here. The published sources do not say in which section of the genus the species belongs.

The IUCN Red List has not followed this treatment: in its assessment published in 2022, it accepts Cycas sainathii in a broad sense and includes Cycas dharmrajii, Cycas pschannae and Cycas andamanica in it as synonyms (Red List dataset relayed by GBIF, July 2026 version). The same Andaman plant may therefore be labelled under different names depending on the database consulted.

Etymology and history

The epithet honours Dharm Raj Misra, professor in the Department of Botany of the University of Allahabad, in India, for his “inspiration” and his “outstanding contribution to the knowledge of gymnosperms”, in the words of the original description as cited by The World List of Cycads.

The type was collected by Lal Ji Singh on 26 December 2013 at Table Excelsior, near Diglipur, in North Andaman, at 12 m above sea level (L.J. Singh 29600, holotype at the Central National Herbarium, Calcutta (CAL); isotype at Port Blair (PBL)). The description was reported in 2017 by the Indian press, together with that of Cycas pschannae (GKToday, 2017).

Common names

No common name is attested for this species, in English or in any language of the Andaman Islands.

In the wild

According to The World List of Cycads, Cycas dharmrajii is known only from the Andaman Islands; the type comes from the Diglipur area, in North Andaman, at low altitude. Its habitat, the extent of its populations, its pollinator and its phenology are not documented, and no herbarium record of the species is published through GBIF.

Conservation status and regulation

Its status depends on the name used. For The World List of Cycads, which accepts the species, Cycas dharmrajii has not been evaluated (NE). The IUCN Red List, on the other hand, includes it in Cycas sainathii, listed as Vulnerable (VU) in L. J. Singh’s assessment published in 2022 (version 2022-1), according to the Red List dataset relayed by GBIF (checked again on 21 October 2026). The criteria and threats of that assessment are not given in the GBIF data. The 2017 original description also proposes a conservation status, but that category is not reported here (paid-access article). No national protection specific to the species is documented.

Like every species in the genus except Cycas beddomei, Cycas dharmrajii is listed in CITES Appendix II, through the listing of the family Cycadaceae, included since 4 February 1977. The listing carries annotation #4 (CoP20 version, in force since 5 March 2026): seeds, pollen, seedlings or tissue cultures obtained in vitro and transported in sterile containers, and cut flowers of artificially propagated plants, are not subject to CITES, whereas international trade in the plants themselves requires an export permit. Australia, the United Kingdom and the United States apply CITES through their own national legislation; in the European Union the species is in Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, whose annexes in force are set by Commission Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026. Species+, the official CITES database, does not yet list this name, published in 2017 (search of 3 October 2026): protection follows from the listing of the whole family, which covers every species, named or not. This general information does not replace the official texts: check the rules in force before buying or exchanging plants, and ask for proof that the plant was raised in cultivation.

Growing Cycas dharmrajii

In short: Cycas dharmrajii is not documented in cultivation anywhere, and nothing indicates that it is in the trade. Coming from a tropical archipelago, it would in all likelihood need tropical conditions or a heated glasshouse; this is a deduction from its origin, not documented information.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

No institution has published growing advice, and no grower’s report has been published on the English, French or Italian forums. In theory it belongs in frost-free tropical gardens, such as those of South Florida, Hawaii or northern Australia, but no planting is documented there.

Container cultivation

In the United Kingdom and other temperate regions, a plant of this species would need a large pot in a heated glasshouse, given its giant size in the wild; again, this is a deduction, not a growing result. The Jardin zoologique tropical at La Londe-les-Maures, in the south of France, has published no observation on Cycas dharmrajii, so no Mediterranean experience can be passed on.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

No record of Cycas dharmrajii is documented in BGCI PlantSearch, in the online catalogues of Montgomery Botanical Center (Florida), the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney and the Australian Botanic Garden Mount Annan, or in the online catalogues of British, American and Australian botanic gardens. No report from a private grower in these countries has been published.

Propagation

Like all Cycas, Cycas dharmrajii is dioecious: a male and a female plant are needed for fertile seed. No germination data specific to the species have been published. Seeds are exempt from CITES under annotation #4.

Pests and diseases

One pest is documented on the species in the wild. Purti, Singh and Pandey (2022) recorded for the first time in the Andaman and Nicobar Islands the cycad blue butterfly, Luthrodes pandava, a small butterfly whose caterpillars attack all the aerial parts of cycads; they name Cycas dharmrajii and Cycas pschannae as new host plants and consider the endemic cycads of the archipelago more susceptible than introduced species. No disease specific to the species has been published. In cultivation, cycad scales, including the Asian cycad scale, Aulacaspis yasumatsui, can affect all species.

Cold hardiness

No cold-tolerance data exist for Cycas dharmrajii. It is a cycad of the Andaman Islands, in the tropics, and nothing allows us to say that it tolerates frost.

European experience

No growing result has been published in Europe, in the open ground or in a pot. No growing report is documented for this species, and no dated frost event concerns it.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No planting, cold reading or growing report is documented. No USDA zone can be given.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity analysis specific to Cycas dharmrajii has been published. The risk is, however, well documented in the genus: Cycas tissues, and the seeds above all, contain cycasin, which is converted during digestion into methylazoxymethanol, toxic to the liver and nervous system, along with a neurotoxic amino acid, BMAA. North Carolina State University notes that cycasin is present in all parts of the plant, and the ASPCA lists the sago palm (Cycas revoluta) as poisonous to dogs, cats and horses, with vomiting, sometimes bloody diarrhoea, jaundice and liver damage that can be fatal. Until proven otherwise, treat the plant as poisonous: wear gloves to handle the seeds and their fleshy coat, pick up fallen seeds, and keep plants and seeds out of reach of children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet straight away.

Traditional uses

Frequently asked questions

Where does Cycas dharmrajii come from?

From the Andaman Islands, in India: the type was collected near Diglipur, in North Andaman, at 12 m above sea level.

Is it a synonym of Cycas sainathii?

It depends on the source. The World List of Cycads has accepted it as a species since the 2020 revision of the archipelago’s cycads, whereas the IUCN Red List still includes it in Cycas sainathii in a broad sense.

Who is it named after?

Dharm Raj Misra, professor of botany at the University of Allahabad, in recognition of his contribution to the study of gymnosperms.

Is it endangered?

Probably. It has not been evaluated under its own name, but the Red List includes it in Cycas sainathii, listed as Vulnerable in 2022, and a pest butterfly was recorded on it in the wild in 2022.

How can it be told from Cycas pschannae?

By its trunk and its megasporophylls. According to the published descriptions, Cycas dharmrajii has a giant trunk swollen at the base and megasporophylls with many hook-shaped lateral structures, whereas Cycas pschannae has a trunk not swollen at the base and megasporophylls with two horn-shaped teeth.

Can it be grown outdoors in the UK?

No. Nothing indicates that it tolerates frost, and it is not documented in cultivation at all.

Is it poisonous?

It must be assumed so. It has not been studied, but all parts of Cycas are poisonous, especially the seeds.
