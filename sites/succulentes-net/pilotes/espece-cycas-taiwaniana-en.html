Cycas taiwaniana is a trunk-forming cycad of southern China, grown for centuries in the gardens of Guangdong and Fujian and now very rare in the wild. This member of the genus Cycas has a misleading name: the English botanist William Carruthers described it in 1893 in the belief that it came from Taiwan, where it does not grow; the cycad of Taiwan is Cycas taitungensis, long wrongly called Cycas taiwaniana. Since 2021, the reference checklist, The World List of Cycads, has also attached to it the cycads of Hainan Island described as Cycas hainanensis, Cycas lingshuiensis (published with the spelling “lingshuigensis”) and Cycas shanyaensis, but not Cycas changjiangensis, which it still accepts as a species. The IUCN lists it as Endangered.

Key facts about Cycas taiwaniana

  • Accepted name: Cycas taiwaniana Carruth. (1893), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15); POWO could not be consulted directly
  • Former names and synonyms: Cycas revoluta var. taiwaniana (Carruth.) J.Schust.   • IUCN status: Endangered (EN); assessment by K. D. Hill (2010, criteria A2cd+4cd), then a reassessment by J. D. Bösenberg published in version 2023-1
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II; Annex B in the European Union; in China, first-class state protection for all Cycas
  • Documented hardiness: no verifiable data; the reports published in France under this name concern plants of disputed identity (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, partial shade along streams on non-calcareous soils (mainland plants, Hill 2008); moist forest on limestone or volcanic soils on Hainan
  • Habit and adult size: trunk up to 3.5 m tall and 15 to 30 cm across; leaves 1.5 to 3 m long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Oxford and Birmingham (BGCI) in the UK; Montgomery Botanical Center (3 accessions under the name Cycas hainanensis, 1993 to 2000) and about ten other gardens in BGCI in the US; Royal Botanic Garden Sydney (wild-origin plant from China, 1993) and four other gardens in Australia; identities not checked (see In cultivation)
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; treat it as poisonous, like all Cycas (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Cycas taiwaniana

It is recognised by its robust habit, with a true aerial trunk and a large crown of leaves, and by its rather small seeds. According to Hill (2008), these are the characters that set it apart among the Chinese cycads with soft male cones; its megasporophylls usually carry a clearly distinct, broad terminal spine, and its leaflets, fairly narrow but very variable, are clearly twisted where they join the rachis. Hill’s (2008) description runs as follows:

  • Trunk: aerial or underground, up to 3.5 m tall and 15 to 30 cm across; 12 to 30 leaves in the crown.
  • Leaves: dark green, very glossy, 1.5 to 3 m long, flat or barely keeled, with 140 to 300 leaflets; an orange or brown felt that falls as the leaf opens; rachis ending in a pair of leaflets; hairless petiole 40 to 150 cm (30 to 60% of the leaf), spiny along its whole length.
  • Median leaflets: 18 to 44 cm by 9 to 16 mm, clearly two-toned, spaced 10 to 24 mm apart, flat, with slightly recurved margins and a fine, sharp tip; midrib raised on the upper side.
  • Cataphylls (the scales that protect the growing point): narrowly triangular, prickly, hairy to densely woolly, 8.5 to 13 cm.
  • Male cones: narrowly egg-shaped to spindle-shaped, yellow, 30 to 45 cm by 8 to 10 cm; soft microsporophylls, 2 to 3 cm, without an apical spine.
  • Megasporophylls (the fertile leaves of female plants): 15 to 22 cm, with brown felt, carrying 2 to 6 hairless ovules; rounded blade 7 to 12 cm by 5.5 to 7 cm, deeply comb-like, with 24 to 52 soft lateral spines of 2 to 4.2 cm; terminal spine distinct or not, 2 to 4.5 cm long and 7 to 30 mm wide at the base.
  • Seeds: almost round to egg-shaped, 28 to 36 mm by 20 to 30 mm; yellow fleshy coat (sarcotesta) without bloom or fibrous layer; warty shell (sclerotesta).

The Flora of China (Chen and Stevenson 1999) gives somewhat different measurements, because it applies the name to a broader group of plants (see Taxonomy): a trunk up to 3.5 m by 35 cm, 60 to 90 leaves of 1.5 to 3 m, 70 to 150 pairs of often arching leaflets 11 to 14 mm wide, more than 30 megasporophylls per cone with a rhomboid blade whose terminal lobe usually has a finely toothed edge, and seeds of 2.8 to 3.3 cm. It places pollination in April and May and seed ripening from September to November.

Hybrids

No natural or horticultural hybrid has been described. A genomic study of six southern Chinese cycads (Tao and colleagues 2021) did, however, detect fairly strong signals of ancient hybridisation involving Cycas dolichophylla, Cycas szechuanensis and the common ancestor of Cycas taiwaniana and Cycas changjiangensis. This is evolutionary history, not hybrids you might find in cultivation.

Possible confusion

The most frequent confusion, and the one with the heaviest consequences, concerns the cycad of Taiwan:

  • Cycas taitungensis, from the mountains of south-eastern Taiwan, was known for decades under the misapplied name Cycas taiwaniana, until it was described in 1994 (Hill 2008). It belongs to another section of the genus, close to Cycas revoluta: downy ovules, red seeds with a shell ribbed lengthwise, a short petiole (15 to 20 cm) and leaflets only 6 to 8 mm wide;
  • Cycas szechuanensis, from Guangdong and Fujian, is its closest relative, with which Cycas taiwaniana forms the “Cycas taiwaniana complex”; according to Hill (2008), its megasporophyll blade has no broadened terminal spine (at most 8 mm wide at the base) and its seeds are larger;
  • the Hainan cycads, Cycas hainanensis and Cycas lingshuiensis, now attached to Cycas taiwaniana, differed from it according to Hill (2008) in more keeled leaves with narrower, more crowded leaflets, a smaller megasporophyll blade with fewer but longer lateral spines, and clearly larger seeds; Cycas changjiangensis, a dwarf plant of western Hainan, remains a separate species for The World List of Cycads;
  • Cycas revoluta, by far the most widely grown, has much narrower, stiff leaflets with rolled-under margins.

In practice, a plant sold as “Cycas taiwaniana” is not necessarily a true Cycas taiwaniana: only the seeds (yellow in this species, red in Cycas taitungensis and Cycas revoluta) and the megasporophylls settle the question.

Taxonomy

Cycas taiwaniana is accepted by The World List of Cycads; POWO could not be consulted. William Carruthers described it in 1893 in the Journal of Botany (31: 1–3, plate 331), from specimens collected by Robert Swinhoe and kept in the herbarium of Henry F. Hance, then acquired by the British Museum; the holotype is in London (BM), an isotype at Kew (K). The species belongs to section Stangerioides, the group of Chinese and Indochinese cycads with soft male cones, a sarcotesta without a fibrous layer and a warty shell (Hill 2008; Zheng and colleagues 2017).

The content of the species has varied a great deal:

  • Schuster (1932) made it a variety of Cycas revoluta, and the Chinese floras of the 1970s included the Taiwanese plants, separated only in 1994 as Cycas taitungensis (Hill 2008);
  • the Flora of China (1999) treated it as a broad species of Guangdong, eastern Guangxi, south-western Hunan and south-eastern Yunnan, with Cycas diannanensis, Cycas fairylakea and a subspecies of Cycas pectinata as synonyms;
  • Hill (2008) restricted it to the mainland plants, and placed those of Hainan under Cycas hainanensis;
  • Feng and colleagues (2021, Taxon) applied three delimitation methods based on molecular data to the six taxa of the complex: they recognise only two species, Cycas szechuanensis (with Cycas fairylakea) and Cycas taiwaniana, which absorbs Cycas changjiangensis, Cycas hainanensis and Cycas lingshuiensis. The World List of Cycads has followed this treatment, except for Cycas changjiangensis, which it still accepts.

This site follows The World List of Cycads: Cycas changjiangensis keeps its own page, while the page on Cycas hainanensis describes the Hainan plant under the name still widely used for it. In China, by contrast, the 2021 national list of protected wild plants treats Cycas changjiangensis as part of Cycas taiwaniana (Li and colleagues 2024).

The result is paradoxical: the current concept of the species, centred on the wild populations of Hainan, is almost the reverse of Hill’s (2008), which excluded precisely the Hainan plants. The databases are not aligned: The World List of Cycads gives Fujian and Guangdong as its range while including the Hainan synonyms, and Species+, the CITES database, still lists Taiwan among its range countries (consulted on 13 October 2026).

Etymology and history

The epithet refers to Taiwan, where the species was believed to come from (Haynes 2022, cited by The World List of Cycads). Hill (2008) has traced the origin of the mistake. Robert Swinhoe, British consul at Amoy (now Xiamen, in Fujian), sent Joseph Dalton Hooker at Kew a megasporophyll on 21 August 1867 with a letter: he described it as “a botanical caricature of a pelican”, which the Chinese called “hai-te-koe”, “sea iron bird”, and used to scent tea; the plant was so rare locally that he had not been able to find out how it grew. Later the same year he sent more complete material to Hance and to Hooker. On the herbarium sheet studied by Carruthers, a note not in Swinhoe’s hand, perhaps Hance’s, reads “Ex insula Formosa”, that is, “from the island of Formosa”: hence the name. Swinhoe never stated where the specimens had been collected.

In 1902 William Thiselton-Dyer reported that consul G. Phillips had found Swinhoe’s plant again in 1883 “on the hills 60 miles from Swatow” (now Shantou, in Guangdong), that he had never seen it in Taiwan, and that it was much sought after as a garden plant on the mainland. The records of Cycas taiwaniana in Taiwan stem from the misapplication of this name to the island’s cycad (Hill 2008).

Common names

No English common name is attested. The Flora of China gives the Chinese name 台湾苏铁, literally “Taiwan cycad”, which reflects the same mistake; oddly, it transcribes it as “guang dong su tie”, “Guangdong cycad”. Hill (2008), after Walters and Yang (1994) and Bonta and Osborne (2007), also cites feng wei cao and feng wei jiao (“phoenix-tail herb” and “phoenix-tail palm”), hai-tie-ou, Guangdong su-tie and Taiwan su-tie; in 1867 Swinhoe transcribed the local name as “hai-te-koe”. The Royal Botanic Garden Sydney labels its plants “Hai nan su-tie”.

In the wild

The range of the species depends on the concept adopted. For Hill (2008), Cycas taiwaniana was widespread but scattered in Fujian, Guangdong, Guangxi, Guizhou and Hunan; long cultivated in Guangdong and Fujian, it was known mainly from cultivated plants and had become extremely rare in the wild. The wild plants from which several of its specimens came grew in partial shade along streams, on non-calcareous soils. The Flora of China places it in sunny, grassy places or open mixed forests, often disturbed, between 400 and 1,100 m.

For recent genetic studies, on the contrary, the species is native to Hainan Island (Feng and colleagues 2021). Wu and colleagues (2022) sampled twelve wild populations on Hainan, mainly around the Yingge and Wuzhi mountains, together with a cultivated population in Fujian descended from one of the island’s genetic groups. They found fairly low genetic diversity, strong differentiation between populations and three distinct groups, to be protected as separate conservation units; the cultivated population is less diverse than the wild ones, which in their view shows the limits of conservation in cultivation. The history of the populations is marked by two phases of contraction, in the Pleistocene and the Holocene, followed by recent expansion, linked to climate and sea-level fluctuations. Hill (2008) described the Hainan plants, then Cycas hainanensis, in the wettest parts of the east of the island, from sea level to about 1,200 m, on limestone or volcanic soils, in moist forest receiving about 2,000 mm of rain a year.

Conservation status and regulation

The IUCN lists Cycas taiwaniana as Endangered (EN). The assessment by K. D. Hill (2010) rested on criteria A2cd+4cd, that is, a past and projected population decline linked to habitat loss and collecting (The World List of Cycads). A reassessment by J. D. Bösenberg was published in version 2023-1 (reference checked on Crossref); the Red List data relayed by GBIF still give EN (consulted on 21 October 2026), and list Cycas hainanensis, Cycas lingshuigensis and Cycas shanyaensis as synonyms. The Red List itself could not be consulted. Hill (2008) recalled that the species had been greatly reduced over the centuries by collecting and habitat destruction, and that the 1997 Red List wrongly attributed Hainan and Taiwan to it. On Hainan, according to the work cited by Wu and colleagues (2022), the threats are habitat destruction, human disturbance and illegal trade; in China, all Cycas are on the list of first-class protected plants.

Like every species in the genus except Cycas beddomei, Cycas taiwaniana is covered by CITES Appendix II, where the family Cycadaceae has been listed since 4 February 1977. The listing carries annotation #4 (version in force since 5 March 2026): seeds, pollen, and seedlings or tissue cultures obtained in vitro in sterile containers are exempt, while international trade in plants requires an export permit. In the European Union, the species is listed in Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, in the version set by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026; Species+ confirms this status (consulted on 13 October 2026).

This information does not replace the official texts: check the regulations in force before any purchase or exchange, ask the seller for proof of origin, and prefer seed-grown plants raised in cultivation.

Growing Cycas taiwaniana

Cycas taiwaniana has been grown in China for a very long time: by the nineteenth century it was much sought after in mainland gardens (Phillips, cited by Hill 2008), and most known specimens come from plants grown in Guangdong and Fujian, at temples, schools or forestry institutes. Yet no institution has published growing recommendations specific to the species.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

No result in the ground that can be attributed with confidence to the species has been published outside China. In France, the name appears on the Fous de palmiers forum mainly between 2011 and 2015, about plants sold as “Cycas taiwaniana” by a Spanish seller on an auction site. Their identity was disputed from the start: a cycad specialist on the forum saw them in 2014 as Cycas taitungensis, then in 2015, after consulting professionals, more probably as hybrids of Cycas revoluta (with Cycas panzhihuaensis or Cycas taitungensis), pointing out that the true Cycas taiwaniana has much broader leaflets and yellow seeds. The growing reports published under this name therefore cannot be attributed to the species (see Cold hardiness). The Jardin zoologique tropical at La Londe-les-Maures has published no observations on it.

Container cultivation

In Europe, the only presence documented in a botanic garden is under glass: in 2018 the new tropical glasshouse of the University of Pisa botanic garden held Cycas circinalis and Cycas taiwaniana (gonews.it, 23 March 2018), without details of the plants’ origin. No pot-culture report specific to the species was found; given its size, a large container and a frost-free winter are prudent choices.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

Because the name was applied for decades to Cycas taitungensis, plants labelled Cycas taiwaniana in collections may belong to that species; only seed colour and the megasporophylls can confirm them. The listings below are therefore presences of the name, not checked identities.

  • United Kingdom: Oxford Botanic Garden and Birmingham Botanical Gardens list it in BGCI PlantSearch; this shows a declaration, not necessarily a living plant, and it would be grown under glass.
  • United States: the online catalogue of Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida) lists three accessions under the name Cycas hainanensis, whose numbers date them to 1993, 1995 and 2000. BGCI PlantSearch lists the species, under Cycas taiwaniana or its Hainan synonyms, in about ten other gardens, among them Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden, The Huntington, Ganna Walska Lotusland, San Diego Botanic Garden, the San Diego Zoo, Brooklyn Botanic Garden, the Como Park Conservatory (Minnesota) and several Hawaiian gardens (Honolulu Botanical Gardens, the National Tropical Botanical Garden, Waimea Valley).
  • Australia: the online catalogue of the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney (updated on 1 October 2026), which gives Cycas hainanensis as a synonym, lists a plant of wild origin from China (S1993-0795/1) in bed 51f and a 2021 accession of garden origin (S2021-0661, four plants). BGCI adds Brisbane Botanic Gardens Mount Coot-tha, Noosa Botanic Gardens, the Cairns Botanic Gardens and the System Garden of the University of Melbourne.

No English-language growing report with a date, a place and a temperature was found.

Propagation

The plant is dioecious: a male and a female plant are needed to obtain fertile seed. Wu and colleagues (2022) even recommend hand pollination in some wild populations on Hainan, to limit inbreeding. No germination data specific to the species have been published. Seeds are exempt from CITES under annotation #4; ask for seed from cultivated plants, and check its colour when ripe: yellow in Cycas taiwaniana, red in Cycas taitungensis.

Pests and diseases

No pest or disease specific to the species is documented in the sources consulted. Like all cycads, it can be attacked by the cycad scale, Aulacaspis yasumatsui, and compost that stays wet for long periods encourages root rot. A Taiwanese survey (Chang 1989) of the cycad blue butterfly, Chilades pandava, concerned seedlings named Cycas taiwaniana in a Taipei nursery in 1981: given how the name was used in Taiwan at that time, these were very probably the cycad of Taiwan, Cycas taitungensis (a deduction).

Cold hardiness

No verifiable data exist on the cold tolerance of Cycas taiwaniana. Figures circulating under this name should also be treated with caution: for decades, the cycad of Taiwan, now Cycas taitungensis, was called Cycas taiwaniana (Hill 2008), and some hardiness statements published under this name may concern it. This is a deduction that no source checks case by case.

European experience

No reliable result has been found. The two reports of growing in the ground published on the Fous de palmiers forum concern plants whose identity was questioned on the forum itself; they are mentioned here for that reason, not as data on the species. On the Côte Bleue, west of Marseille, a grower reports in May 2011 that his plant, planted out the year before and left unprotected, had its foliage slightly damaged, like his Cycas revoluta, after a minimum of −5 °C (23 °F) during winter 2010–2011, and made new leaves earlier than they did in spring; he said it came from a reputable supplier who guaranteed its identity, but another member saw it rather as a hybrid of Cycas revoluta. At Viols-le-Fort (Hérault), another grower reports in April 2014 that his plant bought from the Spanish seller, in the ground and wrapped in horticultural fleece, turned yellow during winter 2013–2014, when the minimum had been −5 °C (23 °F) in early December, then made new leaves from the end of March. The Jardin zoologique tropical has published no observations, so no Mediterranean guide can be drawn from it.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No verifiable cold value has been found in these countries. The listings in subtropical gardens in Florida, Hawaii, southern California and Queensland say nothing about its limits. In the UK, grow it in a pot under frost-free glass.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity analysis specific to Cycas taiwaniana has been found, but the risk is well documented in the genus: the tissues of Cycas, and above all their seeds, contain cycasin, which digestion converts into methylazoxymethanol, toxic to the liver and nervous system, along with a neurotoxic amino acid, BMAA. The ASPCA lists Cycas revoluta among plants toxic to dogs, cats and horses, with vomiting, diarrhoea and liver damage that can be fatal. In the absence of evidence to the contrary, treat the plant as poisonous: keep seeds and leaves out of reach of children and pets, and wear gloves to clean seeds. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet straight away.

Traditional uses

The only documented use is the one Swinhoe reported in 1867: at Amoy, the fertile leaves called “hai-te-koe” were used to scent tea (Hill 2008). Where these plants came from is unknown, and this use does not prove that the plant is safe. The main use of the species in China remains ornamental.

Frequently asked questions

Why is it called taiwaniana if it does not grow in Taiwan?

Because of a labelling mistake. The specimens studied by Carruthers in 1893 bore the note “Ex insula Formosa” (from the island of Formosa, that is, Taiwan), written in a different hand from the collector’s; the species actually lives in southern China.

So which is the cycad of Taiwan?

Cycas taitungensis, described in 1994 for the plant of the mountains of the south-east of the island, which until then had been wrongly called Cycas taiwaniana. It is recognised in particular by its red seeds.

Are Cycas hainanensis and Cycas taiwaniana the same species?

Yes, according to the current taxonomy of The World List of Cycads, which follows Feng and colleagues (2021). Hill (2008), on the contrary, saw two species, one from Hainan and one from the mainland.

Is Cycas changjiangensis a synonym of Cycas taiwaniana?

No, for The World List of Cycads, which still accepts it as a species, unlike Feng and colleagues (2021) and the Chinese list of protected plants, which include it in Cycas taiwaniana.

What is the “Cycas taiwaniana complex”?

A group of six names for very similar cycads of southern China. The 2021 molecular study recognises only two species in it: Cycas taiwaniana and Cycas szechuanensis.

How big does it grow?

It is a trunk-forming cycad. The trunk can reach 3.5 m and carries a crown of leaves 1.5 to 3 m long.

Is it endangered?

Yes. The IUCN lists it as Endangered; it has become very rare in the wild and is protected in China.

Is it hardy in the UK?

Nobody knows. No verifiable data exist, and the reports published under this name concern plants of doubtful identity or, perhaps, Cycas taitungensis: in the UK, keep it under frost-free glass.
