Macrozamia humilis, the Inverell Cycad, is a small cycad with no visible trunk from New South Wales, in eastern Australia, known from a single locality near Inverell, on the state's North West Slopes, where it grows in low, dry woodland on sandy soil over granite. It is the most threatened species of the genus Macrozamia: the only one listed as Critically Endangered on the IUCN Red List. Described as recently as 1998, it is still rare in collections, and no cultivation trial has been published.

Key facts about Macrozamia humilis

  • Accepted name: Macrozamia humilis D.L.Jones (1998), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and the Australian Plant Census
  • Former names and synonyms: none
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales; Macrozamia heteromera complex
  • Common names: Inverell Cycad (New South Wales Government)
  • Natural range: endemic to New South Wales, Australia: a single known population near Inverell (New England Tablelands bioregion)
  • IUCN status: Critically Endangered (CR B1ab(iii)), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated 15 October 2020 and published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union; Endangered in New South Wales since 2002 (Biodiversity Conservation Act 2016); not listed under the federal EPBC Act
  • Documented hardiness: no cultivation data; the nearest weather station, Inverell, has recorded −5.5 °C (22.1 °F) and has about ten frosts a year (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, low, dry woodland on sandy soil over acid granite capped with laterite; no published cultivation advice
  • Habit and adult size: underground stem 18 to 28 cm across; 2 to 7 leaves 35 to 65 cm long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Royal Botanic Garden Sydney (plants of wild origin received in 2022) and Australian Botanic Garden Mount Annan; Montgomery Botanical Center in Florida; no British garden
  • Toxicity: not documented for the species; seeds and leaves toxic throughout the genus (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Macrozamia humilis

Macrozamia humilis is recognised by its few short leaves, dull green or greyish, carrying relatively few leaflets: these are the features the Flora of Australia (Hill, 1998) retains to tell it apart. The Flora of Australia and the New South Wales flora online (PlantNET) give the following description.

  • Stem: underground, 18 to 28 cm across.
  • Leaves: 2 to 7 in the crown, 35 to 65 cm long, moderately keeled, mid- to dark green or grey-green, dull to semi-glossy; petiole 9 to 16 cm long; axis not twisted or moderately twisted in a spiral.
  • Leaflets: 60 to 90 per leaf, simple or, rarely, forked, 10 to 18 cm long and 4 to 7 mm wide (segments 2 to 3 mm wide when forked), distinctly paler beneath, with an entire, non-spiny tip; the lowest leaflets are not reduced to spines.
  • Male cones: spindle-shaped, 14 to 19 cm long and 4.5 to 5.5 cm across, the scales ending in a spine 1 to 10 mm long.
  • Female cones: ovoid, 10 to 15 cm long and 6 to 8 cm across, the scales ending in a spine 2 to 25 mm long.
  • Seeds: ovoid, 25 to 31 mm long and 22 to 26 mm wide, with red flesh.

The New South Wales Government profile sums it up for the non-specialist: a plant that looks like a short-trunked palm, with stiff green to grey-green fronds up to 65 cm long and large red seeds.

Hybrids

No natural or garden hybrid involving Macrozamia humilis has been documented. As the species is known from a single population, isolated from other Macrozamia, the question hardly arises in the wild.

Possible confusion

The most likely confusion is with the other members of the complex of Macrozamia heteromera, to which the Flora of Australia attaches Macrozamia humilis. These small, trunkless cycads of inland New South Wales all have leaves without spines at the base and leaflets that are sometimes forked. Macrozamia humilis differs first by its shorter leaves, dull or greyish, and by its smaller number of leaflets. Away from its only site, a plant of unknown origin cannot be identified with certainty.

Taxonomy

Macrozamia humilis D.L.Jones is accepted by The World List of Cycads and the Australian Plant Census. David L. Jones described it in 1998 in volume 48 of the Flora of Australia (p. 719, with the description on p. 659). The type was collected near Inverell on 16 May 1992 by D.L. Jones and B.E. Jones (no. 9453); the holotype is held at the Australian National Herbarium in Canberra (CANB), with isotypes in Melbourne (MEL) and Sydney (NSW).

The species has no synonym and has never changed its name. It belongs to the complex of Macrozamia heteromera, a group of small species of inland New South Wales, several of which were described in the same treatment of 1998. A 2003 article by B. and S. Ironmonger in the Cycad Newsletter dealt with the taxonomy, distribution and cultivation of this group (cited by The World List of Cycads).

Etymology and history

The epithet is the Latin humilis, “humble” or “low”, referring to the short leaves (Haynes, 2022, cited by The World List of Cycads). The genus name Macrozamia combines the Greek makros, “large”, with the genus name Zamia. The circumstances of the discovery of the population have not been published.

The New South Wales Scientific Committee listed the species as Endangered in December 2002, four years after its description. Its arrival in botanic gardens is recent: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney received plants of wild origin in 2022 (accession S2022-0085), and Montgomery Botanical Center in Florida records seven accessions numbered in 2023.

Common names

In English, the New South Wales Government calls it the “Inverell Cycad” in its threatened species profile, and the Atlas of Living Australia uses the same name. No Aboriginal name specific to the species is recorded, and no name is attested in other languages.

In the wild

Macrozamia humilis is known from a single locality near Inverell, on the North West Slopes of New South Wales, in the New England Tablelands bioregion (subregions of the Eastern Nandewars and the Tingha Plateau). The Flora of Australia, which calls it “an isolated and probably rare species”, and PlantNET describe its habitat as low, dry sclerophyll woodland on sandy soil over acid granite; the New South Wales Government adds that the population occupies an isolated outcrop of granite capped with laterite.

At the Inverell Research Centre weather station, at 664 m, the mean annual rainfall is about 800 mm, slightly more in summer. The mean July minimum is 3.7 °C (38.7 °F), and the temperature falls to 0 °C (32 °F) or below on about ten days a year (Bureau of Meteorology). These figures give an idea of the regional climate; the station is not within the population.

Nothing has been published on the pollinators of the species: the New South Wales Government even lists this lack of knowledge among the threats, because it prevents any assessment of how fire and heat affect reproduction. The profile also notes that fire kills pollinators during the period when the cones produce heat.

Conservation status and regulation

Macrozamia humilis is listed as Critically Endangered (CR) on the IUCN Red List under criterion B1ab(iii): a very small range, few locations and a continuing decline in the area, extent or quality of its habitat. The assessment, by J.D. Bösenberg, is dated 15 October 2020 and was published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, GBIF and Crossref, checked on 26 October 2026). It is the only species of the genus in this category.

In New South Wales, the Threatened Species Scientific Committee listed it as Endangered by a final determination gazetted on 13 December 2002; it is now listed under the Biodiversity Conservation Act 2016. The Committee found that the species is known from a single location, that most of the population lies within a reserve managed for public recreation, that the available habitat has declined historically, and that the plant is further threatened by its small population and continued clearing. The current state profile adds other threats: illegal collection near urban centres, mining, too frequent or too intense fires (including arson), prolonged drought, invasion by Coolatai grass, which raises the fire risk, disturbance by walkers, mountain bikers and motorbikes, and grazing or trampling by goats and other herbivores. The species is not listed under the federal Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act 1999 (EPBC).

Like other Macrozamia, it has been listed in CITES Appendix II since 4 February 1977, with annotation #4: seeds and pollen are exempt, but plants are not. In the European Union it falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026 (Species+, checked on 26 October 2026). The New South Wales Government asks buyers to obtain plants only from licensed nurseries and requires a permit to collect seed in the wild. Check the regulations in force before any purchase or import, and buy only seed-raised plants with their papers.

Growing Macrozamia humilis

No cultivation experience with Macrozamia humilis has been published, in Australia or elsewhere, and none of the gardens that hold it has described how it is grown. The only pointers come from its habitat: a poor, sandy, very free-draining soil under a low, open tree cover, in a region with hot summers and cool, fairly dry winters. A free-draining mineral compost and bright but filtered light are therefore reasonable assumptions, not tested advice.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Light: in the wild, low, open woodland; bright but filtered light is a deduction from the habitat.
  • Soil: in the wild, sand over granite; a very free-draining mineral mix is the logical choice.
  • Water: no published data; the habitat receives about 800 mm a year, more in summer.
  • Space: little; a few leaves up to 65 cm long.

Container cultivation

No container culture has been described. Its small size suits a deep pot of mineral compost. A plant in a pot loses the protection that the soil gives to its underground stem in winter (see Cold hardiness).

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney holds an accession of wild origin received in 2022 (S2022-0085, four plants, according to its Garden Explorer), and the Australian Botanic Garden Mount Annan declares the species to BGCI.
  • United States: Montgomery Botanical Center, in Coral Gables (Florida), lists seven accessions (20230269 to 20230275), entered in 2023 judging by their numbers; it is the only American garden that declares the species to BGCI.
  • United Kingdom: no British garden declares it to BGCI (checked on 26 October 2026).

No grower in the English-speaking world has published a cultivation result.

Propagation

Macrozamia humilis is propagated by seed, like the other species of the genus. Both a male and a female plant are needed and, outside its habitat, hand pollination, since its pollinators are unknown. Given the rarity of the species, any seed must come from a legal, documented source; in New South Wales, collecting seed in the wild requires a permit. Wear gloves to clean the seeds, whose flesh is toxic.

Pests and diseases

No pest or disease specific to Macrozamia humilis is documented. In the wild, the main dangers noted by the New South Wales Government are fire, drought and grazing by goats and other herbivores. For Macrozamia grown in Europe, growers' forums mainly mention scale insects and rot in cold, wet soil (see the genus page).

Cold hardiness

The cold hardiness of Macrozamia humilis has not been tested in cultivation: no result has been published.

Mediterranean and European experience

No result has been published in Europe, in the ground or in a pot. In March 2014, on the French Fous de palmiers forum, a grower reported the opinion of an Australian Macrozamia specialist who placed Macrozamia humilis among the “hardiest” species of the genus, with those of inland New South Wales, without giving any temperature. This ranking is a collector's opinion, not a measurement of cold tolerance.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

The climate of the Inverell district gives a pointer. At the Inverell Research Centre station (664 m), the lowest temperature recorded since 1965 is −5.5 °C (22.1 °F), on 26 June 1986, and frost occurs on about ten days a year on average, mostly from June to August; in summer the thermometer has reached 41.5 °C (106.7 °F) (Bureau of Meteorology). The habitat itself has not been measured, and these figures say nothing about the tolerance of a plant in cultivation, still less under a wet winter. In Britain and in the colder parts of the United States, a pot kept frost-free in winter is the prudent choice for so rare a plant.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data specific to Macrozamia humilis have been published, but the risk is well documented for the genus. Macrozamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a poison of the liver and a carcinogen in laboratory animals; cattle that eat the leaves can develop a locomotor disorder of the hind legs (Healy, 1967), which McKenzie (2006) describes as irreversible, ending in paralysis of the hindquarters, with no treatment.

Treat the plant as toxic: wear gloves to handle the seeds and keep them out of reach of children and animals. In case of ingestion, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use specific to Macrozamia humilis is recorded. Other Macrozamia provided Aboriginal peoples with seeds that could be eaten after long processing (see the genus page); never eat cycad seeds.

Frequently asked questions

Why is Macrozamia humilis so endangered?

Because only one population is known. According to the New South Wales Government, its small numbers expose it to natural catastrophes such as fire, and it also suffers from clearing, illegal collection and disturbance by visitors.

Is it protected by law?

Yes. It is listed as Endangered in New South Wales and Critically Endangered on the IUCN Red List, and it falls under CITES Appendix II; it is not, however, listed under the federal EPBC Act.

Can Macrozamia humilis be bought?

Rarely, and only legally. No commercial offer is documented; the New South Wales Government advises buying only from licensed nurseries, and plants crossing borders need CITES papers.

How hardy is it?

Nobody knows. No trial has been published. Its region has about ten frosts a year and a record low of −5.5 °C (22.1 °F) at Inverell, but this guarantees nothing in cultivation, especially in a wet winter.

How can it be told from Macrozamia heteromera?

By its shorter leaves, 35 to 65 cm long, dull or greyish, and its fewer leaflets, 60 to 90. Both species belong to the same complex and sometimes have forked leaflets.

What does humilis mean?

“Humble” or “low” in Latin. The name refers to the short leaves of the plant.
