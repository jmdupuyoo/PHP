Encephalartos schaijesii is a small cycad from central Africa, with an underground or very short trunk, that grows in the savannas and open woodlands of the southern Democratic Republic of the Congo, near Kolwezi, and of north-western Zambia. This species of the genus Encephalartos was described in 1993; the Zambian plants described in 2006 as Encephalartos flavistrobilus, remarkable for their yellow cones, are now included in it. It is assessed as Vulnerable by the IUCN and remains very little known in cultivation.

Key facts about Encephalartos schaijesii

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos schaijesii Malaisse, Sclavo & Crosiers (1993), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15), IPNI, GBIF and Species+
  • Former names and synonyms: Encephalartos flavistrobilus I.Turner & Sclavo (2006)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: none attested, in English or any other language
  • Natural range: Democratic Republic of the Congo (Lualaba, Kolwezi area) and Zambia (North-Western Province, east of Solwezi)
  • IUCN status: Vulnerable (VU B1ab(ii,iii,v)+2ab(ii,iii,v)), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated April 2020, published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I since 1977, like the whole genus; Annex A in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: none (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, savanna on Kalahari sands (type locality) and miombo woodland; in cultivation in Thailand, raised beds in a sandy, gritty mix
  • Habit and adult size: trunk underground or upright; in the Zambian plants, trunk up to 30–40 cm tall and leaves 70 to 90 cm long (description of Encephalartos flavistrobilus)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley is the only garden in these countries to declare it (BGCI)
  • Toxicity: not documented for the species; toxins known in the genus (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos schaijesii

Encephalartos schaijesii is recognised first by its habit: a tuft of fairly short leaves that rises straight from the ground or from a barely visible trunk. It belongs to a group of central African species of similar appearance, which its authors separate mainly by the colour and shape of the cone scales and by the angle of the leaves and leaflets. The original description of 1993, based on leaf morphology, is not freely accessible.

The description of Encephalartos flavistrobilus, now a synonym, does give a precise portrait of the Zambian plants (Turner, Sclavo and Malaisse, 2006):

  • trunk underground or upright, up to 30–40 cm tall and 15–20 cm in diameter;
  • 10 to 20 green leaves, 70 to 90 cm long, oblong and narrowed towards the base, held erect at 60–70°; petiole 3.5 to 4 cm; rachis yellow;
  • 94 to 102 leaflets, the median ones opposite, oblong, 12 to 13 cm long and 1.5 cm wide, with a gradually tapering, pungent tip and 17 to 21 parallel veins; none or one small lateral spine on the upper margin, none to two on the lower margin;
  • towards the base, leaflets reduced, sometimes forked, the last ones reduced to spines;
  • two or three shortly stalked male cones, ellipsoid to subcylindrical, 16 to 19 cm long and 4 to 5 cm wide (dried), bright banana yellow as soon as they appear, then brownish yellow after maturity;
  • a single female cone, yellowish, shortly stalked, pear-shaped or ellipsoid, 25 cm long and 10 cm wide.

Hybrids

No hybrid of Encephalartos schaijesii is documented.

Possible confusion

Confusion arises with the other species of what Turner, Sclavo and Malaisse call the “Encephalartos poggei complex”: Encephalartos poggei, Encephalartos marunguensis, Encephalartos schmitzii and Encephalartos delucanus, all from south-central Africa and with no trunk or a short one. According to these authors, they look very much alike at first sight but can be told apart without ambiguity by a close examination of the cones (colour, shape of the scales) and of the leaves (leaflet characters, angle of the leaf and of the leaflets). Encephalartos schmitzii, reported in 1990, was the first cycad known from Zambia; the yellow-coned plants from east of Solwezi were the second.

Without cones or a known origin, a young plant of this group is therefore hard to identify.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos schaijesii is an accepted name in The World List of Cycads, IPNI, GBIF and Species+. François Malaisse, Jean-Pierre Sclavo and C. Crosiers published it in 1993 in the Bulletin du Jardin botanique national de Belgique (62: 215–219), within a paper entitled “Recherches sur les Encephalartos Lehm. (Zamiaceae) d'Afrique centrale. 2. Apport de la morphologie foliaire dans la différenciation spécifique” (pages 205 to 219), on the contribution of leaf morphology to telling the central African species apart. The holotype (Malaisse, Moretti and Sclavo 3), collected in March 1988 at Mupenda, 26 km west-south-west of Kolwezi, in savanna on Kalahari sands, is kept at Meise Botanic Garden in Belgium (BR).

In 2006, Ian Turner, Jean-Pierre Sclavo and François Malaisse described in the journal Biotechnologie, Agronomie, Société et Environnement (10(3): 181–183) a new species from Zambia, Encephalartos flavistrobilus, known from a single site east of Solwezi (holotype Turner 1251, April 2002, BR), which they separated from all others by the yellow colour of its male and female cones. The World List of Cycads now treats this name as a synonym of Encephalartos schaijesii, which extends the range of the species into Zambia; the study behind this change has not been identified. For the reader, this means that plants sold as Encephalartos flavistrobilus correspond, according to the reference checklist, to Encephalartos schaijesii. Species+, which lists Encephalartos flavistrobilus as a synonym, still gives only the Democratic Republic of the Congo in its distribution.

Etymology and history

The epithet honours Michel Schaijes, whose personal collections near Kolwezi advanced the knowledge of the plants of the region (Haynes, 2022, via The World List of Cycads). The synonym flavistrobilus means “with yellow cones”.

Knowledge of the central African Encephalartos progressed in stages, as Turner and his co-authors recall: collections by the early explorers up to the beginning of the 20th century, syntheses (including Melville's in 1957), then numerous descriptions from the 1960s onwards. Encephalartos schaijesii belongs to this last wave, with its neighbours from Katanga. How it came into cultivation is not documented.

Common names

No common name is attested for Encephalartos schaijesii, in English or any other language, and Species+ gives none. The English names found on some websites rest on no source.

In the wild

Encephalartos schaijesii grows in the southern Democratic Republic of the Congo, in Lualaba Province, and in the North-Western Province of Zambia. The type locality is a savanna on Kalahari sands near Mupenda, west-south-west of Kolwezi; the Zambian plants grow in miombo woodland east of Solwezi.

According to Turner and his co-authors (2006), the species of this group occupy shrub to wooded savannas, open woodlands and steppe savannas on Kalahari-type sand, in a climate with a dry season of four to seven months, on permeable or stony soils. Their often small ranges, sometimes limited to a single site, suggest to them a palaeoendemic origin. Nong Nooch Tropical Botanical Garden counts the species among the Encephalartos with seasonal foliage (see Growing Encephalartos schaijesii). Altitude, detailed population figures and pollination biology are not documented.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos schaijesii is assessed as Vulnerable (VU) on the IUCN Red List under criteria B1ab(ii,iii,v)+2ab(ii,iii,v), which reflect a restricted range and a continuing decline. The assessment, by J.D. Bösenberg, is dated 3 April 2020 and was published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, GBIF, Crossref). In 2003, the IUCN action plan for cycads estimated its population at 1,000 to 5,000 plants, reported a strong decline over the previous thirty years and named habitat destruction as the main threat; the species was then present only in private collections. The links between this threat and the mining activity of the region, put forward by some websites, are not documented.

Regulation, according to Species+ (consulted through its public API on 26 October 2026):

  • CITES: Appendix I since 4 February 1977, like the whole genus;
  • European Union: Annex A, current list in Regulation (EU) 2026/1383 (in force since 29 June 2026); for artificially propagated plants imported from South Africa (source codes A and D), the EU Scientific Review Group issued a positive opinion for plants with a caudex up to 7 cm in diameter (20 June 2011) and a negative opinion above that size (7 February 2013), both still current;
  • past CITES and EU trade suspensions concerning the Democratic Republic of the Congo (2014 and 2015), no longer in force.

Any plant offered for sale must come from cultivation and carry the CITES documents required by the countries concerned (and, in the European Union, the EU documents). These notes do not replace the official texts: check Species+ and the national authorities before any purchase or import.

Growing Encephalartos schaijesii

The cultivation of Encephalartos schaijesii is very poorly documented. The only reliable source comes from Thailand: at Nong Nooch Tropical Botanical Garden, the curator of the cycad collection wrote in 2022 that the “seasonal deciduous species”, among which he names Encephalartos schaijesii and Encephalartos flavistrobilus, thrive there and cone every year. The plants grow in raised beds, in a mix of one half sand, one quarter compost and one quarter coarse gravel, in a lowland tropical climate.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

Outside the tropics, no result of growing the species in the ground has been published. The Nong Nooch experience suggests a plant for frost-free, warm climates with a free-draining, sandy soil; the long dry season of its habitat also suggests keeping it on the dry side in its rest period, but no grower has published an account to confirm this.

Container cultivation

No account of the species in a pot has been published. For growers in cool climates, a deep pot in a mineral, very free-draining mix, kept in a warm or temperate glasshouse and dry in winter, is the cautious option, following the general rules for Encephalartos.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

BGCI PlantSearch lists only two institutions holding the species: the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley, in the United States, and Prague Botanical Garden, in the Czech Republic. No British or Australian garden declares it, and the Montgomery Botanical Center catalogue in Florida has no accession under either name. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not a living plant on display today. In 2003, the IUCN action plan knew the species only in private collections.

Propagation

No data specific to the species have been published on sowing or on the separation of suckers. The general advice for the genus applies: fresh, fertile seed and a free-draining medium, and caution with seed sold without guarantee, which is covered, like the plants, by CITES Appendix I.

Pests and diseases

No disease or pest specific to Encephalartos schaijesii is documented. As with other cycads, cold, waterlogged soil is the main cause of root rot in cultivation.

Cold hardiness

No cold-hardiness data exist for Encephalartos schaijesii: no grower and no garden has published a temperature reached by the species.

Mediterranean and European experience

No result of cultivation has been documented in France, Italy or elsewhere in Europe. For other Encephalartos, survivals observed in cold areas often concern young plants with an underground caudex, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil and sometimes by snow; a species with an underground trunk might benefit from this, but nothing allows this to be stated for this one. Growing it in a pot, wintered frost-free and dry, is the cautious solution.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No hardiness data have been published. The only published observations of the species in cultivation come from the lowland tropical climate of Nong Nooch, where it thrives. No USDA zone is documented for Encephalartos schaijesii in a reliable source.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data have been published for Encephalartos schaijesii, but the risk is documented in the genus: the toxicity of Encephalartos horridus was demonstrated in 1944 (Van der Walt), and Encephalartos contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; these substances affect the liver, the digestive tract and the nervous system. Treat the plant as poisonous, wear gloves to handle seeds and keep them out of reach of children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet.

Traditional uses

No traditional use of Encephalartos schaijesii is documented.

Frequently asked questions

Are Encephalartos flavistrobilus and Encephalartos schaijesii the same species?

Yes, according to The World List of Cycads, which treats Encephalartos flavistrobilus as a synonym. Described from Zambia in 2006, it was distinguished by its yellow cones; the study behind the change has not been identified.

Where does Encephalartos schaijesii grow?

In the southern Democratic Republic of the Congo and north-western Zambia: in savanna on Kalahari sands near Kolwezi, and in miombo woodland near Solwezi.

Is it endangered?

Yes, it is assessed as Vulnerable by the IUCN. In 2003 its population was estimated at 1,000 to 5,000 plants, in strong decline because of habitat destruction.

Can it be seen in a botanic garden?

Rarely. In the United States, only the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley declares it in BGCI; Nong Nooch, in Thailand, grows it in raised beds.

Can it be grown in the UK or in a cool climate?

In a pot and under glass, probably, but no experience has been published. The only cultivation data come from a tropical botanic garden in Thailand, where it cones every year.

Is it frost-hardy?

Nobody knows. No observation of cold has been documented; keep it frost-free in winter.

Is it poisonous?

It should be treated as such. No data exist for the species, but Encephalartos contain toxins that are dangerous to people and animals.
