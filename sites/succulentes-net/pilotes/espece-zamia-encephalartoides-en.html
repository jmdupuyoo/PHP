Zamia encephalartoides is a cycad of north-eastern Colombia, endemic to the department of Santander, where it grows on the hot, dry rocky slopes of the Chicamocha canyon. It is the most unusual species of the genus Zamia: it forms a true trunk up to 2 m tall, lives in a dry, sunny habitat rather than in rainforest, and looks so much like the African cycads of the genus Encephalartos that it takes its name from them. It is also the only Zamia with white seeds. Described only in 2001, it is listed as Endangered by the IUCN.

Key facts about Zamia encephalartoides

  • Accepted name: Zamia encephalartoides D.W.Stev. (2001), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and by GBIF; POWO could not be consulted directly
  • Former names and synonyms: no synonym; the eighteenth-century plates that depict it had been tentatively referred to “Zamia cf. muricata”
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: “cacao indio” in Spanish (Colombia), also written “cacao del indio” or “cacao de indio”; no English name attested
  • Natural range: Colombia, department of Santander (Chicamocha canyon)
  • IUCN status: Endangered (EN), assessment published in 2022, criteria B1ab(iii,v)+2ab(iii,v)
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (the whole genus, since 1977; seeds exempt under annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: no reliable measurement; Jungle Music describes its cold tolerance as not known (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: dry forest and scrub on very exposed rocky slopes, at about 500 to 760 m according to herbarium specimens; full sun and stony, calcareous soil according to a grower who saw it in the wild
  • Habit and adult size: upright trunk up to 2 m tall and 25 cm across; 10 to 15 stiff, unarmed leaves 0.5 to 1 m long, with 20 to 40 pairs of leathery leaflets
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Montgomery Botanical Center (five accessions, 1994 to 2015), Fairchild and eight other American gardens (BGCI); Royal Botanic Garden Sydney and Cairns; no British record
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; treat it as poisonous (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Zamia encephalartoides

It is easy to recognise: it is the only Colombian Zamia with a true trunk in an arid landscape, with stiff, arching, spineless leaves, leathery leaflets with an entire, strongly rolled-under margin, female cones dark green at maturity and white seeds. The measurements below come from the original description (Stevenson, 2001, pp. 40–43, fig. 5):

  • Trunk: tree-like, up to 2 m tall and 25 cm in diameter.
  • Cataphylls: wedge-shaped base and tapering tip, up to 5 cm long and 2 cm wide.
  • Leaves: 10 to 15, 0.5 to 1 m long; petiole without prickles, 15 to 25 cm; rachis also unarmed, bearing 20 to 40 pairs of leaflets.
  • Leaflets: overlapping, stalkless, lanceolate, very leathery, without grooves, with a wedge-shaped base and pointed tip, and an entire, strongly revolute margin; the median ones are 20 to 35 cm long and 1 to 3 cm wide.
  • Male cones: yellowish to light brown, narrowly cylindrical, 20 to 30 cm by 3 to 5 cm, on a peduncle 5 to 8 cm long; the pollen sacs cover both faces of the microsporophylls.
  • Female cones: light brown when young, dark green at maturity, cylindrical to ovoid-cylindrical, 25 to 40 cm by 10 to 15 cm.
  • Seeds: white to yellowish white, 3 to 4 cm long and 1.5 to 2 cm in diameter.

The Latin diagnosis compares it with Zamia inermis, a Mexican species that also lacks prickles, from which it differs in its tree-like habit, green female cones, white seeds and the shape of its microsporophylls. Stevenson also notes that the fleshy coat of the seeds (sarcotesta) does not break up, unlike that of other Zamia. A French grower who collected seed in the canyon in 2012 reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that this white sarcotesta never becomes soft as it does in other species, and he considers these seeds the largest in the genus.

Hybrids

No hybrid, natural or horticultural, has been documented. The plant remains rare in cultivation, and the publications consulted mention no cross.

Possible confusion

In its habitat, no other known cycad resembles it: its upright trunk among dry vegetation is enough to identify it. In cultivation or in photographs, the confusion is rather with the African genus Encephalartos, with which it shares its habit, its stiff, arching leaves and the shape of its female cone. The two genera nevertheless grow on two different continents, and Zamia encephalartoides has the leaf venation and architecture typical of Zamia; its unarmed petiole and white seeds remove any doubt. Among Zamia, the original description compares it only with Zamia inermis, which is also spineless but much smaller.

In South America, unarmed petioles are known only in Zamia encephalartoides, Zamia boliviana and Zamia brasiliensis, according to Segalla and Calonje (2019); the last two are small plants with underground stems from Bolivia and Brazil.

Taxonomy

The name has never changed and has no synonym. The species was described by the American botanist Dennis W. Stevenson in his treatment of the cycads for the Flora de Colombia (volume 21, pp. 40–42, fig. 5, 2001). The type is the collection F. González 3581, made in the department of Santander at 760 m; the holotype is kept in Bogotá (COL), with isotypes in New York (NY) and Utrecht (U).

Its place within the genus is poorly documented in the sources consulted: the original description compares it only with Zamia inermis.

Etymology and history

The epithet encephalartoides means “resembling Encephalartos”: the original description explains it by the great similarity of the leaves and cone to those of the African genus (Stevenson, 2001; Haynes, 2022).

Its history is out of the ordinary. The plant was drawn during the Real Expedición Botánica del Nuevo Reino de Granada, the great Spanish botanical expedition led by José Celestino Mutis from 1783. Two of the published plates (nos 18 and 19), one in colour and one in black and white, show a female cone and details of the seed and embryo: according to Stevenson, they are the oldest known illustration of Zamiaceae embryos. The plates had been tentatively identified as “Zamia cf. muricata”, and the plant they show remained a puzzle for two centuries; the precision of the cone and the yellowish-white colour of the seeds allowed Stevenson to assign them with certainty to the new species. He cites on this subject the work of Díaz-Piedrahita (1985) and Sergio Sabato (1990). According to the online encyclopedia LLIFLE, no herbarium specimen from the expedition has survived, unlike the plates.

Its arrival in cultivation is recent. According to the Californian nursery Jungle Music, in an undated article, a South African seed merchant first put seed on the market “about fifteen years ago”, which gave nurseries their first chance to grow the species, and it remains “extremely rare in cultivation”.

Common names

The original description gives the Spanish name “cacao indio”, used in Colombia; the LLIFLE encyclopedia writes “cacao del indio”, and the IUCN Red List, as relayed by GBIF, “cacao de indio”. No English common name is attested; the scientific name is used. No source explains the name.

In the wild

Zamia encephalartoides is endemic to the department of Santander, in north-eastern Colombia. According to the original description, it grows in dry forest and scrub, on very exposed rocky ground and on slopes; the herbarium specimens cited were collected at about 500 to 760 m. According to the LLIFLE encyclopedia, it is known from only two localities, near the Chicamocha and Umpalá rivers, and grows in the open, in the shrubby vegetation of the very hot, dry slopes and ridges that the Chicamocha has cut into the Eastern Cordillera of the Andes: a habitat unlike the humid forests where the other Colombian Zamia live. The same source describes the tropical dry forest that shelters it as one of the most fragmented and degraded ecosystems in the country.

A French grower who visited a population in October 2012 reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that the plants grow between 500 and 1,000 m, in a place where, in his view, it never freezes, with minimum temperatures he estimates at about 5 °C (41 °F) and maximums at about 35 °C (95 °F). These are a visitor’s estimates, not measurements. Like other cycads, the species forms coralloid roots that house nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria.

Conservation status and regulation

Zamia encephalartoides is listed as Endangered (EN) on the IUCN Red List, on the basis of Cristina López-Gallego’s assessment published in 2022, under criteria B1ab(iii,v)+2ab(iii,v): a very small range and area of occupancy, with declining habitat quality and number of mature individuals. According to Crossref the assessment is dated 26 June 2020; an earlier assessment by D.W. Stevenson appears in the 2010 Red List. In 2001, Stevenson knew several populations of adults, some of them reproducing, and of seedlings; he named as the main threats unstable ground, damage to trunks by cattle and indiscriminate harvesting of cones laden with seed, and was already calling for the species to be put on the Red List. LLIFLE, without giving its source, adds the spread of farming and soil erosion, and puts the total population at about 5,000 plants, including about 1,000 large specimens in one of the main subpopulations, near Umpalá, for a known extent of occurrence of about 266 km².

Like every Zamia except Zamia restrepoi, Zamia encephalartoides is listed in CITES Appendix II, where the genus has appeared since 4 February 1977. International trade in the plants is therefore allowed but requires an export permit from the country of origin. The listing carries annotation #4: seeds, pollen, seedlings and tissue cultures obtained in vitro and transported in sterile containers are not covered by CITES. In the European Union the species falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, whose current list is set by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026.

Species+ has no separate entry for this species: it is covered by the listing of Zamia spp. CITES and EU status read on Species+; IUCN status taken from The World List of Cycads, as the Red List itself could not be consulted. This summary does not replace the official texts: check the regulations in force before any purchase or exchange, and ask the seller for proof of origin.

Growing Zamia encephalartoides

It is a sought-after but rare collector’s plant, rather less demanding than the rainforest Zamia. The French specialist nursery Cycadales.eu groups it with Zamia lindenii, Zamia huilensis and Zamia tolimensis among the South American Zamia of seasonally dry ecosystems, of “moderate” difficulty: in its view, these species pose no problem in a frost-free glasshouse in southern France, because they can be kept dry in winter, which avoids root rot. The nursery likens them to the Mexican species, for which it recommends 5 to 10 °C (41 to 50 °F) in winter with daytime rises to 20 to 25 °C (68 to 77 °F) and a fairly dry but not parched compost, and in summer 15 to 20 °C (59 to 68 °F) at night and 25 to 30 °C (77 to 86 °F) by day, with at least four to six months of summer temperatures (Cycadales.eu, 2023).

Growers point the same way. The one who collected the seed in 2012 recommends full sun from the first year, in a rather calcareous soil, as for a dry-habitat plant, and suggests growing it like an Encephalartos: moderate watering, a very free-draining compost, strong light and slow growth. In California, Jungle Music describes it as extremely slow-growing, liking well-drained soil and not to be overwatered when cold, and suspects that it prefers sun; it hoped the species would suit dry regions such as Southern California. These are growers’ recommendations, not measured data.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

Planting out has been documented only once, on the Mediterranean coast of France (see Cold hardiness), with no recorded temperature. This Mediterranean experience is of interest to readers in comparable dry-summer climates, such as coastal California or southern Australia, but no outdoor result from those regions has been found.

Container cultivation

In the UK and other cool climates, grow it in a deep pot in a gritty, free-draining compost, in full light under glass, kept frost-free and on the dry side in winter, as the French nursery advises for the group.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In short: a dozen American and Australian collections; no British record.

  • United States: Montgomery Botanical Center, in Coral Gables (Florida), lists five accessions in its online catalogue, numbered 94910, 951376, 971593, 981851 and 20150195, that is, from 1994, 1995, 1997, 1998 and 2015 according to the garden’s numbering (consulted on 24 October 2026). Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden lists it in its online Garden Explorer (inventory of 19 October 2026). Huntington, Lotusland, San Diego Botanic Garden, San Diego Zoo, Atlanta Botanical Garden, Longwood Gardens, the New York Botanical Garden and the University of Hawaii at Hilo garden also declare it to BGCI.
  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney lists the species in its Garden Explorer (inventory of 19 October 2026) and declares it to BGCI, as does Cairns Botanic Gardens.
  • United Kingdom: no record in BGCI or in the British catalogues checked in October 2026. The British nursery Trebrown has a species page with a USDA zone 10a rating, its own commercial classification, without further information.

A BGCI declaration shows that a garden held the plant at some point, not that it is alive today, and the sources do not say whether these plants grow outdoors or under glass.

Propagation

Propagation is by seed. The species being dioecious, male and female plants are needed and, in cultivation, hand pollination. Reports published on the Fous de palmiers forum after a distribution of wild-collected seed in November 2012 show slow, irregular germination: the first seeds germinated in January 2013, others only in February or April, and several sowers got none from four seeds. A seedling that germinated in April took about three months to grow its root before showing anything above ground, and some did not produce their first leaf until December 2013. The above-ground trunk does not give usable offsets unless it branches of its own accord.

Pests and diseases

No pest or disease specific to the species has been documented. The risk mentioned by Cycadales.eu and by growers for dry-habitat Zamia is root rot in a cold, wet compost, avoided with a mineral mix and reduced watering in winter.

Cold hardiness

No reliable measurement exists. Jungle Music writes that its cold tolerance “is not known”, while suspecting that it will tolerate a frost. Two precautions apply in every case: the trunk is above ground and does not benefit from the protection of the soil enjoyed by cycads with underground stems, and wet cold is far more dangerous than dry cold.

Mediterranean and European experience

The only documented in-ground trial comes from the French Mediterranean coast. A grower on the Côte Bleue (Bouches-du-Rhône, France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that he planted a few Zamia encephalartoides in the ground in summer 2014, in full sun, in a calanque-like site, next to other tropical Zamia placed in shade; in January 2015 the plants “seemed fine”, and in September 2015 he wrote that they had all come through a winter without trouble. He gives no minimum temperature, and how they fared afterwards is not known. The Jardin zoologique tropical at La Londe-les-Maures, in the Var, has published no observation of this species.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No dated outdoor report was found from these countries. Elsewhere, a grower in French Guiana reports on the same French forum (March 2014) that two young plants put in the ground seemed to thrive in the equatorial climate, despite a particularly wet rainy season. Trebrown’s zone 10a rating is a commercial classification, not a measured result.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity study specific to Zamia encephalartoides has been found. The risk is documented in the genus and in other cycads: Zamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, and for Zamia furfuracea the ASPCA cites cycasin and BMAA, a neurotoxic amino acid, with vomiting, bloody diarrhoea and liver damage in dogs, cats and horses; the seeds are the most dangerous part. In the absence of evidence to the contrary, treat the plant as poisonous: keep seeds and leaves out of reach of children and animals, and wear gloves to clean seeds. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet straight away.

Traditional uses

The name “cacao indio” is attested by the original description, but none of the sources consulted reliably documents any food or medicinal use of the species, and the uses reported for other Zamia should not be attributed to it.

Frequently asked questions

Is it an Encephalartos?

No. Despite the resemblance, Zamia encephalartoides is a South American Zamia; Encephalartos is an African genus. It is precisely the similar habit, leaves and cone that gave it its name.

Why are its seeds white?

It is a character peculiar to the species: other Zamia have seeds that are usually red or orange, sometimes yellow. This yellowish-white colour, already shown on the plates of the Mutis expedition, made it possible to recognise the species in those illustrations.

Where does it grow?

Only in the department of Santander, Colombia, in the Chicamocha canyon, on hot, dry rocky slopes, at about 500 to 760 m according to herbarium specimens.

Should it be grown in sun?

Yes, according to those who have seen it in the wild and grow it: it is a plant of open, dry ground, to be given full sun, unlike understorey Zamia.

Is it frost-hardy?

Not with any certainty. No measured value has been published, and Jungle Music describes its cold tolerance as not known; in the wild, according to a visitor, it never freezes. Wet cold is the main danger. In the UK it should be kept under glass in winter.

Can it be planted out in a Mediterranean climate?

Only as a trial. A few plants came through the winter of 2014–2015 in the ground and in full sun on the Côte Bleue, near Marseille, with no temperature recorded; a specialist nursery recommends a frost-free glasshouse, kept dry in winter.

Is it poisonous?

It should be treated as such, like all cycads: the seeds are the most dangerous part for people and animals.
