Zamia lucayana is a small cycad of the Bahamas, known from a narrow strip of coastal thicket on the Atlantic side of Long Island, where it grows in sand in the shelter of sea grape (Coccoloba uvifera). This species of the genus Zamia, a member of the Zamia pumila complex, has an underground stem and leaves that barely exceed half a metre. Described in 1907 and long sunk into the synonymy of other Caribbean Zamia, it is once again recognised as a species in its own right. The IUCN lists it as Critically Endangered, yet its conservation collections are among the best studied of any plant from a genetic point of view.

Key facts about Zamia lucayana

  • Accepted name: Zamia lucayana Britton (1907), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and by GBIF; POWO could not be consulted directly
  • Former names and synonyms: no synonym of its own; formerly treated as a synonym of other Caribbean Zamia (see Taxonomy)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: “bay rush” and “Long Island coontie”
  • Natural range: Bahamas, east (Atlantic) coast of Long Island; The World List of Cycads also cites Abaco
  • IUCN status: Critically Endangered (CR), criteria B1ab(i,ii,iii)+2ab(i,ii,iii), assessment published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (the whole genus, since 1977; seeds exempt under annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: no data (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, evergreen coastal thicket on loose sands, at sea level; no growing recommendations specific to the species
  • Habit and adult size: upright underground stem 15 to 40 cm long and up to 15 cm in diameter; leaves up to 0.5 m
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Montgomery Botanical Center (Florida), 19 accessions, 17 of them from 2009; Fairchild and eight other American gardens declare it to BGCI; no British or Australian record
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; treat it as poisonous (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Zamia lucayana

It is a small Zamia with an underground stem, short leaves covered in rusty-brown down when young, and leaflets with entire margins, barely toothed at the tip. In the field, provenance remains the surest guide: it is the only cycad on Long Island. The Leon Levy Native Plant Preserve, a botanical reserve on Eleuthera in the Bahamas, gives the following characters:

  • Stem: underground and upright, 15 to 40 cm long and up to 15 cm in diameter; the leaves arise from the top of the tuber.
  • Leaves: pinnate, up to 0.5 m long, covered in rusty-brown hairs when young.
  • Leaflets: up to 15 cm long, often less, slightly toothed at the tip, with entire, sometimes irregular margins.
  • Male cones: single or clustered, up to 8 cm by 2 cm, with 15 to 20 rows of hexagonal scales.
  • Female cone: single, up to 13 cm by 4 cm, with 5 to 8 rows of scales, entirely covered in reddish hairs.

Like all Zamia, the species is dioecious. In their surveys of 2009–2011, Calonje and colleagues (2013) found about 80 % males among fertile adults and about 27 % juveniles, recognisable by their small size, few leaves (usually fewer than four) and few leaflets (fewer than five pairs). Hoban and colleagues (2020) describe it as a fast-growing cycad that cones frequently.

Hybrids

No hybrid, natural or horticultural, has been documented. Genetic studies have shown ancient gene flow between the Zamia populations of the archipelago (Salas-Leiva and colleagues, 2017), but no present-day hybrid.

Possible confusion

Three Zamia grow in the Bahamas (Calonje and colleagues, 2013): Zamia integrifolia, native to Abaco, Andros, Eleuthera, Grand Bahama and New Providence; Zamia angustifolia, of the coastal dunes of Eleuthera; and Zamia lucayana, of Long Island. Each island holds only one species, except Eleuthera, where the first two coexist without their ranges overlapping. All three belong to the Zamia pumila complex, a monophyletic group of about ten closely related species from the Caribbean islands and Florida, whose number has long been debated.

Without a known origin, telling a young Zamia lucayana from a cultivated Zamia integrifolia is difficult, and no published identification key has been consulted.

Taxonomy

Zamia lucayana is accepted by The World List of Cycads and by GBIF; POWO, the site’s nomenclatural authority, could not be consulted directly. The species was published in 1907 by the American botanist Nathaniel Lord Britton, of the New York Botanical Garden, in the Bulletin of the New York Botanical Garden (volume 5, issue 18, page 311). According to The World List of Cycads, the type is the collection Britton and Millspaugh 627, made at Clarence Town on Long Island, and the holotype is kept in New York (NY). Calonje and colleagues (2013) add that Britton described it from a single plant, from a poorly known locality apparently close to Clarence Town.

The name was long sunk in synonymy. The World List of Cycads states that Stevenson (1987) treated it as a synonym: contrary to what is sometimes written, that paper therefore did not reinstate the species. Depending on the author, the Zamia of the Caribbean and Florida have been treated as one species or as many separate species (Lindstrom and colleagues, 2024, citing in particular Eckenwalder, 1980, and Stevenson, 1987). The 2013 study of the cycads of the Bahamas again treats Zamia lucayana as a species in its own right, as does The World List of Cycads.

Genetic studies shed light on its history. According to Salas-Leiva and colleagues (2017), the Long Island populations may result from a second colonisation of the Bahamas by Zamia, or from a rapid, early divergence. In the transcriptome-based phylogeny of Lindstrom and colleagues (2024), which sampled a plant from Long Island, the species falls in the Caribbean clade (clade I), which brings together all the island Zamia and the Florida Zamia integrifolia.

Etymology and history

The epithet lucayana honours the Lucayans, the Indigenous people who lived in the Bahamas before the arrival of Europeans (Haynes, 2022, as quoted by The World List of Cycads).

After its description in 1907 the species remained poorly known. According to Calonje and colleagues (2013), Hill (1974) found it again, as a dense population near Hamilton’s Settlement; their own surveys, between December 2009 and July 2011, confirmed this population and found two others, near Buckley’s and Petty’s Settlements.

Common names

Griffith and colleagues (2017) call it “bay rush”, and the Leon Levy Native Plant Preserve “Long Island coontie”; “coontie” is the English name of the Florida Zamia. No name is attested in French.

In the wild

Zamia lucayana grows on the east coast of Long Island, between Hamilton’s and Buckley’s Settlements. According to the field surveys of Calonje and colleagues (2013), its three main populations occupy only a narrow strip of coastal thicket, about 6.5 km long and 100 m wide, on sandy soils with sea grape, giving an extent of occurrence of about 1 km²; the area actually occupied is only 0.06 km². The authors estimate the population at about 980 adults, with 240 to 400 plants in each main population (Buckley’s, Petty’s and Hamilton’s), plus two small localities: Galloway Landing, with 10 adults seen, and Mangrove Bush, with 20. Hoban and colleagues (2020) speak of a single population of fewer than 1,000 plants, on a single island.

The Leon Levy Native Plant Preserve describes the habitat as dry, evergreen broadleaf coppice or low coppice on loose Holocene sands, along part of the Atlantic coast of Long Island. The 2026 version of The World List of Cycads also cites the island of Abaco, without giving the source of this addition; the 2013 study, which surveyed Abaco, recorded only Zamia integrifolia there (and Zamia cf. integrifolia on Tilloo Cay).

The 2013 authors noted that all females bearing ripe cones had almost complete seed set, a sign of effective pollination, and they saw land hermit crabs (Coenobita clypeatus) eating the fleshy coat of the seeds, which may speed up germination and ensure short-distance dispersal. Like other cycads, the species forms coralloid roots that house nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria.

Conservation status and regulation

The species is listed as Critically Endangered (CR) on the IUCN Red List, under criteria B1ab(i,ii,iii)+2ab(i,ii,iii), in the assessment by Michael Calonje and M. Patrick Griffith published in 2022 and cited by The World List of Cycads; GBIF gives the same category (the Red List itself could not be consulted). These criteria reflect a very small extent of occurrence and area of occupancy and a decline in habitat. The move from Endangered to Critically Endangered had been proposed in 2013 by Calonje and colleagues, on the basis of the extent of occurrence (1 km²) and the area of occupancy (0.06 km²).

In 2013 the authors judged that human activity had not yet had a major negative effect on the populations. The Hamilton’s Settlement population, however, lies in an area used for sand mining, and all the sites, reached by road, are on seafront land subdivided for sale, still little built on. As the plants grow on private land, they recommended limiting sand extraction, steering building to other parts of the island, setting up conservation collections in the Bahamas and elsewhere, raising public awareness and reaching voluntary agreements with landowners.

The same study analysed the genetics of the three main populations with 15 microsatellite markers (33 to 46 plants per population). Differentiation between them is low (mean FST of 0.067) and they long formed a single panmictic population: they should be managed as a single conservation unit. The high number of alleles unique to each, however, shows that genetic drift, a sign of recent fragmentation, is under way. The Petty’s population, the most “mixed”, was considered the best source of material for reintroduction or a collection.

During their fieldwork the authors collected 910 seeds from 17 plants of Zamia lucayana, which reached 85 % germination, and shared them between the Bahamas National Trust, Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden, Montgomery Botanical Center, the USDA genetic resources unit in Miami, the Francisco Javier Clavijero botanic garden in Xalapa (Mexico) and the Fairylake botanic garden in Shenzhen (China). Griffith and colleagues (2017) compared these collections with the wild plants using 10 microsatellite markers, alongside Zamia decumbens: the cultivated plants faithfully reflect their source populations. In 2020 a wider study, in which the same team took part, calculated that the 244 plants in collections hold 95 % of the alleles found in the wild, the best result among the species studied, and that optimal sampling would have achieved the same result with about 44 plants (Hoban and colleagues, 2020).

On the regulatory side, Zamia lucayana is covered by the listing of the genus Zamia in CITES Appendix II, in force since 4 February 1977; only one species of the genus, Zamia restrepoi, is in Appendix I. Annotation #4 exempts in particular seeds, pollen and seedlings from in vitro culture in sterile containers. In the European Union the species is listed in Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97 (list updated by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, in force since 29 June 2026): importing live plants requires a permit.

CITES and EU status checked on Species+ (listing “Zamia spp.”) in October 2026; IUCN status taken from The World List of Cycads. This information does not replace the official texts: check the regulations in force before buying or exchanging plants, and prefer plants raised from cultivated seed, with proof of origin.

Growing Zamia lucayana

No growing recommendations specific to the species have been published, and the plant is rare outside conservation collections. The minimum glasshouse temperatures and compost recipes that circulate for it cite no verifiable source.

The French specialist nursery Cycadales.eu lists Zamia lucayana among the eight official species of the Caribbean Zamia clade, with Zamia erosa, Zamia portoricensis and Zamia pygmaea among others, which it rates as easy to grow: in its view these species all tolerate temperatures close to freezing and cope well with Mediterranean-type winters, come almost all from limestone habitats and are drought-tolerant. It advises a free-draining, slightly to strongly limy compost, 80 % mineral, any kind of water, 1 to 10 °C (33.8 to 50 °F) in winter with daytime rises to 15 to 20 °C (59 to 68 °F) and a compost kept fairly dry, then 15 to 20 °C (59 to 68 °F) at night and 25 to 30 °C (77 to 86 °F) by day in summer, for at least four to six months (Cycadales.eu, 2023). This is experience-based advice for the whole group, not observations on Zamia lucayana, which grows on coastal sands rather than on solid limestone.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

No outdoor result has been published. The species comes from a frost-free subtropical island, and planting out can only be considered in a similar climate, such as southern Florida; anywhere colder it is a trial without reference points.

Container cultivation

In the UK and other cool climates it is a plant for a pot under glass, in a mineral, free-draining compost, kept frost-free and fairly dry in winter and warm in summer, following the nursery’s advice for the group.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In short: a species held mainly in Florida conservation collections; no British or Australian record.

  • United States: Montgomery Botanical Center, in Coral Gables (Florida), lists 19 accessions in its online catalogue, two from 2002 and seventeen from 2009 according to the garden’s numbering (the 2009 date fits the seed collection of the 2009–2011 fieldwork, though the catalogue does not say so), plus eight accessions of Zamia aff. lucayana from 2011 (consulted on 24 October 2026). Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden, which received part of the seed in 2013, also declares it to BGCI, as do Naples Botanical Garden, Pinecrest Gardens (Florida), the Huntington (California), Atlanta Botanical Garden, Chicago Botanic Garden, Longwood Gardens, the botanical garden of the University of Hawaii at Hilo and the US National Plant Germplasm System.
  • United Kingdom and Australia: no record in BGCI or in the British and Australian catalogues checked in October 2026.

A BGCI declaration shows that a garden held the plant at some point, not that it is alive today. The British nursery Trebrown gives it a USDA zone 9b rating, its own commercial classification, without any other information.

Propagation

As the species is dioecious, a male and a female plant are needed to obtain seed and, away from its habitat, hand pollination. Its tendency to cone often (Hoban and colleagues, 2020) makes seed raising in collections easier, and seed collected in the wild in 2009–2011 germinated at 85 % (Calonje and colleagues, 2013). Clean the seeds of their flesh, wearing gloves, and sow them fresh; no published data on germination time have been found.

Pests and diseases

No pest or disease specific to the species has been documented. In cultivation, Zamia are mainly at risk from stem rot in a cold, waterlogged compost, and from scale insects, including the cycad aulacaspis scale (Aulacaspis yasumatsui).

Cold hardiness

There are no data on the cold resistance of Zamia lucayana. The species lives at sea level on a subtropical island in the Bahamas; the USDA zones sometimes suggested (10b to 11) rest on no observation, and Trebrown’s zone 9b is a commercial classification.

Mediterranean and European experience

No in-ground cultivation has been reported in France, and no dated frost episode concerns it. For comparison only, and without transferring the result to this species: at the Jardin zoologique tropical in La Londe-les-Maures, in the Var, a Zamia integrifolia, a species of the same complex, survived −7 °C (19.4 °F) in February 2012, with damage to its leaves. Without any trial, it is better to grow Zamia lucayana in a pot, frost-free in winter.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No cold measurement has been found for the species, either on forums or in botanic collections. For the Caribbean Zamia as a whole, Cycadales.eu reports tolerance of temperatures close to freezing, a group value and not a measurement on this species.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity study specific to Zamia lucayana has been found. The risk is, however, documented in the genus and in other cycads: Zamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, and for Zamia furfuracea the ASPCA cites cycasin and BMAA, a neurotoxic amino acid, with vomiting, bloody diarrhoea and liver damage in dogs, cats and horses; the seeds are the most dangerous part. In the absence of evidence to the contrary, treat the plant as poisonous: keep seeds and leaves out of reach of children and animals, and wear gloves to clean seeds. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet straight away.

Traditional uses

No use is documented. According to the Leon Levy Native Plant Preserve, the species has no known medicinal or cultural use in the Bahamas. The food uses of Zamia recorded on other Caribbean islands cannot be attributed to it without evidence, and no published source states that the Lucayans ate it.

Frequently asked questions

Where does Zamia lucayana grow?

In the Bahamas, on a narrow strip of sandy coastal thicket on the east coast of Long Island, between Hamilton’s and Buckley’s Settlements; The World List of Cycads also cites Abaco, without a published source.

Who were the Lucayans?

The Indigenous people of the Bahamas before the arrival of Europeans. The epithet lucayana pays tribute to them.

Is it the same plant as Zamia integrifolia?

No, according to current classification. It has at times been treated as a synonym, but it is again recognised as a separate species, notably since the 2013 study. Both belong to the same group of Caribbean Zamia.

Why is it Critically Endangered?

Because it occupies a tiny area (an extent of about 1 km² and 0.06 km² actually occupied, according to the 2013 study), with about 980 adults, on coastal land that is subdivided for sale and partly used for sand mining.

Can I buy it?

Rarely. It is found mainly in conservation collections; in Europe, trade in the plants requires CITES documents. Always ask about provenance.

Is it frost-hardy?

Nobody knows: no data exist. It comes from a subtropical island; in the UK, grow it in a pot under glass, frost-free.

Is it poisonous?

Yes, like all cycads: in the absence of data on the species, it should be treated as poisonous, especially its seeds.
