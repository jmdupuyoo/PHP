Encephalartos whitelockii is a large tree-like cycad that grows wild in a single place in the world: the gorge of the Mpanga River, in western Uganda, near Lake George. Described in 1995 by the South African botanist Johan Hurter and named after the cycad specialist Loran M. Whitelock, this species of the genus Encephalartos forms trunks up to 3.5 m tall that sucker at the base and carry a dense crown of green leaves more than 3 m long. The gorge holds a few thousand plants, in almost pure stands, but a small hydroelectric power station built at the Mpanga Falls in 2011 destroyed part of them, and the IUCN lists the species as Critically Endangered (assessment published in 2024). In cultivation, young plants are reputed to grow vigorously; outside the tropics, they are kept in containers and overwintered frost-free.

Key facts about Encephalartos whitelockii

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos whitelockii P.J.H.Hurter (1995), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15), Species+ and GBIF
  • Synonym: Encephalartos successibus Vorster (1995), described from Uganda in the same year and referred to Encephalartos whitelockii by The World List of Cycads and Species+
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: “Muhure cycad” in the Kew account of the Mpanga Gorge
  • Natural range: endemic to western Uganda (Kamwenge District), gorge of the Mpanga River and neighbouring hills, upstream of Lake George
  • IUCN status: Critically Endangered (CR), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated 6 March 2020 and published in the IUCN Red List 2024-1; already CR in 2010; Vulnerable (VU) in the IUCN action plan of 2003
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I since 1977, like the whole genus; Annex A in the European Union, with a negative EU opinion for artificially propagated plants from South Africa
  • Documented hardiness: no measured value; all dated cultivation reports concern potted plants overwintered in a heated glasshouse or indoors (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, steep rocky slopes of the gorge, between riverine forest and wooded savanna, at about 920 to 1,300 m; in cultivation, bright light, free-draining compost, frost-free winters
  • Habit and adult size: trunk up to 3.5 m (rarely 4.2 m) tall and 35 to 40 cm in diameter, suckering; leaves 3.1 to 3.4 m long (up to 4.1 m); male cones about 50 cm, female cones about 45 cm long (Hurter, 1995)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: no British garden declares it to BGCI; in the United States, the Montgomery Botanical Center (eight accessions), Fairchild, the Huntington, Lotusland, the San Diego gardens and others; in Australia, Melbourne (outdoor beds), Sydney, Brisbane, Adelaide, Cairns and others
  • Toxicity: seeds toxic, as in all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos whitelockii

It is recognised by its suckering trunk crowned with very long, stiff green leaves arching towards the tip, by its toothed leaflets, spreading and not overlapping, whose tips point towards the apex of the leaf, by its lower leaflets reduced to a row of prickles and by its male cones, which hang down when ripe. Hurter's original description (1995) gives the following characters:

  • Trunk: tree-like, suckering at the base, up to 3.5 m long (rarely 4.2 m) and 35 to 40 cm wide, covered with the persistent leaf bases;
  • Leaves: many, in a dense crown, stiff, almost sessile, green, arching towards the tip, 3.1 to 3.4 m long (up to 4.1 m); petiole swollen at the base, up to 13 cm long, hairless; rachis woolly at first, then hairless;
  • Leaflets: toothed, with slightly rolled margins, spreading, inserted at an angle of about 160° to each other, not overlapping; the median ones 23 to 30 cm long and 20 to 28 mm wide, narrowly elliptic and sickle-shaped, gradually tapering to a sharp point, the tip turned slightly towards the apex of the leaf; the lower ones progressively reduced to a row of prickles;
  • Male cones: up to five per trunk, narrowly ovoid, about 50 cm long and 9 cm wide, on a stalk up to 32 cm long, light green turning yellow, hanging down when ripe;
  • Female cones: one to three per trunk, ovoid, about 45 cm long and 35 cm wide, apparently sessile but carried on a stalk up to 10 cm long hidden by the cataphylls;
  • Seeds: about 400 per cone, with an orange-red sarcotesta; sclerotesta ellipsoid and smooth, 30 to 35 mm long and 25 to 30 mm wide.

Jungle Music, a nursery in southern California, describes leaves that can exceed twelve feet (about 3.7 m) and bright green cones in cultivation; it writes that the species tends to be solitary and only occasionally produces basal suckers, whereas Hurter describes it as suckering at the base.

Hybrids

No natural hybrid of Encephalartos whitelockii is documented. The question arises mainly for cultivated plants: Tang and co-authors (2018) stress that seedlings raised in the village nurseries of the Mpanga Gorge are much less likely to be hybrids than those produced in botanic gardens or commercial nurseries, where several species can cross.

Possible confusion

According to its author, the closest species is Encephalartos hildebrandtii, from the coast of Kenya and Tanzania, which it resembles in its stiff, toothed, spiny green leaves. Hurter (1995) separates them even without cones: in Encephalartos hildebrandtii the leaflets are ascending, overlap and have their tips turned towards the base of the leaf, whereas in Encephalartos whitelockii they are spreading, do not overlap and point towards the apex. The cones differ too: the male cones of Encephalartos whitelockii hang down when ripe, those of Encephalartos hildebrandtii remain erect, and the scales of the female cones of Encephalartos whitelockii are extended downwards, which is not the case in the coastal species.

The history of the species shows that confusion was long the rule. The plants of the Mpanga valley were known from the early twentieth century: Prain (1917), in the Flora of Tropical Africa, referred them to Encephalartos laurentianus, from the Congo basin, on the strength of observations by the British administrator Harry Johnston, who described them in open woodland strewn with boulders at the south-eastern foot of the Rwenzori. Melville (1957) then placed them in Encephalartos hildebrandtii, while judging them intermediate between that species and Encephalartos laurentianus and expecting that their status might be revised with more complete material. Hurter did so in 1995.

The other large green Encephalartos of Uganda, Encephalartos equatorialis, from the northern shore of Lake Victoria (Mayuge District), and Encephalartos macrostrobilus, from the north-west of the country, live far from the Mpanga; no published source compares their characters directly with those of Encephalartos whitelockii. The same applies to Encephalartos ituriensis, from the east of the Democratic Republic of the Congo: a grower in Poland who had sown both species at the end of 2009 merely noted on the Fous de palmiers forum, in 2013, that his Encephalartos ituriensis grew much more slowly.

Taxonomy

Johan Hurter, of the Lowveld National Botanical Garden at Nelspruit (South Africa), described the species in June 1995 in the journal Phytologia (78(6): 410–411), in an article on two new cycads from Africa, together with Encephalartos nubimontanus. The type is a leaf and a section of male cone collected on 27 October 1994 at the falls of the Mpanga River, at 1,200 m (Hurter 94U/3a, holotype in Pretoria, PRE); a second number, 94U/5, provided the female cone illustrated in the article.

In the same year, P. Vorster and P. Heibloem published in the South African Journal of Botany (61(6): 347–351) a “new species from Uganda”, Encephalartos successibus. The World List of Cycads and Species+ treat it as a synonym of Encephalartos whitelockii, whose name, published earlier in the year, has priority. The published sources do not say whether the two descriptions were based on the same populations.

Etymology and history

The epithet honours Loran M. Whitelock (1930–2014), a horticulturist and cycad specialist, author of the book The Cycads (2002): Hurter writes that he had made most of his recent explorations in central and eastern Africa possible, and that he had devoted his life to cycads (Hurter, 1995; Haynes, 2022, via The World List of Cycads).

The large colonies of the Mpanga were not unknown before 1995: Harry Johnston had reported them in the early twentieth century, and collections from the valley were kept in herbaria under other names (see Possible confusion). It was Hurter's fieldwork in Uganda in 1994 that led to their recognition as a distinct species. Since then the gorge has become a reference site for the conservation of African cycads, and tourist trails have been opened there (Kew, 2024).

Common names

The account of the Mpanga Gorge as a Tropical Important Plant Area, published by the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, uses the English name “Muhure cycad”, formed on a local name; the language of origin of this name is not stated.

In the wild

Encephalartos whitelockii is known only from the gorge of the Mpanga River and the hills around it, in Kamwenge District, western Uganda. According to the Kew account (2024), the Important Plant Area corresponding to its population covers 6.16 km²; the gorge runs down towards Lake George, 3 km to the south-west, with slopes of up to about 35° and altitudes from 920 m on the valley floor to almost 2,000 m on the ridges. The type was collected at the falls, at 1,200 m.

The species forms almost pure stands on the very steep, rocky slopes lining the gorge, next to a riverine forest of Cynometra alexandri and Baphia wollastonii; higher up, it mixes with a wooded savanna of Combretum, Bridelia and Cymbopogon grasses, on very stony soils (Kalema, 2012, cited by Kew). The bedrock consists of partly granitised Precambrian rocks, mainly phyllites and schists, with quartzites. Roberts (2008) considered this concentration of large tree-like Encephalartos over about 73 ha to be unique in the world.

Population estimates vary: 5,000 to 10,000 plants in the IUCN action plan (Donaldson, 2003), about 8,000 individuals according to the 2010 assessment cited by Kew, and 6,000 to 8,000 according to the Darwin Initiative project (2014–2017), which could not georeference them because it had no access to the land of the power station. Field surveys show many seedlings and young plants but few individuals between that stage and maturity, which suggests repeated disturbance; maturity may take about ten years (Ogwal, 2017, cited by Kew). Germination was long thought to depend on baboons, but the seeds germinate without difficulty in the seedbeds of the village nurseries (Kew, 2024).

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos whitelockii is listed as Critically Endangered (CR) on the IUCN Red List. The assessment in force, by J.D. Bösenberg, is dated 6 March 2020 and was published in the Red List 2024-1 (GBIF, Crossref, re-checked on 26 October 2026). The previous assessment, by J. Kalema (October 2009, published in 2010), already listed it as Critically Endangered under criteria B1ab(iii,v)+2ab(iii,v), that is a tiny range, a single location and a decline in habitat and in the number of individuals; it is still the one cited by The World List of Cycads. In 2003 the IUCN action plan listed it as Vulnerable, with a decline judged slight.

The main threat came from a hydroelectric project. Roberts (2008) described in the journal Oryx the small Mpanga power station, planned for 18 MW: a weir just above the 40 m falls was to divert the river into a side channel and a power house below. The Uganda Wildlife Authority had challenged the environmental impact assessment, but in May 2008 visitors found newly cut roads in the gorge, lined with heaps of crushed cycads. According to the Kew account (2024), the dam was built in 2011 despite these objections; the tracks cut along the contours, where the cycads are densest, killed many plants, the works encouraged invasive plants (Lantana camara, Ricinus communis, Mimosa pigra), and the effect on the species of the change in flow and in the spray from the falls remains poorly known. The other threats are agriculture, fires lit to renew grazing, trampling by cattle coming down to drink at the river and some collection of ornamental plants. More than 90% of the population lies outside Queen Elizabeth National Park, without formal protection (Kew, 2024; Rufford Foundation, 2016).

Conservation work has followed. Ugandan organisations, with the IUCN Save Our Species programme, installed pumps to water cattle away from the gorge, marked out the cycad area and set up village nurseries. The project “Protecting Ugandan endemic cycads from biodiversity loss and trafficking”, funded by the British Darwin Initiative from April 2014 to March 2017 and coordinated by Kew, had about 7,000 seeds collected, some 6,700 plants raised and more than 5,000 young plants replanted in the gorge, on both sides of the river (final report, 2017). Tang and co-authors (2018) count more than 6,000 plants reintroduced from the two village nurseries of Kalere and Kanara; how many have survived is not yet known.

Regulation, according to Species+ (consulted through its public API on 26 October 2026):

  • CITES: Appendix I since 4 February 1977, like the whole genus;
  • European Union: Annex A, current list in Regulation (EU) 2026/1383 (in force since 29 June 2026); a negative EU opinion covering the whole genus for artificially propagated plants from South Africa, issued on 3 December 2010, was lifted on 11 March 2011; for this species, a negative opinion of its own was issued on 20 June 2011, confirmed on 7 February 2013, and remains in force.

Seeds are covered in the same way as plants. Any plant offered for sale must come from cultivation and carry the CITES documents required by the countries concerned (and, in the European Union, the EU documents). These notes do not replace the official texts: check Species+ and the national authorities before any purchase or import.

Growing Encephalartos whitelockii

Reliable sources on cultivation are few, but they agree on the vigour of young plants. The species suckers at the base (Hurter, 1995) and lives on very free-draining rocky slopes between forest and savanna: a free-draining compost, bright light, regular watering in the warm season and frost-free winters suit young plants.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

Jungle Music, at Encinitas near San Diego, calls it a fast grower, adaptable and easy to grow, which does well in sun on the coast and inland, grows less quickly in partial shade, and should be given partial sun or shade in desert areas; the nursery recommends a well-drained, slightly acidic topsoil and plenty of room, as the plant forms a large trunk and very long, upright leaves. No dated in-ground result from a temperate or Mediterranean climate has been published.

Container cultivation

  • South-eastern France: in the Var, a collector showed a young Encephalartos whitelockii about two years old on the Fous de palmiers forum in August 2010. In December 2012 he explained that all his Encephalartos are in pots and overwintered in a heated glasshouse, and that among his young plants of the same age, grown in the same conditions, Encephalartos whitelockii was more vigorous than Encephalartos gratus or Encephalartos ferox; he would not, however, have planted it outdoors all year round.
  • Poland: a grower who had sown the species at the end of 2009 reported on the same forum in September 2013 that the plant had leaves 115 cm long and a caudex about 8 cm across, and that he had repotted it into a larger pot, in a free-draining mix of compost and gravel; his Encephalartos overwintered indoors or in the cellar without losing their leaves. In September 2014 the plant had produced two new leaves and stood 2 m tall with its pot. In February 2015 he regretted that it had pushed out, in January, in an overheated room, a leaf more than a metre long that he would rather have seen grow outdoors in the sun.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

BGCI PlantSearch lists 28 institutions declaring Encephalartos whitelockii. None is in the United Kingdom. In the United States, the Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida) lists eight accessions in its online catalogue, whose numbers point to 1976, 1978, 2000 and 2006 (five); BGCI also lists Fairchild, the Huntington, Lotusland, Descanso Gardens, the San Diego Botanic Garden, the San Diego Zoo and its Safari Park, the Cal State Fullerton Arboretum, UC Berkeley, the New York Botanical Garden, the Marie Selby gardens, the National Tropical Botanical Garden and the University of Hawaii garden at Hilo, as well as the U.S. National Plant Germplasm System. In Australia, Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria grows it in outdoor beds at Melbourne Gardens according to its living collection census, the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney lists it in its Garden Explorer, and the Brisbane (Mount Coot-tha), Adelaide, Cairns and Noosa gardens and James Cook University at Townsville declare it. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not a living plant on display today. Jungle Music describes the species as fairly rare yet reasonably affordable, much sought after for its long upright leaves, and usually offers a range of sizes.

Propagation

Seed is the usual method: a female cone carries about 400 seeds (Hurter, 1995). In the village nurseries of the Mpanga Gorge, the seeds germinated well in seedbeds (eight seedbeds with a capacity of 6,400 seeds according to Kew), and the Darwin Initiative project raised some 6,700 plants in three years. Adult plants sucker at the base (Hurter, 1995), but no published source describes the removal of offsets in this species. Seeds are covered by CITES Appendix I as well. For general advice, see the article on growing cycads from seed.

Pests and diseases

No disease specific to Encephalartos whitelockii is documented. In the wild, Roberts (2008) reports that the gorge harbours insects associated with cycads, including a beetle of the genus Antliarhinus first observed there in 2007. In cultivation, the main risk remains, as for all Encephalartos, rot of the roots and caudex in cold, waterlogged compost; a grower in Poland also noted in 2013 that wind damaged the young, developing leaves of Encephalartos put outdoors.

Cold hardiness

No measured cold limit is documented for Encephalartos whitelockii, nor any temperature record from the Mpanga Gorge, an equatorial valley at middle altitude. Jungle Music gives no cold limit for it either. As with other Encephalartos, survivals in cold areas often concern young plants whose caudex is still underground, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil; an adult plant with an aerial trunk, as this species becomes, does not benefit from this protection.

Mediterranean and European experience

No in-ground trial is documented in France or Italy: the plants reported on the Fous de palmiers forum, in the Var and in Poland, are in pots and overwintered in a heated glasshouse or indoors (2010–2015). For readers in comparable climates, such as coastal California or southern Australia, a large container overwintered frost-free remains the prudent choice until in-ground results are published.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

  • Australia: plants grow in outdoor beds at Melbourne Gardens (Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria census), without published winter data.
  • United States: Jungle Music grows the species in coastal southern California, without giving a cold limit; no dated, measured report was found.
  • United Kingdom: no British garden declares the species, and no outdoor result is documented.

No USDA hardiness zone is documented for Encephalartos whitelockii in a botanical source.

Toxicity and precautions

Like other cycads, Encephalartos whitelockii contains toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a toxin affecting the liver and the nervous system; the seeds are the most dangerous part. No study deals with the toxicity of this species in particular, but the plant must not be considered harmless, even if its cones are said to be eaten locally (see Traditional uses). Wear gloves to clean the seeds, and keep plants and seeds out of reach of children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

According to Kalema and Beentje (2012), cited by Kew, Encephalartos whitelockii is used as a building material around the gorge, and its cones are eaten. Local people also sell plants raised in the village nurseries, a source of income encouraged by the conservation projects. Food preparations based on cycads require long treatment to remove the toxins and must not be imitated.

Frequently asked questions

Where does Encephalartos whitelockii grow?

Only in the gorge of the Mpanga River and on the neighbouring hills, in western Uganda, near Lake George. The whole wild population of the species lives on a few square kilometres.

Why is it Critically Endangered when thousands remain?

Because the whole species depends on a single, already damaged site. The building of a small hydroelectric power station at the Mpanga Falls destroyed many plants, and agriculture, fires and cattle put pressure on the rest of the population, almost entirely outside the neighbouring national park.

Who was Whitelock?

Loran M. Whitelock (1930–2014), a horticulturist and cycad specialist, author of The Cycads (2002). He funded Hurter's explorations in central and eastern Africa.

How can it be told from Encephalartos hildebrandtii?

By the leaflets and cones: in Encephalartos whitelockii the leaflets spread, do not overlap and point towards the apex of the leaf, and the male cones hang down when ripe; in Encephalartos hildebrandtii the leaflets overlap and point towards the base, and the male cones remain erect.

Does it grow fast?

According to growers, yes, for an Encephalartos: sown at the end of 2009, a plant grown in Poland had leaves 115 cm long in 2013, a collector in the Var found it more vigorous than Encephalartos gratus or Encephalartos ferox of the same age, and Jungle Music calls it a rapid grower.

Is it cold-hardy?

No reliable data allow this to be said. The plants grown in France and Poland are overwintered in a heated glasshouse or indoors; grow it in a container overwintered frost-free wherever frosts occur.

Is it poisonous?

It should be treated as such, like all cycads, especially its seeds.
