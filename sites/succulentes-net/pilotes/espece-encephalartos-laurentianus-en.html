Encephalartos laurentianus, the Kwango giant cycad, is a huge cycad of Central Africa that grows wild only in the valley of the Kwango, the river that forms the border between the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Angola, on steep banks, sandstone cliffs and in the gallery forests of the river and its tributaries. Described in 1903 by the Belgian botanist Émile De Wildeman and named after Émile Laurent, professor at the agricultural institute of Gembloux, this species of the genus Encephalartos forms trunks 10 to 15 m long, often lying on the ground, and carries leaves 4 to 7 m long with leaflets of 35 to 50 cm. Its male cones are carmine red. The IUCN lists it as Near Threatened. Plants brought to Belgium early in the twentieth century were still grown in 1991 at Meise Botanic Garden, where the species was hand-pollinated for the first time. In cultivation it is a fast-growing, frost-tender plant for tropical gardens and large heated glasshouses.

Key facts about Encephalartos laurentianus

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos laurentianus De Wild. (1903), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15), Species+ and GBIF
  • Former names and synonyms: no synonym; plants from Uganda long referred to this species belong to Encephalartos whitelockii
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Kwango giant cycad (Species+); “Malele”, a local name recorded by De Wildeman (1903)
  • Natural range: the Kwango valley and the valleys of its tributaries, on both sides of the border between the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Angola, from the confluence with the Fufu to that with the Kikasu (Melville, 1957)
  • IUCN status: Near Threatened (NT, criterion B1a), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated 3 April 2020 and published in 2022; Data Deficient (DD) in the IUCN action plan of 2003
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I (whole genus, since 1977); Annex A in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: no measured value; dated reports concern plants under glass (Meise), in pots (France, Naples) or outdoors in the Canary Islands (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, steep banks and sandstone cliffs, gallery forest, riverside grassland and sometimes open steppe in full sun; in cultivation, warmth, bright filtered light and generous watering in the growing season
  • Habit and adult size: trunk 10 m (De Wildeman, 1903) to 15 m long (Melville, 1957), often reclining; leaves 4 to 6 m, sometimes 7 m; median leaflets 35 to 50 cm by 4 to 7 cm; female cones 35 to 45 cm
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Cambridge (wild-collected accession of 1970); Montgomery Botanical Center (accessions of 1996 and 2014), Fairchild and others in the United States; Royal Botanic Garden Sydney and Adelaide in Australia
  • Toxicity: seeds poisonous, as in all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos laurentianus

It is recognised by its sheer size: a whitish trunk, often lying down and winding, leaves several metres long with about a hundred and twenty pairs of very large toothed leaflets, and cones covered in reddish down. Melville (1957), who revised the Encephalartos of Central Africa, gives the following characters:

  • Trunk: up to 15 m long and 125 cm in diameter, often reclining and snaking, whitish, marked with the scars of the leaf bases
  • Leaves: linear-oblong, 4 to 6 m long and 50 to 90 cm wide, with about 120 pairs of leaflets; rachis and swollen base of the petiole at first covered with reddish down
  • Leaflets: the median ones linear-lanceolate, 35 to 50 cm long and 4 to 7 cm wide, with an acuminate, pungent or three-toothed tip and slightly reflexed margins bearing 9 to 15 sharp, fairly evenly spaced teeth; the lower leaflets are gradually reduced to branched, then simple, prickles
  • Male cones: 2 to 6, sometimes 8, ellipsoid to subconical, 17 to 35 cm long and 6 to 10 cm wide, on a stalk 25 to 30 cm long covered with red down; De Wildeman (1903) described them as carmine red
  • Female cones: 2 to 4, ellipsoid to oblong-ovoid, 35 to 45 cm long and about 20 cm wide, on a short stalk; scales covered with dark red down
  • Seeds: vermilion, oblong-ellipsoid, 40 to 50 mm long and 24 to 28 mm wide

De Wildeman's original description (1903), based on a male cone and leaves, gave a trunk up to 10 m tall and 60 to 70 cm across, and leaves 6 to 7 m long. Crosiers and Malaisse (1995) sum up: stems up to 15 m long, leaves usually 4 to 6 m but sometimes 7 m; they call it a “giant cycad”. Jungle Music, a cycad nursery in Encinitas, California, adds a grower's view: the leaflets are heavily armed but pliable to the touch, whereas the petioles carry long, very sharp spines and prickles; the nursery describes the female cones as ruddy brown and the male cones as mainly yellow with hints of brown, colours that differ from the red down noted by botanists and may reflect the stage of the cones.

Hybrids

No natural or garden hybrid of Encephalartos laurentianus is documented. At Meise Botanic Garden, the 1991 pollination was made with pollen from a male plant of the same species (Crosiers and Malaisse, 1995).

Possible confusion

The most persistent confusion concerns Uganda. In the Flora of Tropical Africa, Prain (1917) referred plants from the Mpanga valley, at the foot of the Rwenzori, to Encephalartos laurentianus; Melville (1957) excluded them and placed them in Encephalartos hildebrandtii, and in 1995 they were described as Encephalartos whitelockii. Likewise, Bamps and Lisowski (1990) consider probably mistaken a record of the species from Ituri, made by Robyns (1948) on sterile material, which corresponds to Encephalartos ituriensis.

Both species have smaller leaflets than Encephalartos laurentianus. In Encephalartos ituriensis, the only other species near the equator with soft leaflets, they reach 30 by 3 cm, and the female cones lack the reddish down of Encephalartos laurentianus (Vorster and Gereau, 1998). In Melville's key (1957), Encephalartos laurentianus stands out among the Central African species by its uniformly hairy female cone scales, its crest of finger-like tubercles and its leaves 4 to 7 m long with about 120 pairs of leaflets. Jungle Music compares the leaflet tips, armed with several spines, to a pitchfork; botanists describe a tip that is acuminate and pungent or three-toothed, which is not a diagnostic character on its own.

Taxonomy

Émile De Wildeman described the species in 1903 in the Annales du Musée du Congo (series V, Botany, 1(1): 10, plate 25), from a collection made by L. Gentil, forestry inspector of the Congo Free State, downstream of Kasongo on the Kwango (Gentil 98, holotype at Meise, BR). The original description gives September 1902; Melville (1957) and Crosiers and Malaisse (1995) date the collection to August 1902. De Wildeman then knew only the male cone; he returned to the species in 1904, in his Notices sur des plantes utiles ou intéressantes de la flore du Congo, then Lebrun (1930) devoted an illustrated note to it after finding the populations again, before Melville's synthesis (1957).

The World List of Cycads gives no synonym. The mistaken attribution to Encephalartos laurentianus of plants from Uganda (now Encephalartos whitelockii) and from Ituri is explained under Possible confusion.

Etymology and history

The epithet honours Émile Laurent (1861–1904), to whom De Wildeman dedicated the species because he had introduced the first Encephalartos from the Congo into Belgium (De Wildeman, 1903). According to Crosiers and Malaisse (1995), those first plants were Encephalartos poggei; The World List of Cycads, citing Haynes (2022), states instead that Laurent introduced Encephalartos laurentianus itself into cultivation, and Jungle Music that he first collected it. Laurent's dates come from Haynes.

The history of the species is tied to Belgium. In 1902 Gentil found a population of several hundred plants along the sandstone cliffs of the Kwango, downstream of the hamlet of Kasongo-Lunda, and sent two hundred young plants to the Colonial Garden at Laeken, near Brussels (Crosiers and Malaisse, 1995); as early as 1903 De Wildeman noted that this garden had “several fine living plants”. In January 1930 Jean Lebrun found the locality again and mapped the range of the species; in October 1948 Walter Robyns photographed a female plant there, which served as the model for the colour plate of the Flore du Congo belge et du Ruanda-Urundi.

The plants grown at the National Botanic Garden of Belgium, at Meise, since the early twentieth century had never produced fertile seed when, in September 1991, a male and a female plant coned there at the same time. Catherine Crosiers and François Malaisse hand-pollinated them from 16 October 1991; the female cones released their seeds in August 1992, ten months later. This was, according to them, the first known ex situ pollination of the species.

Common names

In English, Species+ uses the name Kwango giant cycad, after the river to which the species is confined. De Wildeman (1903) reports that the local people knew the plant and called it “Malele”, without saying in which language, but did not use it. No other vernacular name is documented.

In the wild

Encephalartos laurentianus lives only in the Kwango valley, from its confluence with the Fufu in the north to that with the Kikasu in the south, and in the valleys of its tributaries, on both sides of the border between the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Angola (Melville, 1957; Crosiers and Malaisse, 1995). The World List of Cycads and Species+ record it from both countries.

According to Gentil's information reported by De Wildeman (1903), the species seems confined to the steep banks of the Kwango, on the Congolese and Angolan sides alike, without straying from the river; it sometimes grows there in groups of several hundred plants in riverside grassland and is found even in open steppe in full sun. Its roots are taproots, whitish and very well developed. Lebrun found it in 1930 in the gallery forests that line the Kwango and most of its tributaries (Crosiers and Malaisse, 1995).

During an expedition in 1988, the populations reported where the species had been described were not found again, according to a communication from J.-P. Sclavo and A. Moretti cited by Crosiers and Malaisse (1995), who then considered it “particularly rare in the wild”. No figures have been published on current numbers, pollinators or seed dispersers, and no climate data are available for the localities.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos laurentianus is listed as Near Threatened (NT) on the IUCN Red List, under criterion B1a, which refers to the small extent of its range. The current assessment, by J.D. Bösenberg, is dated 3 April 2020 and was published in 2022 (GBIF, Crossref); the previous one, by the same author, dates from October 2009. The species does not appear in the IUCN table of category changes for 2022, which indicates that its category did not change between the two assessments. In 2003 the IUCN action plan listed it as Data Deficient (DD), and as Rare (R) in the 1997 list.

Regulation, according to Species+ (consulted through its public API on 26 October 2026):

  • CITES: Appendix I since 4 February 1977, like the whole genus; seeds are covered as well as plants;
  • European Union: Annex A, current list in Regulation (EU) 2026/1383; for artificially propagated plants from South Africa, the EU Scientific Review Group has given a positive opinion since 20 June 2011 for plants with a caudex up to 7 cm in diameter, and a negative opinion, confirmed on 7 February 2013, above that size;
  • trade suspensions affecting Angola (2016, 2024) and the Democratic Republic of the Congo (2014, 2015) are recorded in Species+ but are no longer in force.

Any plant offered for sale must come from cultivation and be accompanied by the required CITES and national documents. These notes do not replace the official texts: check Species+ and EUR-Lex before any purchase or import.

Growing Encephalartos laurentianus

Encephalartos laurentianus is a plant for warm climates, known for growing fast when heat and water are plentiful. On the Italian Tropicamente forum, a grower in the Canary Islands wrote in December 2008 that at his home Encephalartos laurentianus and Encephalartos arenarius “grow like grass”, and compared the former to “a banana among cycads”, putting out new leaves very often while consuming a great deal of water and fertiliser; a collector in Salento, Apulia, considered it the same month “perhaps the fastest of all Encephalartos”. Its adult size makes it a plant for a very tall glasshouse or a tropical garden: at Meise it has been grown under glass since the early twentieth century (Crosiers and Malaisse, 1995).

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

Jungle Music, writing from coastal southern California on an undated page, advises filtered light or shade in all areas: full sun is possible only in the tropics or in very humid areas, and the leaves burn in drier climates. The nursery recommends a rich topsoil that drains well, notes that the species tolerates plenty of water and tropical plantings, and stresses that it needs space and protection, ideally under an established canopy of tall trees or palms, where a young plant without much trunk already sends its leaves above the roof of a single-storey house. In the Canary Islands the species grows outdoors, and fast (Tropicamente, 2008); the plants photographed on this page are grown at the Palmetum of Santa Cruz de Tenerife and at the Jardín de Aclimatación de La Orotava. No in-ground planting is documented in mainland Europe.

Container cultivation

Young plants can be grown in pots for a few years. A grower in Naples described on Tropicamente, in June 2010, seedlings with a trunk about 3 cm across grown in 10 cm pots in a mix of peat and perlite: they had spent their first winter indoors without trouble and stood in full sun and rain for the summer. On the Côte Bleue, near Marseille in south-east France, a collector wrote on the Fous de palmiers forum in November 2014 that he grew Encephalartos laurentianus in a pot, while other cycads of his collection were in the ground. Given its growth rate, a pot only postpones the question of space; in Britain and other cool climates, only a large heated glasshouse can hold an adult plant.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In Britain, Cambridge University Botanic Garden lists in its online catalogue an accession of wild origin received in 1970 according to its number, and also declares it to BGCI PlantSearch; no other British garden does. In the United States, Montgomery Botanical Center in Coral Gables, Florida, holds two accessions, received in 1996 and 2014 according to their numbers; Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden lists it in its Garden Explorer; Ganna Walska Lotusland, the San Diego Botanic Garden, the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley, the Miami Beach Botanical Garden, the University of Hawaii at Hilo, Waimea Valley in Hawaii and the US National Plant Germplasm System declare it to BGCI. Jungle Music reports that it often has young plants for sale, at prices among the highest for green cycads, because seed is almost never available. In Australia, the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney lists the species in its online catalogue and the Botanic Garden of South Australia in Adelaide declares it to BGCI. In Italy, the Naples Botanical Garden held a specimen in 1993–94 (Gaudio and colleagues, 1997) and the Messina Botanical Garden listed the species in 2009. A BGCI listing shows that a garden has declared the species at some point, not that a plant is alive today.

Propagation

Seed is the documented method. At Meise, Crosiers and Malaisse (1995) proceeded as follows:

  • they collected the pollen by shaking the male cones over a sheet of paper and kept part of it at 4 °C (39.2 °F);
  • they introduced it into the female cones by removing a scale at the top and with a brush between the scales, three times in one week;
  • each female cone gave about 160 orange-red seeds, 4.7 by 2.4 cm, of which 40% were fertile according to the flotation test;
  • ten seeds sown in pots, under glass, on 1 October 1992 came up between 15 December 1992 and 5 January 1993, a germination rate of 60%; at eighteen months the seedlings already had coralloid roots.

No published source describes the removal of suckers in this species, although Jungle Music notes that large specimens more often carry basal suckers and offshoots than single stems. Seeds, like plants, fall under CITES Appendix I.

Pests and diseases

No disease or pest specific to Encephalartos laurentianus is documented. As in other Encephalartos, the main risk in cultivation is rot of the roots and trunk in a cold, waterlogged mix, a risk that grows when a plant used to tropical warmth is watered generously in cool weather.

Cold hardiness

No measured value of cold hardiness is documented for Encephalartos laurentianus, and no temperature record has been published for the Kwango valley. Jungle Music describes it as not very frost-tolerant, “truly a tropical species” of Encephalartos, and no source reports a plant that has come through frost.

Mediterranean and European experience

No record has been published for a plant grown outdoors in France or Italy: the only plant reported on the Fous de palmiers forum is in a pot, and the young Neapolitan plants spend the winter indoors. In the Canary Islands, by contrast, the species grows fast in the open. As with other Encephalartos, cold survivals reported for the genus often concern young plants with an underground caudex, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil and sometimes by snow; an adult plant with an aerial trunk, which this species quickly becomes, has no such protection. A large container kept frost-free in winter remains the prudent choice, even on the Mediterranean coast.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No dated cold report has been published from these countries, and no botanical source gives a USDA zone; the zone ratings found on nursery websites cite no measurement. The American gardens that declare it are mostly in Florida, Hawaii and California, and Jungle Music grows it in coastal southern California, in shade or filtered light; in Britain it is a plant for a heated glasshouse.

Toxicity and precautions

Like other cycads, Encephalartos laurentianus should be regarded as poisonous. Encephalartos contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a toxin of the liver and the nervous system; the seeds are the most dangerous part. No study deals with the toxicity of this species itself, but the risk documented in other species of the genus means it should not be considered harmless. The ASPCA reports vomiting, diarrhoea, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses that have eaten cycads.

Wear gloves to clean seeds, and keep plants and seeds out of reach of children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No use is documented: as early as 1903 De Wildeman noted that the inhabitants of the Kwango valley knew the plant but did not use it.

Frequently asked questions

Where does Encephalartos laurentianus grow?

Only in the Kwango valley and the valleys of its tributaries, on both sides of the border between the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Angola, on steep banks, sandstone cliffs and in gallery forest.

How big does Encephalartos laurentianus get?

It is a giant among cycads: a trunk 10 to 15 m long, often lying down, leaves 4 to 6 m and sometimes 7 m long, and leaflets of 35 to 50 cm. The female cones measure 35 to 45 cm.

Does Encephalartos laurentianus grow in Uganda?

No. The plants of the Mpanga valley in Uganda, long referred to this species, were described in 1995 as Encephalartos whitelockii. The species is known only from the Kwango.

Does Encephalartos laurentianus grow fast?

Yes, when it is warm. Growers in the Canary Islands and southern Italy described it on Tropicamente in 2008 as one of the fastest Encephalartos, putting out new leaves very often, provided it gets plenty of water and fertiliser.

Can Encephalartos laurentianus be grown outdoors?

Only where frost is rare or absent. It thrives outdoors in the Canary Islands, and Jungle Music grows it in coastal southern California in filtered light; elsewhere it needs a frost-free glasshouse in winter.

Who was Laurent?

Émile Laurent (1861–1904), professor at the agricultural institute of Gembloux, who had introduced the first Encephalartos from the Congo into Belgium. De Wildeman dedicated the species to him in 1903.

Is Encephalartos laurentianus toxic?

It should be treated as such, like all cycads, especially its seeds.
