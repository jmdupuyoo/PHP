Encephalartos middelburgensis, the Middelburg cycad, is a tall blue cycad of the South African highveld, known only from the upper catchment of the Olifants River between Middelburg and Loskop Dam, in Mpumalanga, with a few plants in Gauteng. Long confused with Encephalartos eugene-maraisii, of which it was briefly a subspecies (Encephalartos eugene-maraisii subsp. middelburgensis), this species of the genus Encephalartos was described in 1989. It forms trunks that can exceed 6 m, often in clumps, crowned with stiff, straight, glaucous leaves whose leaflets have no teeth. Poaching has brought it close to extinction: the IUCN lists it as Critically Endangered (assessment published in 2022), and South Africa's CITES Scientific Authority estimated in 2015 that no more than 350 wild plants remained, after the loss of about 59% of the plants counted in 1983. In cultivation it is a sun-loving blue cycad, reputedly frost-hardy, but seldom offered and expensive.

Key facts about Encephalartos middelburgensis

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos middelburgensis Vorster, Robbertse & S.van der Westh. (1989), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15), Species+ and GBIF
  • Former name: Encephalartos eugene-maraisii subsp. middelburgensis Lavranos & D.L.Goode (1988), a synonym according to The World List of Cycads
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Middelburg cycad (PlantZAfrica, Species+)
  • Natural range: South Africa, upper Olifants River catchment, Middelburg and Witbank (eMalahleni) districts of Mpumalanga, marginally in Gauteng; the 2017 management plan also cites the Bronkhorstspruit area
  • IUCN status: Critically Endangered (CR), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated 13 May 2020 and published in 2022; already CR in the 2003 IUCN action plan
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I since 1977, like the whole genus; Annex A in the European Union, with a negative EU opinion on artificially propagated plants from South Africa; wild collection banned in South Africa since February 2007; national Biodiversity Management Plan (2017)
  • Documented hardiness: no measured value in cultivation; brief night frosts in a dry winter in the wild; described as frost-hardy by PlantZAfrica (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, rocky sites and steep grassy slopes on slightly acidic quartzite, in full sun, between 1,000 and 1,400 m, with about 600 mm of summer rain; in cultivation, full sun and a very free-draining soil
  • Habit and adult size: trunk up to 6 m (7 m according to PlantZAfrica) and 25 to 40 cm across, in clumps of up to twelve stems; leaves 1.1 to 1.5 m; male cones 30 to 35 cm (45 to 70 cm according to PlantZAfrica), female cones 35 to 45 cm
  • In the UK, US and Australia: the Huntington, Berkeley, Atlanta, San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance and two Hawaiian gardens in the United States; Royal Botanic Garden Sydney and Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria, Melbourne (outdoor bed) in Australia; no British garden declares it
  • Toxicity: seeds poisonous, as in all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos middelburgensis

Look for tall trunks, often in clumps, blue-green leaves that are stiff and straight on a true, spineless petiole, narrow toothless leaflets held almost folded against one another, and above all lower leaflets reduced to simple, entire spines towards the base of the leaf. The original description by Robbertse, Vorster and van der Westhuizen (1989) gives these characters:

  • Trunk: arborescent, unbranched but often suckering from the base, upright then sometimes reclining with age, up to 6 m long and 25 to 40 cm across; PlantZAfrica describes clumps of up to twelve trunks and trunks up to 7 m
  • Leaves: 1.1 to 1.5 m long, glaucous, straight and stiff, on a well-developed, three-angled, spineless petiole 10 to 20 cm long; the tip curves very slightly upwards (PlantZAfrica); young leaves are often silvery with a dense bloom
  • Leaflets: pointing towards the tip at 45° or less and almost folded against one another; the median ones 18 to 19 cm long and 14 to 19 mm wide, very narrowly elliptic, slightly S-shaped, with spineless margins (rarely one or two spines in young plants); towards the base they are spaced 2.5 to 5 cm apart and reduced to single spines
  • Male cones: five to eight per stem, narrowly ovoid, 30 to 35 cm long and 8.5 to 12 cm across, on a stalk of 5 to 17 cm, the scales drawn out into a drooping lip
  • Female cones: four or five (up to eight) per stem, shortly cylindrical, 35 to 45 cm long and 17 to 20 cm across, apparently stalkless, their stalk (up to 15 cm) hidden among the scale leaves at the top of the trunk
  • Seeds: 170 to 260 per cone, yellow to light brown, 38 to 40 mm long and 22 to 24 mm wide (PlantZAfrica)

The colour of the cones is described differently depending on the source. The original description calls them bright green and hairless, with only patches of short, sparse reddish-brown down, whereas PlantZAfrica (2013) describes them as reddish brown and covered with fine brown hairs, and gives male cones 45 to 70 cm long; Jungle Music, a nursery in southern California, describes yellowish green female cones with fine brown hairs and darker male cones covered in dark brown hairs. PlantZAfrica adds that the cones do not break up on their own as in other species, but dry out in September and October, releasing their seeds. According to the original description, the cones emerge in midsummer, pollination takes place around April, and coning occurs only at intervals of several years.

Hybrids

No natural hybrid of Encephalartos middelburgensis is documented. In cultivation, hybridisation is possible wherever several related species cone at the same time: a collector who had lived in South Africa reported in 2013 on the French forum Fous de palmiers that seed of many rare species is sold there sterile or hybrid. The 2015 document of the South African Scientific Authority also stresses the uncertain origin of the parent plants in some nurseries.

Possible confusion

The authors of the species compared it, for its stiff, pungent, glaucous leaves and its tree-like habit, with Encephalartos eugene-maraisii, Encephalartos lehmannii, Encephalartos princeps and above all two species they had just described, Encephalartos graniticolus and Encephalartos verrucosus, now placed by The World List of Cycads in Encephalartos dyerianus and Encephalartos dolomiticus respectively. Encephalartos middelburgensis differs from all of them in combining a well-developed petiole with lower leaflets reduced to entire spines. It differs from Encephalartos eugene-maraisii in its four to eight cylindrical green female cones, instead of two ovoid rust-coloured cones, and from the graniticolus form of Encephalartos dyerianus in its clearly stalked leaves, with basal leaflets reduced to simple, untoothed spines, and in the scales of its male cones, drawn out into a drooping lip (Robbertse and colleagues, 1989).

Encephalartos cupidus, another blue species of the Olifants catchment, is hardly a source of confusion: it is a dwarf plant, with toothed adult leaflets and almost stalkless leaves. Hurter and Glen (1995) compared Encephalartos middelburgensis with Encephalartos hirsutus when they described the latter. In cultivation, labels are often uncertain: in 2013 a grower in southern Corsica doubted that his plant, bought as Encephalartos middelburgensis, really belonged to the species, as its leaflets bore small spines.

Taxonomy

In 1988 John Lavranos and Douglas Goode published the taxon as a subspecies of Encephalartos eugene-maraisii (Bulletin du Jardin botanique national de Belgique 58: 219–224). The following year Robbertse, Vorster and van der Westhuizen described it as a full species in the South African Journal of Botany (55(1): 122–126), from a dissertation by Suzelle van der Westhuizen. The type is a leaf and a female cone collected on 26 June 1970 on the farm Doornkop, north of Middelburg, at 1,400 m (Vorster, Codd and Melville 1898b, holotype in Pretoria, isotype at Kew); the description places it 20 km north of Middelburg, The World List of Cycads 19 km.

The authors recognised the species because of discontinuities in its vegetative and cone characters compared with the graniticolus form, and because of its distinct geographical range. They also noted that its leaflets have the same anatomy (isobilateral, with stomata on both faces) as those of Encephalartos eugene-maraisii, Encephalartos cupidus and the graniticolus form, four species that are geographical neighbours along the Olifants catchment. The World List of Cycads now treats Lavranos and Goode's subspecies as a synonym. For readers, a label reading “Encephalartos eugene-maraisii subsp. middelburgensis” refers to this species.

Etymology and history

The epithet refers to the district of Middelburg, in Mpumalanga, where the species was discovered and mainly occurs (Haynes, 2022, cited by The World List of Cycads; PlantZAfrica). According to the South African Scientific Authority, plants may have been taken from the wild under the name Encephalartos eugene-maraisii before the provincial laws of the 1970s, and seedlings from the public nursery at Hartebeesthoek, active from 1975 to 1998, reached collectors. An aerial and ground survey by the Transvaal conservation services in 1983 counted about 420 plants (Robbertse and colleagues, 1989).

Common names

The English name used by PlantZAfrica and Species+ is Middelburg cycad. No other name is documented in a reliable source.

In the wild

Encephalartos middelburgensis grows in the upper catchment of the Olifants River, between Middelburg and Loskop Dam, over some 45 km, in the Middelburg and Witbank districts of Mpumalanga, with a few plants in Gauteng. The national management plan (2017) speaks of the Witbank, Middelburg and Bronkhorstspruit areas of Mpumalanga and Gauteng, and of a fragmented distribution. The original description records it on rocky sites, mostly on steep slopes, more rarely on flat ground as at the type locality, on slightly acidic quartzite, in full sun, between 1,100 and 1,400 m (1,000 to 1,400 m according to PlantZAfrica), with typical species of the Bankenveld (Acacia caffra, Bequaertiodendron magalismontanum, Cussonia transvaalensis, Dombeya rotundifolia, Elephantorrhiza burkei); at the type locality it grows close to a large concentration of Encephalartos lanatus. Annual rainfall is about 600 mm, mostly in summer; summers range from moderate on flat ground to very hot in enclosed, north-facing gullies, and some plants probably experience winter frosts.

A member of the Fous de palmiers forum who had seen the species in the wild described in 2013 a very dry winter, almost without rain, with days sometimes reaching 20 °C (68 °F) after a frosty night, and frosts that never last more than a few hours; he mentioned a population reduced to four plants several kilometres apart, which no longer reproduced.

Natural regeneration is poor. As early as 1989, the authors reported that at the type locality five groups totalling about 32 plants produced only sterile seed, although both sexes coned at the same time, and that in a population of about 200 plants seedlings were almost absent, perhaps because of collectors. A conservation project supported in 2012 by the Mohamed bin Zayed Species Conservation Fund states that, during reassessments in 2009 and 2010, most females were isolated from males, cones did not appear at the same time, and no pollinating weevil was present in any subpopulation but one. PlantZAfrica notes that the fleshy seed coat is eaten by monkeys, birds, rodents and bats, which disperse the seeds.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos middelburgensis is Critically Endangered (CR) on the IUCN Red List. The current assessment, by J.D. Bösenberg, is dated 13 May 2020 and was published in 2022 (GBIF, Crossref); The World List of Cycads gives the criteria A2acde; C1, that is a rapid and continuing decline of a very small population, due in particular to collecting. In 2003 the IUCN action plan (Donaldson, 2003) already listed it as Critically Endangered, with 420 to 450 plants, a rapid decline, collecting and reproductive failure as threats; the 2017 management plan quotes the criteria A2acd+4acd; C1.

The non-detriment finding document of the South African Scientific Authority (approved in February 2012, updated in May 2015) details this collapse. It puts the wild population at no more than 350 plants: 100 to 200 in a provincial nature reserve in Mpumalanga, about 150 on private land and seven in Gauteng. New surveys of the plants counted in 1983 show a loss of about 59%, mainly through poaching for the horticultural trade and private collections. In 2006 and 2007 illegal removal of suckers was particularly intense, large plants often dying after being cut to detach them; whole plants were also stolen, and the Avontuur form is considered extinct. In 2008, 24 hand-pollinated wild cones were stolen; in 2011 some twenty poached plants were seized, three of them with trunks over 5 m, and fifteen 80 kg bags of suckers taken to Gauteng were never recovered. Two of the nine Gauteng plants counted in 2004 died, perhaps of a disease. The document notes that 30 to 50% of cycads taken from the wild die within a few years, that microchips implanted in many plants did not deter poachers, and that enforcement agencies were seriously understaffed. It concludes that trade in artificially propagated plants has been detrimental, and that exports of seedlings should come only from CITES-registered nurseries. The 2012 Mohamed bin Zayed project mentions a decline of more than 80% in 50 years and about 120 adults, according to Donaldson (2009).

The national Biodiversity Management Plan for the Critically Endangered and Endangered Encephalartos (Government Gazette No. 40793, 21 April 2017) puts the total population at fewer than 350 plants, most of them in a single nature reserve in Mpumalanga, with a decline of about 60% and continued poaching; outside the reserve, many plants now grow in isolation. It reports that the Mpumalanga Tourism and Parks Agency runs several restoration projects, that a small number of plants grow in a private nature reserve in Gauteng whose owner has submitted a management plan based on hand pollination, and that the species is represented in one viable ex situ collection in the National Botanical Gardens. Its targets are a research project on ecology and restoration, the planting of 140 additional seeds or seedlings in secure wild sites in Mpumalanga, and of at least 400 in the private reserve in Gauteng. The plan also mentions preliminary in vitro germination work by Kew's Millennium Seed Bank on this species, with 85% recovery of germination on a culture medium.

Regulation, according to Species+ (consulted through its public API on 26 October 2026):

  • CITES: Appendix I since 4 February 1977, like the whole genus;
  • European Union: Annex A, current list in Regulation (EU) 2026/1383; for artificially propagated plants from South Africa, a negative opinion covering the whole genus, issued on 3 December 2010, was lifted on 11 March 2011; a negative opinion specific to the species was issued on 20 June 2011, confirmed on 7 February 2013 and is still current; a new consultation covering the whole genus has been open since 16 September 2026;
  • South Africa: collection of wild cycads banned throughout the country since February 2007; a permit is required to possess a cycad (PlantZAfrica).

These notes do not replace the official texts: check Species+ and EUR-Lex before any purchase or import, and ask for documents proving that a plant was propagated in cultivation.

Growing Encephalartos middelburgensis

Reliable sources on cultivation are few. According to PlantZAfrica, the species thrives in full sun, is frost-hardy and is easily propagated from seed and suckers. In the wild it lives in full sun, on very free-draining rocky soil, with summer rain and a dry winter: these conditions are the best guide.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

In southern California, Jungle Music recommends full sun on the coast and inland, partial sun or filtered light in desert areas, and a soil that drains quickly, warning that excessively wet soil leads to rot; it describes a moderately vigorous blue cycad that usually makes one flush of leaves a year, needs room for its full crown, and loses much of its blue colour in shade. The nursery's statement that stems can exceed ten metres over centuries goes beyond the 6 to 7 m of the botanical sources. No dated result of planting out has been published outside South Africa.

Container cultivation

Two collectors in France described their young potted plants on the Fous de palmiers forum in 2013 and 2014. In the Tarn (south-west France), a plant with a caudex of about 10 cm produced new leaves in autumn 2013 and seemed to grow faster than Encephalartos lehmannii. A collector in the Ain and the Hérault reported that his plant, with a 7 to 8 cm caudex, had made two flushes in a year, the last in midwinter; his South African Encephalartos overwinter in a barely heated glasshouse, while it sometimes falls to −17 °C (1.4 °F) outside. His plant remained less blue than his Encephalartos lehmannii or Encephalartos horridus, for lack of full sun. In Italy, a collector in Ravenna noted on the Tropicamente forum in June 2013 that his potted Encephalartos middelburgensis had not yet produced new leaves that year.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

BGCI PlantSearch lists 14 institutions holding the species. In the United States they are the Huntington (San Marino, California), the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley, the Atlanta Botanical Garden, the two gardens of San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance, the National Tropical Botanical Garden and the University of Hawaii at Hilo. In Australia, the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney lists the species in its online catalogue, and the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria grows it in an outdoor bed at Melbourne Gardens, according to its online plant census (consulted in October 2026). No British garden declares it, and the Montgomery Botanical Center has no accession. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not a living plant on display today. In the trade, Jungle Music classes it as an expensive cycad, rarely available beyond seedling size, because seed is scarce.

Propagation

According to PlantZAfrica, the species is easily propagated from seed and suckers. Seeds cleaned while wearing gloves can be stored for six months or more at 10 to 15 °C (50 to 59 °F) so that the embryo completes its development, then germinated half buried in washed sand at about 28 °C (82 °F). Seedlings at the one-leaf stage are susceptible to damping-off; they are kept in shade for their first years and can be planted out at three to five years. Suckers are removed with a clean knife, the wound treated with sulphur or a fungicide and dried for a week before planting. In the wild, suckers play little part in regeneration, which relies mainly on seed (South African Scientific Authority, 2015). Seeds and suckers are covered by CITES Appendix I, and no plant should be taken from the wild.

Pests and diseases

An unidentified disease may have killed two of the nine wild plants in Gauteng after 2004; the same document reports trunks pierced with small holes, attributed to porcupines. In cultivation, PlantZAfrica lists scale insects, beetles that attack the emerging leaves and chewing insects, as well as damping-off of seedlings. As with all Encephalartos, rot of the roots and caudex in cold, waterlogged soil remains the main risk.

Cold hardiness

No measured cold value in cultivation is documented for Encephalartos middelburgensis. PlantZAfrica describes it as frost-hardy, and its authors thought that some plants experience winter frosts in the wild.

Mediterranean and European experience

No result of planting out in Europe has been published: the plants mentioned on the French and Italian forums are in pots and overwintered under cover. In its habitat, frosts are brief and the winter very dry: in a climate with wet winters, a plant in the ground runs more risk than a large container kept dry under cover. This is the most relevant lesson for growers in comparable climates, such as the Mediterranean, coastal California or southern Australia.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No measured value has been published for these countries. Jungle Music notes that winters in the habitat are cold, with periodic freezes, but gives no temperature for cultivation, and the gardens listed above do not publish any. The USDA zones that circulate for this species rest on no published measurement. As with other Encephalartos, survivals in cold areas often concern young plants with an underground caudex, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil; an adult plant with an aerial trunk, as this species becomes, does not benefit from this protection.

Toxicity and precautions

Like other cycads, Encephalartos middelburgensis contains toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a substance toxic to the liver and the nervous system; the seeds are the most dangerous part, and PlantZAfrica advises wearing gloves to clean their flesh, which contains slow-acting poisons. No study deals with the toxicity of the species itself, but the plant should not be regarded as harmless. The toxicity of Encephalartos was first demonstrated for Encephalartos horridus (Van der Walt, 1944), and the ASPCA reports vomiting, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses that have eaten cycads. Keep plants and seeds out of reach of children and pets, and if any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use specific to Encephalartos middelburgensis is documented. The species is sought mainly for gardens and collections, which drives poaching.

Frequently asked questions

Why is Encephalartos middelburgensis so endangered?

Because of poaching. Plants, suckers and even cones are stolen for the horticultural trade: according to the South African Scientific Authority, about 59% of the plants counted in 1983 had disappeared by 2015, and no more than 350 wild plants remained.

Where does it grow?

Only in the upper Olifants River catchment, between Middelburg and Loskop Dam in Mpumalanga, with a few plants in Gauteng, on rocky grassy slopes between about 1,000 and 1,400 m.

How can it be told from Encephalartos eugene-maraisii?

By its clearly stalked leaves whose lower leaflets are reduced to entire spines, and by its more numerous (four to eight) cylindrical female cones, whereas Encephalartos eugene-maraisii bears two ovoid, rust-coloured cones.

Are its cones green or reddish brown?

The sources disagree. The original description calls them bright green with patches of reddish-brown down; PlantZAfrica describes them as reddish brown and covered with brown hairs.

Is Encephalartos middelburgensis frost-hardy?

It is reputed to be, in South Africa, where frosts are brief and winters dry, but no measured value in cultivation has been published. In Europe the few plants known are in pots and overwintered under cover.

Can seeds or plants be bought?

Only from cultivated stock and with the required CITES and EU documents. The EU has a negative opinion in force on artificially propagated plants from South Africa, and the South African authority has judged the trade detrimental to the species.

Is Encephalartos middelburgensis poisonous?

It should be treated as such, like all cycads, especially its seeds.
