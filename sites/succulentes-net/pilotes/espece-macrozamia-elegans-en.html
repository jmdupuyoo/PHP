Macrozamia elegans is a cycad endemic to the Blue Mountains west of Sydney, in New South Wales, eastern Australia, where it grows only in a small area around Mountain Lagoon and Bilpin, in open eucalypt forest on sandstone. This species of the genus Macrozamia forms, from an underground trunk, a crown of a few large bluish-grey leaves, little or not twisted, with sharp-tipped leaflets. Described in 1998, it was previously confused with Macrozamia spiralis, from which Australian enthusiasts already set it apart under the nickname “robust spiralis”. It is a threatened species, listed as Endangered by the IUCN.

Key facts about Macrozamia elegans

  • Accepted name: Macrozamia elegans K.D.Hill & D.L.Jones (1998), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and the Australian Plant Census
  • Former names and synonyms: no formal synonym; plants formerly included in Macrozamia spiralis, nicknamed “robust spiralis” by enthusiasts
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: no English common name is attested
  • Natural range: Australia, New South Wales, Blue Mountains: a restricted area around Mountain Lagoon, east and north-east of Bilpin
  • IUCN status: Endangered (EN), criteria B1ab(v), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated September 2020 and published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II, like the whole genus; Annex B in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: no dated cultivation report; regular frosts in the Blue Mountains (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, dry sclerophyll forest on sandstone, on undulating to steep ground; in cultivation, not documented
  • Habit and adult size: underground trunk 15 to 30 cm across; 3 to 10 leaves 110 to 150 cm long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: listed at the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney; declared to BGCI by the Huntington (California); no British garden lists it
  • Toxicity: not studied for the species; seeds and young leaves are toxic throughout the genus (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Macrozamia elegans

It is recognised by its few large, bluish-grey leaves, with a straight or slightly twisted axis and a long petiole, and by its broad, sharp-tipped leaflets whose base forms an orange or red callus. According to the Flora of Australia (Hill, 1998), these features ally it to Macrozamia spiralis, but its leaves, leaflets, cones and seeds are all larger. The Flora's measurements are as follows:

  • Trunk: underground, 15 to 30 cm across;
  • Leaves: 3 to 10 in the crown, 110 to 150 cm long, moderately keeled, grey-green, semi-glossy; petiole 20 to 35 cm; axis straight to moderately spirally twisted;
  • Leaflets: 90 to 140 per leaf, undivided, 22 to 31 cm long and 9 to 14 mm wide, clearly paler beneath, with flat or slightly recurved margins and a sharp tip; the lowest leaflets are not reduced to spines;
  • Male cones: narrowly ovoid to spindle-shaped, 16 to 32 cm long and 6 to 6.5 cm wide, with terminal spines 0 to 10 mm long;
  • Female cones: ovoid, about 24 cm long and 11.5 cm wide, with spines 20 to 55 mm long;
  • Seeds: ovoid, 30 to 32 mm long and 24 to 26 mm wide, with red flesh.

Craig Thompson and Paul Kennedy, two growers who followed the species in the field and wrote the account published by the Palm and Cycad Societies of Australia (PACSOA), add that the leaves, grey-green at first, turn dark green as they age; that the leaflets form a broad V on either side of the axis; that the callus at their base is pinkish red; that plants most often carry 4 to 6 leaves; and that young cones, male and female alike, are a powder blue that sets them apart from most other Macrozamia.

Hybrids

No natural or garden hybrid has been reported.

Possible confusion

The main confusion is with Macrozamia spiralis, from the Sydney region and the lower Blue Mountains, from which it was separated. According to the Flora of Australia, Macrozamia spiralis is smaller in every respect: trunk 8 to 20 cm across, leaves 35 to 100 cm long, 45 to 120 leaflets 12 to 20 cm long and 5 to 9 mm wide, and above all leaflets with a blunt tip, whereas those of Macrozamia elegans are sharp. The PACSOA sums up the relationship by describing Macrozamia elegans as, in essence, a size-increased Macrozamia spiralis, just as Macrozamia reducta is, in effect, a size-reduced Macrozamia communis.

Macrozamia communis, common on the coast of New South Wales, is easily told apart: according to the Flora of Australia, it carries 50 to 150 flat, dark green leaves whose lowest leaflets are reduced to spines.

Taxonomy

Macrozamia elegans K.D.Hill & D.L.Jones was published in 1998 in volume 48 of the Flora of Australia (page 718), which covers the cycads of the country. It is accepted by The World List of Cycads, has no synonym and belongs to the family Zamiaceae.

Before 1998, these plants were included in Macrozamia spiralis. Ken Hill and David Jones separated them on the basis of consistent differences in size and of the sharp tip of the leaflets. For the reader, this means that old plants, seeds or photographs labelled “Macrozamia spiralis” from the Blue Mountains may belong to Macrozamia elegans.

The PACSOA places it in section Parazamia, which groups the species with an underground trunk, few leaves and lowest leaflets not reduced to spines. The World List of Cycads does not use sections.

Etymology and history

The epithet comes from the Latin elegans, “elegant”, in reference to the neat and attractive habit of the plant (Haynes, 2022, as given by The World List of Cycads, and the Flora of Australia). The genus name joins the Greek makros, “large”, to the genus name Zamia.

The type specimen was collected by Ken Hill and L. Stanberg on 27 February 1995 north of Kurrajong; the holotype is kept at the National Herbarium of New South Wales in Sydney (NSW), with duplicates in Canberra, Brisbane, New York and Kew. The plant was known long before: the Flora of Australia cites a collection by L.A.S. Johnson and E.F. Constable at Grassy Hill, between the Colo River and the Putty road, dated 29 October 1954, and one by P. Kennedy and C. Thompson at Kurmond in 1991. Before its description, Sydney cycad enthusiasts called it “robust spiralis” (PACSOA).

Common names

No common name is attested for this species. The nickname “robust spiralis”, used by Australian enthusiasts before 1998, is not a vernacular name.

In the wild

Macrozamia elegans has a very small range. According to the Flora of Australia, it is restricted to a limited area around Mountain Lagoon, in the Blue Mountains, where it is locally common over small areas, in dry sclerophyll woodland on undulating to steeply sloping ground on sandstone. The PACSOA describes a range forming an arc running east and north-east from Bilpin, at 606 m. The plants grow on steep slopes under eucalypts, with a low shrubby understorey, usually singly, sometimes in small groups. The authors suggest that these groups come from seed dispersed by kangaroos or possums, without having shown it.

There is no weather station near the habitat. The PACSOA gives figures for Katoomba, at 1,015 m, some 400 m higher than Bilpin: 1,400 mm of rain a year over 117 days, about 60% of it in summer and autumn; above 30 °C (86 °F) on nine days a year on average; below 2 °C (35.6 °F) on 54 days; 42 frost days and 4 snow days a year on average; and recent extremes of 36.6 °C (97.9 °F) and −8.2 °C (17.2 °F). A member of the French Fous de palmiers forum pointed out in 2014 that these figures, taken 400 m higher up, probably overstate the cold the species endures, the difference possibly reaching 3 °C (5.4 °F).

Cones are usually solitary on each plant, sometimes two (PACSOA). No pollination study specific to the species has been published; in Macrozamia, pollination is carried out by specialist insects (see the genus page).

Conservation status and regulation

Macrozamia elegans is threatened: the IUCN Red List classes it as Endangered (EN), under criteria B1ab(v), in the assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated September 2020 and published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, the Flora of Australia, GBIF and Crossref, checked on 26 October 2026). Criterion B1 refers to a very small extent of occurrence, and (v) to a continuing decline in the number of mature individuals. The Flora of Australia considers it potentially threatened by habitat destruction and poaching. An older profile in the online encyclopedia of the Palm and Cycad Societies of Florida (around 2000) already gave it as vulnerable.

The Atlas of Living Australia, which compiles the national and state threatened-species lists, shows no listing for it under the federal Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act or New South Wales legislation (checked on 26 October 2026).

Like all species of the genus, it is listed in CITES Appendix II and, in the European Union, in Annex B of the wildlife trade regulations (Species+, checked on 26 October 2026). International trade in plants and seeds requires permits. For a threatened and poached species, buy only seed-raised plants from cultivation, with their documents, and check the regulations in force before any purchase or import.

Growing Macrozamia elegans

No reliable source describes the cultivation of Macrozamia elegans. On the Fous de palmiers forum, a grower wrote in 2014 that he had never seen the species for sale. The following pointers come from its natural habitat and remain assumptions.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Soil: in the wild, poor, free-draining soils derived from sandstone; a very free-draining sandy or gritty soil appears essential.
  • Light: the plant grows under eucalypts; a bright position with a little shade in the hottest hours seems closest to its habitat.
  • Water: the habitat is fairly wet (Katoomba, higher up, receives 1,400 mm a year, mostly in summer and autumn), so the plant is not adapted to a long summer drought. Avoid, on the other hand, cold combined with waterlogged soil.
  • Space: with leaves 1.1 to 1.5 m long, the plant needs more room than its small crown suggests.
  • Growth: not documented for the species.

No observation of the Jardin zoologique tropical in southern France has been published for this species.

Container cultivation

No container-growing report has been published. With its underground trunk and few leaves, a young plant can be kept in a deep pot with a very free-draining mix; the long leaves of an adult call for a large container.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney lists the species in its Garden Explorer (updated on 1 October 2026) and declares it to BGCI.
  • United States: the Huntington (San Marino, California) declares it to BGCI.
  • United Kingdom: no garden declares it to BGCI, and no other source mentions it there.

Four institutions in all declare it to BGCI, the other two in the Czech Republic and France. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not necessarily a living plant on display today. The species does not appear in the online catalogue of the Montgomery Botanical Center or in the census of Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria. No English-language grower's report has been found.

Propagation

Propagation is by seed. The plants being male or female, both sexes and, in cultivation, hand pollination are needed to obtain fertile seed. No germination data specific to the species has been published, and no source mentions offsets.

Pests and diseases

No problem specific to the species is documented in reliable sources. In Macrozamia grown in Europe, growers' forums mainly mention scale insects and rot in cold, wet soil (see the genus page).

Cold hardiness

No dated cultivation report exists for Macrozamia elegans. The only indications come from the climate of its habitat and from an expert ranking reported on a forum.

Mediterranean and European experience

No planting in the ground is documented in Europe, and no recorded cold spell concerns the species. In 2014, on the Fous de palmiers forum, a grower from the Ain and Hérault departments of France reported the opinion of an Australian nurseryman specialising in cycads, who ranked Macrozamia elegans among the “relatively hardy” species of the coast and ranges of New South Wales, with Macrozamia communis, after a first group of inland species judged hardier. This ranking gives no temperature. The same grower thought the species promising in view of the −8.2 °C (17.2 °F) recorded at Katoomba; another member objected that this station is 400 m higher than the habitat.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No cultivation result has been published from Britain, North America or Australia, and no source gives a USDA zone for the species. Its habitat in the Blue Mountains has regular frosts and occasional snow higher up, but the plant's own limits are unknown.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data specific to Macrozamia elegans has been published, but the risk is documented throughout the genus. Macrozamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a toxin of the liver; the young leaves cause irreversible paralysis of the hindquarters in cattle (McKenzie, 2006; see the genus page). Wear gloves to handle seeds, keep them out of reach of children, dogs and livestock, and contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay in case of ingestion.

Traditional uses

No use specific to this species is documented. Other Macrozamia of New South Wales provided Aboriginal peoples with edible seeds after lengthy treatment (see the genus page).

Frequently asked questions

How does it differ from Macrozamia spiralis?

In size and in its leaflets. Macrozamia elegans has leaves 110 to 150 cm long, leaflets 9 to 14 mm wide with a sharp tip, and larger cones and seeds; Macrozamia spiralis has leaves 35 to 100 cm long and blunt leaflets.

Why was it called “robust spiralis”?

Because before 1998 Sydney enthusiasts saw it as a robust form of Macrozamia spiralis. Ken Hill and David Jones made it a separate species in 1998.

Is it endangered?

Yes. The IUCN classes it as Endangered (EN) in its 2022 assessment, because of its very small range and a decline in the number of adult plants. Habitat destruction and poaching are the threats cited.

Is it cold-hardy?

Nobody knows precisely. Its Blue Mountains habitat has regular frosts, and the Katoomba station, higher up, has recorded −8.2 °C (17.2 °F), but no grower has published a result.

Are its cones really blue?

Yes, at first. According to the PACSOA, the young male and female cones are powder blue, which sets the species apart from most other Macrozamia.

Where can it be bought?

It is very rare in the trade. Since it is threatened and poached, buy only a seed-raised plant from cultivation, with its CITES documents.

Is it poisonous?

Very probably. No analysis concerns this species, but the seeds and young leaves of Macrozamia are toxic to people and animals.
