Encephalartos macrostrobilus is a cycad of north-western Uganda, known from a single area of Moyo District, on the granite plateau above the Albert Nile close to the border with South Sudan. This species of the genus Encephalartos was described in 1997 by Scott Jones and Jeff Wynants, and its name refers to the size of its female cones, which can reach 80 cm in length. It is a trunk-forming plant, up to 2.5 m tall, with a dense crown of dark green leaves. Listed as Critically Endangered by the IUCN, it is one of the least known species of the genus: the last published count, in 2022, found 181 adult plants, most of them on community land with no legal protection. It is almost absent from collections, and very little has been published on its behaviour in cultivation.

Key facts about Encephalartos macrostrobilus

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos macrostrobilus S.Jones & Wynants (1997), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15); IPNI gives the authors as “Scott Jones & J.Wynants”
  • Former names and synonyms: none
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: no English name attested; in the Madi language, “Ci-eia”, meaning “like the date palm” (original description, 1997)
  • Natural range: northern Uganda, Moyo District (Era area, Madi country), between about 630 and 1,400 m depending on the source
  • IUCN status: Critically Endangered (CR B1ab(ii,iii,v); C2a(ii)) in the 2010 assessment (Donaldson) still cited by The World List of Cycads; category kept by the assessment dated 5 March 2020 and published in the IUCN Red List 2024-1, which is now in force
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I since 4 February 1977, like the whole genus; Annex A in the European Union; negative opinion of the EU Scientific Review Group on imports of artificially propagated plants from South Africa (7 February 2013)
  • Documented hardiness: none; a frost-free tropical climate with a dry season in the wild (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, degraded wooded savanna on shallow sandy soils (lithosols) near seasonal streams, often among large boulders and in about 50% shade; in cultivation, filtered light, shelter and a porous soil according to a Californian nursery (Jungle Music)
  • Habit and adult size: trunk usually single, upright then somewhat reclining with age, up to 2.5 m tall and 30 to 45 cm thick; 35 to 60 leaves 1.4 to 2.2 m long; female cones up to 80 cm (Jones and Wynants, 1997)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Montgomery Botanical Center (accession of 1998), San Diego Botanic Garden and San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance gardens in the United States (BGCI); no British or Australian garden declares it
  • Toxicity: not studied for the species; documented risk for the genus (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos macrostrobilus

The feature that gave the species its name is the size of its female cones, much larger than those of Encephalartos septentrionalis, its neighbour in South Sudan and northern Uganda (Haynes, 2022, cited by The World List of Cycads). Add a real trunk, a dense crown of stiff, dark green, toothed leaves and very slender male cones, and the plant is distinctive within its region. The original description by Jones and Wynants (1997), published in Encephalartos, the journal of the Cycad Society of South Africa, gives the following characters:

  • Trunk: arborescent, usually single and unbranched, sometimes with basal suckers, becoming somewhat reclining with age; up to 2.5 m tall and 30 to 45 cm thick; covered with old leaf bases in exposed plants, while plants in shade keep only a few of them
  • Leaves: 35 to 60, spreading to erect, stiff and straight but slightly recurved at the tip, very dark green; 1.9 to 2.2 m long and 39 to 59 cm wide in sheltered plants, 1.4 to 2 m in exposed ones; petiole 12 to 15 cm, strongly swollen at the base; the lowest leaflets abruptly reduced to one to three pairs of forked prickles; rachis grooved along its whole length
  • Leaflets: opposite, held almost at right angles to the rachis, arched, with slightly rolled margins, stiff and hairless, with 21 veins that do not stand out; the upper margin bears 6 to 8 teeth of 2 to 6 mm, the lower margin 2 to 4 teeth of 2 to 5 mm; they are no more than 25 mm wide (the 1997 text gives “190 to 250 mm” by mistake, an error pointed out by Newton in 2002), and their length is not given
  • Cones: male and female cones appear at the same time; hairless, dark green, turning olive green when ripe
  • Male cones: 6 to 14 per plant, upright, narrowly ovoid and remarkably slender, 18 to 20 cm long and 4.5 to 5 cm across, on a stalk of 8 to 9 cm
  • Female cones: three per plant, upright when they emerge, then leaning or even hanging when ripe; up to 80 cm long and 30 cm across, on a stalk of 12 to 14 cm
  • Seeds: about 300 per cone, 35 to 40 mm by 25 mm with their fleshy coat (sarcotesta), 32 to 36 mm by 19 to 22 mm without it; sarcotesta yellow to dark ochre, turning orange when ripe

The field reports of S. Ojelel, of Makerere University, also show trunks blackened by bush fires, which the plants survive. The Californian nursery Jungle Music describes long, pendulous leaves and leaflets coated in white wool, which does not match the original description (stiff, straight leaves and hairless leaflets); the difference is not explained.

Hybrids

No hybrid of Encephalartos macrostrobilus has been described, in the wild or in cultivation.

Possible confusion

The species most likely to be confused with it is Encephalartos septentrionalis, which occurs in northern Uganda, South Sudan, the Central African Republic and the Democratic Republic of the Congo according to The World List of Cycads; Jones and Wynants noted a population of it less than 50 km away. According to them, Encephalartos macrostrobilus differs in:

  • its well-developed, upright trunk (very short, reclining or even underground in Encephalartos septentrionalis);
  • its dense crown (one to two dozen often damaged leaves in the other species);
  • its much larger female cones (about 80 cm against 27 to 36 cm), olive green when ripe, with yellow to ochre seeds (light brown cones and bright red seeds in Encephalartos septentrionalis);
  • its remarkably slender male cones;
  • even its seedlings: the first leaf carries six pairs of almost toothless leaflets, against four or five pairs of clearly toothed leaflets in Encephalartos septentrionalis.

Newton (2002) also separates it from Encephalartos mackenziei of South Sudan, which forms up to ten trunks and has broader leaflets (up to 35 mm) and smaller female cones (40 cm at most).

The identity of some plants is still unclear. From 2016 to 2021, S. Ojelel ran two projects funded by the Rufford Foundation on a population attributed to Encephalartos macrostrobilus in the Agoro-Agu Central Forest Reserve, in Lamwo District, above 1,500 m and far to the east of Moyo District; he counted 665 individuals along three transects (Ojelel and Anywar, 2017). Yet the Kew fact sheet written in 2024 with the same researcher presents the Era area, in Moyo District, as the only known site of the species. The published sources do not explain the difference, so the Agoro-Agu data are kept separate on this page.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos macrostrobilus is accepted by The World List of Cycads and by IPNI, and has no synonym. Scott Jones and Jeff Wynants described it in 1997 in Encephalartos (50: 13–17, figures 1 and 2), under the title “Encephalartos macrostrobilus (Zamiaceae), a new cycad species from Northern Uganda”. The holotype, collected by the two authors under number 01, comes from the Madi area of north-western Uganda (3°35′ N, 31°40′ E), between 900 and 1,400 m, and is kept at Meise Botanic Garden in Belgium (BR).

Hurter (1995) had already reported recent fieldwork on the Encephalartos of Uganda, which led to the description of Encephalartos whitelockii. No published source states how Encephalartos macrostrobilus is related to the other East African species, and no genetic study compares it with Encephalartos septentrionalis: the species rests on the morphological differences set out by its authors.

Etymology and history

The epithet comes from the Latin macro- (“large”) and strobilus (“pine cone”), in reference to the large female cones compared with those of Encephalartos septentrionalis (Haynes, 2022, cited by The World List of Cycads).

The history of the species is bound up with insecurity in the region. In 1997 Jones and Wynants knew fewer than a hundred plants, in an area accessible only on foot, and thought that other populations might exist; they noted that insecurity, with Sudanese refugee camps and the rebels of the Lord's Resistance Army, kept collectors away. According to Ojelel and Anywar (2017), the 2010 IUCN assessment put the population at 200 to 300 plants and mentioned areas made inaccessible by the rebellion. In May 2018 a survey funded by the Mohamed bin Zayed Species Conservation Fund and led by S. Ojelel counted 181 mature individuals, all georeferenced, and a specimen was deposited at the Makerere University Herbarium, the first for the species according to the project's case study. The results were published in 2022 (Ojelel, Mucunguzi and Kalema): 181 adults in 43 colonies, which the Kew fact sheet reads as a decline from the earlier estimates. Work in preparation (Ochom and colleagues) is said to indicate a larger number.

Common names

No English name is attested in a reliable source. In the Madi language the plant is called “Ci-eia”, meaning “like the date palm” (Phoenix), according to the original description (Jones and Wynants, 1997). Names such as “Madi cycad” or “giant-coned cycad”, which can be found online, are informal descriptions with no reliable source, and Species+ gives no English name for the species.

In the wild

Encephalartos macrostrobilus grows in the Era-Lama Tropical Important Plant Area described by Kew: Era Central Forest Reserve and the villages to the north and north-east, in the sub-counties of Moyo and Palorinya (West Madi County, Moyo District). The site covers 100 km² between 630 and 1,040 m, on the plateau above the Albert Nile, in a mainly Madi area; the type itself was collected between 900 and 1,400 m according to The World List of Cycads.

The vegetation is open woodland on granite outcrops, with Acacia hockii, Combretum molle and Grewia mollis among others, as well as wooded savanna and grassland. According to the original description, the species grows on sandy lithosols and rocky slopes along seasonal streams, in degraded savanna with a canopy of 5 to 20 m. It is found mostly where the vegetation is a little denser, within 100 m of the gullies, usually among the large boulders that mark the stream beds, in about 50% shade. The two main populations studied in 1997 were on slopes of about 10°, one facing east and the other west; groups held from four to at least thirteen adults, and plants in shade among the boulders were larger and in better condition than those exposed by tree felling. Rainfall is about 1,250 mm a year, with a marked dry season from December to February (Kew). The same area is home to other threatened trees, such as Afzelia africana and Vitellaria paradoxa, the shea tree; the 2018 survey also recorded Dalbergia melanoxylon there.

More than 90% of the population grows on community land and less than 5% in the forest reserve, according to Ojelel and colleagues (2022), cited by Kew. The 2018 survey found a high proportion of male plants: a female to male ratio of two to five, or 28.6% females among the plants that could be sexed. This is the opposite of what Jones and Wynants observed in 1997, when adult plants seemed to be mostly female.

The original description also records observations on reproduction. Ripe seeds were carried off by baboons and hornbills during the day and by other animals at night, probably rodents; they germinate as soon as they fall. No weevil or other beetle was found in cones of either sex, but larvae, apparently of moths, were feeding on the seed coats in one cone. In 1997 all age classes were present at every site visited, with good regeneration.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos macrostrobilus is Critically Endangered (CR) on the IUCN Red List under criteria B1ab(ii,iii,v); C2a(ii), according to the 2010 assessment by Donaldson cited by The World List of Cycads: a very small range, a continuing decline in area and quality of habitat and in mature individuals, and a very small population. The assessment now in force, by the Eastern Arc Mountains and Coastal Forests CEPF Plant Assessment Project and J. D. Bösenberg, is dated 5 March 2020 and was published in the Red List 2024-1 (Crossref); it keeps the species in this category, which GBIF, relaying the Red List, also gives. It is the one cited by the Kew fact sheet (Bösenberg, 2024); its criteria are not given by these sources.

According to Kew, the threats are frequent fires, stone quarrying on the many granite outcrops, cattle grazing, tree cutting for charcoal, clearing for cultivation and the use of seeds for spiritual purposes by some communities from South Sudan; leaves are also cut for no known reason. Habitat degradation is thought to prevent the recruitment of young plants. The 2018 case study adds that the land is crossed by trails and used for firewood and timber, and that baboons persistently destroy the cones. In 1997, on the contrary, Jones and Wynants observed good regeneration, but already reported grazing by cattle and goats, cutting of timber and firewood, loss of tree cover and regular fires lit to renew the grass: exposed plants survive them, as their blackened trunks show, but regeneration could suffer. For the Agoro-Agu population, Ojelel and Anywar (2017) add illegal collecting by outsiders and the eating of female cones and seeds by baboons, beetles and ants.

The Mohamed bin Zayed Fund project (2018) brought the site to the attention of the Moyo District local government and raised awareness of the species in the neighbouring communities; with the few seeds that could be collected, 21 seedlings were raised, and a team of local residents was set up to monitor the female colonies. In Lamwo District, the Rufford projects raised about 4,000 seeds in a community nursery in 2017, giving 746 seedlings, of which at least 235 were planted out between 2018 and 2021.

Regulation, according to Species+ (consulted through its public API on 26 October 2026):

  • CITES: Appendix I since 4 February 1977, like the whole genus; international trade in wild-collected plants for commercial purposes is prohibited;
  • European Union: Annex A, current list in Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, in force since 29 June 2026; the EU Scientific Review Group has given a negative opinion, still current, on imports of artificially propagated plants from South Africa (source codes A and D) since 7 February 2013;
  • Uganda: no national protection specific to the species is documented.

These notes do not replace the official texts: check Species+ and EUR-Lex before any purchase or import, and ask for documents proving that a plant was propagated in cultivation.

Growing Encephalartos macrostrobilus

Very little has been published on growing Encephalartos macrostrobilus: no botanic garden or grower has described its requirements, and the only practical advice comes from a specialist nursery in southern California, Jungle Music, whose undated page presents the species as exceptionally rare in cultivation, slow-growing and not easy. What follows combines that commercial source, clearly attributed, with what the wild habitat suggests.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

In the wild the species lives in a frost-free tropical climate with about 1,250 mm of rain and a short dry season, on shallow, sandy, well-drained soils among rocks, often in partial shade. Planting out can therefore only be considered in frost-free tropical or subtropical climates, in a very free-draining soil. In southern California, Jungle Music recommends filtered light and a protected spot in coastal and inland gardens, a porous soil that drains well, and advises against desert areas; according to the nursery, the thin, pliable leaflets make the plant sensitive to cool climates, where it tends to be semi-dormant and may lose all or part of its leaves in winter, and it usually produces one flush of leaves a year. No measured result of planting out has been published.

Container cultivation

In temperate countries the only realistic option is a large pot in a heated glasshouse or conservatory. The general rules for Encephalartos apply (a mineral, free-draining mix, watering in the growing season, a drier rest), but none has been tested and reported for this species.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

The species is very rare in botanic gardens. BGCI PlantSearch lists four institutions, all in the United States: the Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida), whose online catalogue shows one accession, of 1998; the San Diego Botanic Garden (Encinitas, California); and the two gardens of San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance (San Diego Zoo and the Safari Park at Escondido). A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not a living plant on display today. No British or Australian garden declares the species.

Propagation

Propagation is by seed. In the wild, seeds germinate as soon as they fall (Jones and Wynants, 1997), and the community nurseries show that they come up readily: 746 seedlings from about 4,000 seeds sown at Agoro-Agu in 2017 (Rufford reports), and 21 seedlings from the little seed collected in Moyo District in 2018 (Mohamed bin Zayed Fund). The plant sometimes produces basal suckers, but nothing has been published on rooting them. The general methods for sowing cycads apply; seed of this species is not normally available.

Pests and diseases

Nothing is documented for Encephalartos macrostrobilus in cultivation. In the wild, larvae of moths were found feeding on the seed coats (1997), baboons destroy many cones (2018), and in the Agoro-Agu population female cones and seeds are eaten by baboons, beetles and ants, while dead stems are eaten by antelopes (Ojelel and Anywar, 2017).

Cold hardiness

The cold hardiness of Encephalartos macrostrobilus is not documented: no observation of its resistance to cold has been published, and the species lives in a tropical climate with a dry season and no recorded frost.

Mediterranean and European experience

No result of cultivation in the Mediterranean basin or elsewhere in Europe has been published, and the species is practically absent from European collections. No region of Europe can be recommended for planting it out.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No measured value has been published for the United Kingdom, the United States or Australia. In southern California, Jungle Music only notes that the species is sensitive to cool climates and may lose its leaves in winter, without giving a temperature. The plants declared by the gardens of Florida and southern California give no published information on hardiness, and no USDA zone is documented for the species. The site lies between 630 and 1,040 m (up to 1,400 m for the type locality), in a climate where frost is not known: any value would be a guess.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data exist for Encephalartos macrostrobilus itself, but the risk is documented for the genus. The toxicity of Encephalartos was demonstrated for Encephalartos horridus as early as 1944 (Van der Walt), and Encephalartos contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; these substances affect the liver, the digestive tract and the nervous system, and the seeds are the most dangerous part. For another cycad, the sago palm (Cycas revoluta), the ASPCA reports vomiting, black stools, jaundice, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses.

Wear gloves to handle seeds and their fleshy coat, keep plants and seeds out of reach of children and pets, and if any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

According to the original description, the Madi collect a few seeds to “chase away bad omens”, and they do not fell cycads when clearing land, perhaps for religious reasons, perhaps because they make poor fuel; the Kew fact sheet also mentions the spiritual use of seeds by some communities from South Sudan. At Agoro-Agu, 82% of the residents questioned knew of no use for the plant, and 18% mentioned an ornamental use (Ojelel and Anywar, 2017).

Frequently asked questions

Where does Encephalartos macrostrobilus grow?

Only in north-western Uganda, in Moyo District, around Era Central Forest Reserve. A population attributed to the species at Agoro-Agu, in Lamwo District, is not mentioned in the 2024 Kew fact sheet, and the difference is unexplained.

What does macrostrobilus mean?

“With large cones”. The name refers to the female cones, up to 80 cm long, much larger than those of Encephalartos septentrionalis.

How many plants are left in the wild?

About 181 adults, according to the 2018 survey published in 2022. Work in preparation is said to suggest a somewhat larger number, but it has not been published.

Is Encephalartos macrostrobilus endangered?

Yes. It is Critically Endangered on the IUCN Red List; fires, quarrying, grazing and clearing threaten a habitat that lies almost entirely outside the forest reserve.

How can it be told from Encephalartos septentrionalis?

By its trunk and cones. It has a real upright trunk up to 2.5 m, a dense crown, female cones about 80 cm long that ripen olive green with yellow to ochre seeds, and very slender male cones; Encephalartos septentrionalis has a short or underground trunk, smaller light brown female cones and red seeds.

Can Encephalartos macrostrobilus be grown outdoors?

Only in a frost-free climate. A nursery in southern California recommends a protected, lightly shaded spot, but no measured result has been published. In temperate countries it needs a heated glasshouse, and the plant is almost impossible to obtain.

Is Encephalartos macrostrobilus poisonous?

Very probably. The species has not been studied, but Encephalartos are toxic, especially their seeds.
