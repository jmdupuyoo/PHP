Macrozamia mountperriensis is a cycad from south-eastern Queensland, in eastern Australia, confined to the Mount Perry area west of Bundaberg, where it forms extensive colonies in open eucalypt forest and dry araucarian forest. A species of the genus Macrozamia, it has a mostly underground stem, a dense crown of straight, pale to yellowish-green leaves, and leaflets that never shrink into spines at the base of the leaf. Described in 1886, it was reduced in 1932 to Macrozamia tridentata subsp. mountperriensis, then long regarded as a mere form of Macrozamia miquelii. It is not threatened.

Key facts about Macrozamia mountperriensis

  • Accepted name: Macrozamia mountperriensis F.M.Bailey (1886), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and the Australian Plant Census
  • Former names and synonyms: Macrozamia tridentata subsp. mountperriensis (F.M.Bailey) J.Schust. (1932)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales; section Macrozamia, Macrozamia miquelii group
  • Common names: “Tchalli” (Flora of Australia); “Mt. Perry Cycad” (Mississippi State University)
  • Natural range: endemic to south-eastern Queensland, from the Mount Perry area to Brooweena and Aramara, at 200 to 450 m
  • IUCN status: Least Concern (LC), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated August 2020 and published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union; no listing under Australian threatened-species law (Atlas of Living Australia)
  • Documented hardiness: no measured value and no dated cultivation report with a temperature; “frost and drought tolerant” according to the PACSOA, without figures (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, sheltered slopes under open forest on gravelly granite loams, and dry araucarian forest on volcanic soils; in cultivation, a sunny, well-drained position (PACSOA)
  • Habit and adult size: stem usually underground, up to 30 cm long and 40 cm across; 10 to 80 leaves 0.6 to 1.5 m long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: grown at the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney (accession of 1992) and Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria; declared to BGCI by 16 institutions, including the Huntington, San Diego Botanic Garden and the San Diego Zoo
  • Toxicity: not documented for the species; seeds and leaves toxic throughout the genus (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Macrozamia mountperriensis

It is recognised by its flat, straight, pale to yellowish-green leaves on a long petiole, and by the lack of spines at the base of the leaves: the lowest leaflets are only slightly shorter. The most detailed description is that of David Jones, Paul Forster and Ish Sharma (2001), which the measurements below follow.

  • Stem: usually underground, sometimes emergent, cylindrical, up to 30 cm long and 40 cm across, unbranched.
  • Leaves: 10 to 80 in a dense crown, 0.6 to 1.5 m long, erect to spreading and arching, flat in section; the swollen base is covered with pale grey woolly hairs; petiole 30 to 65 cm long (base included); rachis straight, not or barely twisted; young leaves pale to yellowish-green.
  • Leaflets: 50 to 110 per leaf, inserted at about 40°, linear, 20 to 35 cm long and 6 to 9 mm wide, flat, thin, pale green to yellowish, with a whitish callus at the base and a sharp tip; the lowest leaflets are slightly smaller but never reduced to spines.
  • Male cones: 1 to 4, cylindrical, often curved, green, 12 to 25 cm long and 2.5 to 4 cm across, on a stalk 12 to 22 cm long.
  • Female cones: 1 or 2, cylindrical to barrel-shaped, green, 17 to 30 cm long and 6 to 10 cm across, on a stalk 23 to 40 cm long; the scales end in an erect spine, 2.5 to 4 cm long towards the top of the cone.
  • Seeds: ovoid to oblong, 2 to 2.5 cm long and 1.5 to 2 cm wide, with orange to red or yellow flesh.

The sources do not agree on every figure. The Flora of Australia (Hill, 1998) describes more numerous (35 to 80) and longer (90 to 150 cm) leaves, mid- to dark green and highly glossy, male cones 17 to 23 cm long and seeds 25 mm by 16 mm with orange or yellow flesh; Jones and his co-authors point out “numerous discrepancies” between their measurements and those of that flora. In the original description of 1886, F.M. Bailey wrote of about 80 deep green leaves, about 5 feet (1.5 m) long, the male plants having the finest crowns, and of seeds with a deep orange coat.

Hybrids

No natural or garden hybrid of Macrozamia mountperriensis has been reported.

Possible confusion

Macrozamia mountperriensis belongs to the group of Macrozamia miquelii, seven medium-sized Queensland species whose leaves are not or barely twisted (Jones, Forster and Sharma, 2001). Two confusions are common.

  • With Macrozamia miquelii: Macrozamia mountperriensis has shorter, paler leaves with a proportionally longer petiole, no spiny leaflets at the base of the leaf (Macrozamia miquelii has 6 to 16 pairs) and much smaller cones and seeds.
  • With Macrozamia longispina, its closest relative: that species has fewer leaflets (usually 52 to 58 against 70 to 86), narrower ones (4.5 to 6 mm against 6.5 to 9 mm), and male cone scales with much longer terminal spines (15 to 20 mm against 3 to 13 mm). An allozyme analysis published in the same revision confirms how close the two species are.

In cultivation, the likeliest confusion is with Macrozamia communis, whose lowest leaflets are reduced to spines. On the French Fous de palmiers forum, a specialist grower judged in 2017 that a plant sold in France as Macrozamia communis was probably a Macrozamia mountperriensis, because of its spine-free petiole, and recalled that many seeds imported from Australia as Macrozamia communis came from other populations, or even other species. Without a known origin, a young plant remains hard to identify.

Taxonomy

Macrozamia mountperriensis is accepted by The World List of Cycads and the Australian Plant Census. Frederick Manson Bailey, Colonial Botanist of Queensland, described it in 1886 in the first supplement to his Synopsis of the Queensland Flora (pp. 50–51), giving simply “Mount Perry” as its locality. As no original type material has been found, a neotype was designated by Forster and Jones (1992): a collection by D.L. and B.E. Jones (no. 6340) made on 29 August 1990 near Schuh's lookout, west of Mount Perry, held at the Australian National Herbarium in Canberra (CANB), with duplicates in Brisbane (BRI) and Sydney (NSW).

In 1932 Julius Schuster made it a subspecies of a very broad Macrozamia tridentata, a concept since abandoned. The species was then long treated as a form of Macrozamia miquelii, as the Palm and Cycad Societies of Australia (PACSOA) recall. The revision of the Macrozamia miquelii group by Jones, Forster and Sharma (2001), published in Austrobaileya, confirmed its rank as a species and gave a full description. The group belongs to section Macrozamia; it differs from the Macrozamia communis group in the narrow bases (2 to 5 mm) of the spines on the female cone scales.

Etymology and history

The epithet refers to the place of discovery, the small town of Mount Perry in Queensland, with the Latin suffix -ensis, “originating from” (Haynes, 2022, as given by The World List of Cycads; Jones et al., 2001). Bailey named no collector in his description.

Common names

The Flora of Australia gives the name “Tchalli”, without stating its origin; no other published source confirms it. A Mississippi State University extension publication calls the species “Mt. Perry Cycad”, a descriptive name after its locality. No other English name is attested.

In the wild

Macrozamia mountperriensis grows from the Mount Perry area, west of Bundaberg, south to Brooweena and east to Aramara, in the Wide Bay and Burnett districts, at 200 to 450 m (Jones et al., 2001). It grows on sheltered slopes, ridges and gullies, under open forest or dry woodland with tall, scattered trees, on gravelly loams derived from granite or granodiorite, more rarely sandstone, and also in dry araucarian forest on red-brown volcanic loams. Associated trees include Angophora leiocarpa, Corymbia citriodora, Corymbia trachyphloia, Eucalyptus acmenoides, Eucalyptus crebra and Eucalyptus siderophloia. The Flora of Australia describes its habitat as ranging from grassy eucalypt forest or woodland to Araucaria rainforest.

Pollen is shed in October and November. The cones are visited by the thrips Cycadothrips chadwicki, a specialist pollinator of Macrozamia (Forster et al., 1994, as cited by Jones et al., 2001), and ripe seeds fall in March and April. The colonies are extensive, with good recruitment from seed.

Conservation status and regulation

Macrozamia mountperriensis is not threatened. The IUCN Red List classes it as Least Concern (LC), in the assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated 21 August 2020 and published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, GBIF and Crossref, checked on 26 October 2026). In 2001, Jones and his co-authors found it locally abundant, widespread and well represented in several national parks and state forests, and neither rare nor threatened. The Atlas of Living Australia shows no national or state listing for it.

Like other Macrozamia, it has been listed in CITES Appendix II since 4 February 1977, with annotation #4: seeds and pollen are exempt, but plants are not. In the European Union it falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026 (Species+, checked on 26 October 2026). Check the regulations in force before any purchase or import. According to a grower on the Fous de palmiers forum, thousands of Macrozamia legally removed during road or firebreak works in Australia have been exported, including Macrozamia mountperriensis; this account has not been confirmed, but it is a reason to ask about the origin of large plants.

Growing Macrozamia mountperriensis

Macrozamia mountperriensis is rare in cultivation and poorly documented. The PACSOA recommends a sunny, well-drained position and describes it as frost and drought tolerant, without figures; Mississippi State University, by contrast, lists it for part sun to shade. Its habitat, well-drained slopes under open forest, confirms the need for a free-draining soil and good light.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Light: sun to light shade; the two published recommendations differ (see above).
  • Soil: very free-draining, as on the granite slopes of its habitat.
  • Water: the species is grown at the Jardin zoologique tropical in La Londe-les-Maures, in the Var (southern France). The garden's experience with Macrozamia shows that watering once a week in summer, when there is no rain, is a minimum and twice a week ideal. This Mediterranean experience is useful for gardens with a similar climate, such as coastal California or southern Australia.
  • Winter: at the same garden, a dry frost proves less dangerous than the same frost after a spell of rain, when soil and plant are waterlogged.

Container cultivation

On the forums, the few European growers who mention it keep it in a pot. A grower on the Italian Tropicamente forum noted in 2010 that his plant, potted for two years, produced only one leaf a year; a grower in Poland, on the Fous de palmiers forum, grows it in a pot under cover, with a heating cable, alongside other young Macrozamia. A deep pot and a free-draining mix suit the taproot, as for the rest of the genus.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney's Garden Explorer lists two plants of wild origin, from an accession of 1992, in bed 102i (updated 1 October 2026). The census of Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria, downloaded on 19 October 2026, records one plant at Cranbourne, planted in 2024. The Australian National Botanic Gardens, the Botanic Garden of South Australia, Brisbane Botanic Gardens Mount Coot-tha, Mackay Regional Botanic Gardens and the University of Melbourne's System Garden also declare it to BGCI.
  • United States: the Huntington (San Marino), San Diego Botanic Garden and the San Diego Zoo declare it to BGCI. The species does not appear in the online catalogue of Montgomery Botanical Center.
  • United Kingdom: no British garden declares it to BGCI.

Sixteen institutions in all declare the species to BGCI (checked on 26 October 2026), the others in China, France, Germany, New Zealand and Spain. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not necessarily a living plant on display today. No English-language grower's report that could be read in full has been found.

Propagation

Macrozamia mountperriensis is propagated by seed. The seeds ripen in March and April in its native range; fresh, fertilised seed is needed, which means a male and a female plant, and hand pollination outside Australia, where its thrips pollinator is absent. Wear gloves to clean off the flesh of the seeds, which is toxic.

Pests and diseases

No pest or disease specific to Macrozamia mountperriensis is documented. As for other cycads, too much water in winter and scale insects are the risks most often mentioned for the genus.

Cold hardiness

The cold hardiness of Macrozamia mountperriensis is poorly known: no measured value and no dated report with a temperature that could be read in full have been published. Mississippi State University places it in USDA zone 9b, adding that “some sources say hardy to around 22°F (9a)”, that is about −5.6 °C, without naming them; the PACSOA calls it frost tolerant without a figure.

Mediterranean and European experience

The species is grown at the Jardin zoologique tropical in La Londe-les-Maures, but the garden has published no cold data for it; elsewhere in Europe, no result in the ground is documented. A plant in the Vendée, on the French Atlantic coast, mentioned on the Fous de palmiers forum, has not been identified with certainty. In 2014, a member of the same forum relayed the ranking of an Australian specialist, who placed the species among the “least hardy”, those of inland Queensland, without giving a temperature. Without data, a pot overwintered under cover remains the prudent choice.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No dated result has been published. In Britain and the colder parts of the United States, it is a plant for a pot brought under glass in winter.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data specific to Macrozamia mountperriensis have been published, but the risk is well documented for the genus. Macrozamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a poison of the liver. Their leaves, above all the young shoots, cause irreversible hind-leg paralysis in cattle, and their seeds damage the liver (Healy, 1967). In Macrozamia miquelii, a related species, the seed kernel is among the richest in macrozamin in the genus (Ladd et al., 1993, as cited by Asmussen, 2011).

Treat the plant as toxic: wear gloves to handle the seeds, pick up those that fall and keep them out of reach of children, dogs and cats. In case of ingestion, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use specific to Macrozamia mountperriensis has been documented. Asmussen's review (2011) of the consumption of cycad seeds by Aboriginal people does not list it, whereas food use is well attested for the related Macrozamia miquelii, whose seeds were roasted and then leached for six to eight days.

Frequently asked questions

Is Macrozamia mountperriensis a form of Macrozamia miquelii?

No. It was long treated as a form of Macrozamia miquelii, but the 2001 revision confirmed it as a distinct species, and The World List of Cycads accepts it. It differs in its paler, shorter leaves without spiny leaflets, and its smaller cones and seeds.

How do I tell it from Macrozamia communis?

Look at the base of the leaves. Macrozamia communis has lowest leaflets reduced to spines, which Macrozamia mountperriensis never has. A plant of unknown origin remains hard to identify when young.

Is it frost-hardy?

Probably a little, but data are lacking. The PACSOA calls it frost tolerant without figures, and Mississippi State University places it in USDA zone 9b. Grow it in a pot and protect it in winter.

Is it endangered?

No. The IUCN lists it as Least Concern (2022), and its colonies are extensive and protected in several national parks. Like all cycads, it is still covered by CITES.

Is it poisonous?

Probably, like all cycads. No study covers this species, but the seeds and young leaves of Macrozamia are toxic to people and livestock.

Why does it grow so slowly in a pot?

That is true of most Macrozamia. An Italian grower gets only one leaf a year on a young potted plant. No source gives a method specific to the species to speed up its growth.
