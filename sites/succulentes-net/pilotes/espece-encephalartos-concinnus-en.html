Encephalartos concinnus is a cycad endemic to southern Zimbabwe, where it grows on granite hills covered with dry open woodland in the provinces of Matabeleland South, Midlands and Masvingo. This species of the genus Encephalartos forms clumps of trunks branching from the base, sometimes lying on the ground, and owes its name, “neat”, to the tidy, tile-like scales of its male cones. Described in 1969, it is now listed as Critically Endangered (CR) by the IUCN after decades of collecting from the wild.

Key facts about Encephalartos concinnus

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos concinnus R.A.Dyer & I.Verd. (1969), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15)
  • Former names and synonyms: no synonym; the plants were previously included in Encephalartos manikensis in the broad sense
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Runde cycad (English, Species+ and Flora of Zimbabwe)
  • Natural range: southern Zimbabwe (Matabeleland South, Midlands, Masvingo), around West Nicholson (Gwanda), Mberengwa (formerly Belingwe) and the Runde river (formerly Lundi)
  • IUCN status: Critically Endangered (CR), A4acd, assessment published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I (whole genus); Annex A in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: a single, incomplete report of damage after one night at −1 °C (30.2 °F) in southern Italy in December 2009 (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, granite hills (kopjes) in dry open woodland, among boulders; in cultivation, full sun to part shade and well-drained soil according to horticultural sources
  • Habit and adult size: trunks branching from the base, up to about 2.5 m long and 35 to 45 cm thick, sometimes lying down; leaves 1.5 to 2 m long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Montgomery Botanical Center and Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden in Florida, several Californian gardens, an outdoor bed at the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria, Melbourne, and three other Australian gardens; no British record
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; treat it as poisonous like all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos concinnus

Look for green, narrow male cones whose many crowded scales overlap like the tiles of a roof, and for long leaves with short, sparsely spined, evenly spaced leaflets. The original description by Robert Allen Dyer and Inez Verdoorn (1969) gives these characters:

  • Habit: a plant branching from the base, rarely single-stemmed; main root tuberous; trunks up to about 2.5 m long, sometimes gradually leaning or even lying on the ground and producing buds along their length; 35 to 45 cm in diameter
  • Leaves: mostly oblong, rounded at the tip, 150 to 200 cm long and 20 to 30 cm wide, with about 50 pairs of leaflets
  • Leaflets: inserted 1 to 1.5 cm apart, reduced to prickles towards the base of the leaf; middle leaflets 10 to 15 cm long and 2 to 2.3 cm wide, straight or slightly upturned towards the tip, set like a half-open to almost closed Venetian blind, pointed, with (0) 1 to 3 (rarely 7) fine prickles on one or both margins, mostly towards the base
  • Male cones: green, one to four per season, narrowly ovoid to subcylindrical, up to 50 cm long and 7.5 to 10 cm in diameter, on a stalk 5 to 12 cm long; middle scales 4 to 5 cm long, narrowly oblong
  • Female cones: green, about 45 cm long and 20 cm wide, on a yellowish stalk about 8 cm long
  • Seeds: red, angular, 3.5 to 3.75 cm long and 2 to 2.5 cm wide, with a fleshy coat

The authors noted differences between populations: according to the collector J. A. Smit, the leaflets of the Belingwe plants were on average shorter, more crowded and more closed than those of West Nicholson. The San Diego nursery Jungle Music describes new leaves with white, woolly leaflet margins, petioles coated in white felt and cones of both sexes green to blue-green.

Hybrids

No hybrid of Encephalartos concinnus has been described.

Possible confusion

Encephalartos concinnus belongs to the “Encephalartos manikensis complex”, a group of cycads from Zimbabwe and Mozambique with very similar foliage, which Dyer and Verdoorn separated mainly by the male cones:

  • Encephalartos manikensis and Encephalartos pterogonus have male cone scales almost as broad as long (4 to 5 cm), whereas those of Encephalartos concinnus are narrow, up to 5 cm long and about 2 cm wide
  • Encephalartos chimanimaniensis and Encephalartos munchii have loose male cones with spreading scales that leave the axis visible, whereas those of Encephalartos concinnus are compact
  • Encephalartos tegulaneus, from Kenya, also has male cone scales overlapping “like tiles”; Dyer and Verdoorn distinguished it by its broader scales and more robust plants

Without cones, identification rests on the leaflets, which the authors considered unreliable: in Encephalartos concinnus they are short (10 to 15 cm) and taper gradually towards the base, with an oblique tip.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos concinnus is an accepted name with no synonyms. Robert Allen Dyer and Inez Verdoorn published it in 1969 in the journal Kirkia (7: 147–158; description on pp. 152–154, figs 9–12), in a paper on “Encephalartos manikensis and its near allies”, in which they separated four new species from Encephalartos manikensis as it was then broadly understood. The holotype was collected a few miles south of Belingwe (now Mberengwa) by J. A. Smit (PRE 30142, National Herbarium, Pretoria).

The authors chose a Belingwe collection by Smit as the holotype because he had found cones of both sexes there. They also compared the species with Encephalartos tegulaneus and, more broadly, with Encephalartos gratus and Encephalartos bubalinus, more northern species (Malawi, Kenya, Tanzania) that the Kew botanist Ronald Melville had kept apart from this complex in 1957. The World List of Cycads, the checklist of the IUCN Cycad Specialist Group, accepts the species in its version of 15 August 2026.

Etymology and history

The epithet concinnus is Latin for “neat” or “trim”. The authors chose it for the tidy appearance of the male cones, whose scales overlap like the tiles of a roof (Haynes, 2022, cited by The World List of Cycads).

According to Dyer and Verdoorn, D. C. McGaw and then Professor H. Wild had reported a female plant in cultivation at Belingwe as early as 1953. Cycads had also been reported for several years in the Lundi area, without confirmed observation. In July 1966, L. C. Leach, E. J. Bullock and R. H. Rochat discovered a colony a few miles south-east of West Nicholson, in the Gwanda district, where only male cones were collected. Over the Easter weekend of 1967, J. A. Smit visited this colony and then, guided by local people, found a vigorous stand near Belingwe bearing fresh cones of both sexes. At the same time, a team from the Salisbury herbarium (now Harare), followed by R. C. Munch, located plants in a ravine near the Lundi bridge.

Common names

The English name Runde cycad is used by Species+ and by the Flora of Zimbabwe; it refers to the Runde river, the former Lundi, near which one of the populations grows. No name in a local language is documented.

In the wild

Encephalartos concinnus is endemic to southern Zimbabwe. The Flora of Zimbabwe describes it as a species of dry open woodland on granite kopjes, the rocky hills typical of the country. The original description places the collections on a granite hill among boulders and trees south-east of West Nicholson, in a ravine near the Lundi and near Belingwe.

According to the Flora of Zimbabwe, the species was abundant and coning at West Nicholson in 1968; only a few adults remain there today. Smaller populations are reported in the Bubiana Conservancy and north of the Runde river. LLIFLE, citing the 2010 IUCN assessment, recognises three subpopulations, at Gwanda, Mberengwa and on the Runde, with about 50 adults at Gwanda and at least 160 at Mberengwa (only accessible areas were counted), the Runde subpopulation having possibly disappeared; it gives an altitude of 800 to 900 m and mentions frequent night and morning mists. These figures could not be checked at source.

As early as 1967, J. A. Smit and R. C. Munch judged the West Nicholson colony, in a drier area, to be “slightly moribund”. The pollinators of the species have not been studied.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos concinnus is listed as Critically Endangered (CR) on the IUCN Red List (assessment by J. D. Bösenberg, published in 2022), under criterion A4acd: a population decline of at least 80% estimated over three generations spanning past and future, based on direct observation, loss of habitat and collecting. The Flora of Zimbabwe and several secondary sources still give it as Endangered (EN), an earlier status whose date could not be checked. The category and criterion are those recorded by The World List of Cycads.

The Flora of Zimbabwe describes it as “highly threatened by collectors”. According to LLIFLE, local people also cut the leaves to make sleeping mats, which reportedly killed old plants; this is not documented in a primary source.

Like the whole genus, it has been listed in CITES Appendix I since 4 February 1977, so plants, seeds and pollen are all covered, and in the European Union it is in Annex A of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, according to Species+, the official CITES and EU database, consulted in October 2026. Plants sold must come from cultivation and carry the required documents; check the current rules before any purchase or import.

Growing Encephalartos concinnus

Published growing advice comes mainly from horticultural sources. LLIFLE presents Encephalartos concinnus as an easy plant that is hard to find, suited to subtropical climates, needing well-drained soil, protection from frost and regular watering in the dry season. Jungle Music describes it as a quick grower with beautiful green leaves that makes a large, showy plant needing room.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

In southern California, Jungle Music recommends full or part sun on the coast, part sun or filtered light inland and filtered light or shade in desert climates, in a loamy, well-drained soil; it considers the species fairly tolerant of slightly moist conditions and notes that it is often planted beneath larger species for some protection from the sun. LLIFLE adds that it tolerates full sun, where the leaves stay shorter and stand up better to wind, whereas in shade they lengthen. According to LLIFLE, seedlings are less vigorous than those of Encephalartos manikensis or Encephalartos munchii during their first five years, then grow faster once the small trunk reaches 2 to 3 cm in diameter; these statements are not backed by published trials. Dyer and Verdoorn had already noted that a plant transplanted into a rock garden at Belingwe had shorter, more crowded leaflets than wild plants, which they put down to the change of habitat.

Container cultivation

In cooler climates the species is grown in pots and kept frost-free in winter. A grower in Naples (Campania, Italy) reports on the Tropicamente forum in June 2010 that he grows seedlings of Encephalartos concinnus with a trunk of about 3 cm in pots 10 by 10 cm and 25 cm deep, in a peat and perlite mix: they spent their first winter indoors without problems and were then put outside in full sun. In the same discussion, a grower in Ravenna advises against peat and heavy soils for young Encephalartos in general, recommending a free-draining mix of one third coarse sand, one third potting compost and one third lapilli or fine gravel, in large, deep pots, without repotting during growth. In France, a grower in the Deux-Sèvres reported on the Fous de palmiers forum in November 2010 that a young plant was producing a leaf that was pinkish orange at first and later turned green; another member replied that such coloured flushes often point to a deficiency in Encephalartos, without certainty for this species.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

Montgomery Botanical Center, in Coral Gables, Florida, lists three accessions in its online catalogue (6956, 200069 and 20011086, the last from 2001 according to its number), and Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden lists the species in its Garden Explorer. The Huntington, Lotusland, San Diego Botanic Garden and San Diego Zoo in California, the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley and five other American gardens report it to BGCI PlantSearch, which shows that a garden has declared the species at some point, not that the plant is alive today. In Australia, the census of the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria places it in an outdoor bed at Melbourne Gardens, and Brisbane Botanic Gardens Mount Coot-tha, Cairns Botanic Gardens and the Adelaide Botanic Garden declare it to BGCI. No British garden has been found listing it.

Propagation

The species is propagated from seed taken from cultivated plants. As it forms clumps and its fallen trunks can produce buds, suckers can also be taken from cultivated plants; no source details how they root. LLIFLE considers that seeds germinate best in shade.

Pests and diseases

No disease or pest specific to Encephalartos concinnus is documented. In cultivation, the risks reported for cycads in general apply: rot in soil that stays wet, and scale insects. A French grower reported losing his seedlings shortly after germination, without a stated cause.

Cold hardiness

No precise measurement of the cold tolerance of Encephalartos concinnus exists, and the only report found describes damage after a light frost. LLIFLE describes it as frost-sensitive.

Mediterranean and European experience

A grower in Salento (Apulia, Italy) reports on the Tropicamente forum that in December 2009 a single night at −1 °C (30.2 °F), between two days with highs close to 20 °C (68 °F), caused “quite a lot of damage” to several of his Encephalartos, including Encephalartos concinnus, Encephalartos ferox and Encephalartos chimanimaniensis; he does not say whether they were in pots or in the ground, how old they were or whether they were protected. A year earlier he had placed the species among the Encephalartos that, in his experience, have no chance of surviving a frost. The Naples seedlings mentioned above were wintered indoors. No in-ground trial is documented in France; a forum post of 2012 in which a member says his “concinnus” is doing very well refers in fact to a cactus, Notocactus concinnus, not to this cycad. For gardeners in comparable Mediterranean climates, these observations argue for frost-free winter protection.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No published source gives the lowest temperatures the species meets in its habitat, and no English-language growing report with a measured value has been found. Survival in cold conditions among Encephalartos often concerns young plants whose caudex is still underground, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil; an adult with a trunk above ground has no such protection. No USDA zone is published for the species by a botanical source. In Britain it can only be grown under glass; it is grown outdoors in southern Florida, coastal California and at Melbourne, but none of these gardens publishes cold data for it.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data are published for Encephalartos concinnus itself, but the risk is documented in the genus: the toxicity of Encephalartos horridus was demonstrated as early as 1944 by Van der Walt, and Encephalartos, like other cycads, contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; the seeds are the most dangerous part. The ASPCA reports vomiting, diarrhoea, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses that have eaten cycads.

Treat the plant as poisonous: wear gloves to clean seeds, pick up any that fall, and keep plants and seeds away from children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

The only reported use is the making of sleeping mats from the leaves, mentioned by LLIFLE on the basis of the IUCN assessment, without further detail. No ethnobotanical source confirms it.

Frequently asked questions

Why is it called concinnus?

Because its male cones look neat and orderly. Concinnus means “neat” or “trim” in Latin: Dyer and Verdoorn compared its cone scales to the tiles of a roof.

Where does it grow?

Only in southern Zimbabwe. It lives on granite hills in dry open woodland around West Nicholson (Gwanda), Mberengwa and the Runde river.

Is it endangered?

Yes, severely. The IUCN has listed it as Critically Endangered since the assessment published in 2022, because of a steep population decline caused mainly by collecting.

How big does it get?

Its trunks reach about 2.5 m long and 35 to 45 cm thick, and often end up lying down. Its leaves are 1.5 to 2 m long. Some horticultural sources quote trunks of 3 to 4 m, which the original description does not confirm.

Is it frost-hardy?

No, according to the only report available: an Italian grower saw damage after a night at −1 °C (30.2 °F). Grow it outdoors only in frost-free gardens; elsewhere keep it in a pot under glass in winter.

Can I buy one legally?

Yes, if the plant was propagated in cultivation and carries the CITES documents required for a species in CITES Appendix I and, in the European Union, Annex A. Be wary of large plants without proven origin.

Is it poisonous?

It must be assumed so. No data exist for the species, but Encephalartos seeds contain toxins dangerous to people and animals.
