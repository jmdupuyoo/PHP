Macrozamia secunda is a small cycad with no visible trunk from central New South Wales, in eastern Australia, where it grows as scattered plants in dry eucalypt forest on sandy or stony ground, from Gilgandra to Grenfell and east to the ranges around Mudgee and Capertee. This species of the genus Macrozamia is recognised by its blue-grey, arching leaves with an untwisted axis, whose leaflets all rise from the same side, almost vertically, like the teeth of a comb. Described by Charles Moore in 1884, it was reduced in 1932 to a variety of Macrozamia tridentata, before being recognised again as a species. It is listed as Vulnerable by the IUCN.

Key facts about Macrozamia secunda

  • Accepted name: Macrozamia secunda C.Moore (1884), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and the Australian Plant Census, which cite the author differently (see Taxonomy)
  • Former names and synonyms: Macrozamia tridentata var. secunda (C.Moore) J.Schust. (1932)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales; section Parazamia
  • Common names: no English common name is attested
  • Natural range: central New South Wales, Australia (central-western slopes and lower central tablelands, from Gilgandra to Grenfell, east to Mudgee and Capertee)
  • IUCN status: Vulnerable (VU B2ab(iii,v)), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated 18 September 2020 and published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union; not listed under the federal EPBC Act or in New South Wales
  • Documented hardiness: no cultivation data; in the wild, about 57 frost days a year at Mudgee according to PACSOA, with a weekly July minimum around −3.2 °C (26.2 °F) (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, dry sclerophyll forest on sandy or stony soils, under a light eucalypt canopy; in cultivation, a very free-draining soil
  • Habit and adult size: stem almost entirely underground, 8 to 15 cm across; 1 to 12 leaves 40 to 80 cm long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Australian Botanic Garden Mount Annan (plants of wild provenance); declared to BGCI by the Australian National Botanic Gardens, Burrendong Botanic Garden and the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley
  • Toxicity: no data for the species; the genus is very toxic to people and animals (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Macrozamia secunda

It is recognised by its few blue-grey, sharply keeled, arching leaves with an untwisted axis, whose narrow leaflets stand almost vertically on the upper side of the axis. The Flora of Australia (Hill, 1998) separates it by its sharply keeled, bluish grey leaves with little or no spiral twist and its short petioles. Its measurements are as follows.

  • Stem: underground or nearly so, 8 to 15 cm across, usually unbranched.
  • Leaves: 1 to 8 in the crown according to the Flora of Australia (2 to 12, 60 to 80 cm long, according to the flora of New South Wales), 40 to 80 cm long, grey-green or bluish, dull or semi-glossy; petiole 5 to 15 cm long, concave above; axis not or only slightly twisted.
  • Leaflets: 80 to 170 per leaf, simple, 8 to 20 cm long and 3 to 8 mm wide, clearly paler beneath, with an entire tip or with 2 or 3 minute teeth, and 5 to 11 veins prominent beneath; the lowest leaflets are not, or rarely, reduced.
  • Male cones: spindle-shaped, 15 to 20 cm long and 4 to 5 cm across.
  • Female cones: ovoid, 15 to 25 cm long and 7 to 9 cm across, with terminal spines up to about 3 cm long.
  • Seeds: ovoid, 20 to 35 mm long, with scarlet to red flesh.

The two field observers who wrote the page of the Palm and Cycad Societies of Australia (PACSOA) add useful details:

  • plants usually carry three or four leaves, sometimes only one, rarely up to about ten; new leaves, produced very infrequently, are a striking blue-grey;
  • the callus at the base of each leaflet is pinkish red on young leaves and turns creamy white, while the leaf fades from blue-grey to a dull blue-green;
  • the sickle-shaped or arched axis often bends sharply near its end, so that the tip of the leaf sometimes almost touches the ground;
  • near Mudgee the leaves are on average 30 to 50 cm long but stand only 20 to 35 cm above the ground because of this curvature;
  • the leaves are often damaged by insects.

L.A.S. Johnson (1959) already gave as characters the concave petiole and the stiff, usually narrow, one-sided leaflets with very crowded veins, as well as abundant supporting tissue and some glaucous bloom, typical of the species of dry places.

Hybrids

Yes, natural hybrids are reported where the species meets other Macrozamia. Johnson (1959) noted some hybridism with Macrozamia heteromera where the two species meet, and wrote that one or two inadequate specimens suggested some interbreeding with Macrozamia spiralis, although he had seen no intergrading living plants. PACSOA observes hybrid plants near Mudgee, where Macrozamia secunda grows very close to a small stand of Macrozamia reducta. Johnson also thought that the name Macrozamia secunda var. dichotoma C.Moore & Betche (1893), whose type has been lost, probably applied to a hybrid with Macrozamia heteromera. No garden hybrid has been documented.

Possible confusion

Confusion is possible mainly with the neighbouring species of New South Wales and their hybrids.

  • Macrozamia spiralis: its sister species, which replaces it further east; much larger, without sickle-shaped leaves or leaflets rising at a very acute angle (PACSOA).
  • Macrozamia heteromera and the related species with divided leaflets: their leaflets are mostly forked, whereas those of Macrozamia secunda are simple.
  • Macrozamia reducta: a larger species, with which it hybridises near Mudgee.

Taxonomy

Macrozamia secunda is accepted by The World List of Cycads and the Australian Plant Census. It belongs to section Parazamia, that of the small species with an underground stem and no mucilage canals in the leaflets, as defined by L.A.S. Johnson in his revision of the Australian Zamiaceae (1959).

Charles Moore described it in 1884. In 1932, J. Schuster, in his monograph of the cycads for Das Pflanzenreich, reduced it to a variety of Macrozamia tridentata, as Macrozamia tridentata var. secunda. Johnson (1959) restored it as a species, regarding it and Macrozamia spiralis as “a pair of vicarious species”, two related species that replace each other geographically, because the distinction seemed to him sharp enough to justify specific rank. Johnson placed Macrozamia lucida, Macrozamia spiralis and Macrozamia secunda in one small group, in which the two extreme species are both close to Macrozamia spiralis.

A point of nomenclature divides the sources. Bentham had named a doubtful variety “Macrozamia spiralis var. ? secunda” in 1873. Johnson (1959) explains that Moore made no reference to it, so that this name is not the basionym of the species but an earlier synonym in a different rank. The World List of Cycads follows this view and attributes the species to C.Moore alone; the flora of New South Wales (PlantNET) and the Australian Plant Census, as shown by the Atlas of Living Australia, write “(Benth.) C.Moore”. The plant is the same; only the citation of the author differs.

Etymology and history

The epithet comes from the Latin secundus, in the botanical sense of “with the organs turned to the same side”: it describes the leaflets, whose upper faces turn towards each other, standing “in a rather erect or vertical form” in Moore’s words, which makes a keeled leaf (Haynes, 2022, quoted by The World List of Cycads).

According to Moore, quoted by Johnson (1959), the plant was first found in 1858 near Reedy Creek, east of Mudgee, without fruit, then found again in 1883 not far from Dubbo by the Rev. J. Milne Curran, with a single old female cone. Only the 1858 collection survives, in the National Herbarium of Victoria, Melbourne (MEL); Johnson selected it as the lectotype in 1959. The original description appeared in the Journal and Proceedings of the Royal Society of New South Wales (vol. 17, p. 120). Nothing is documented about its introduction into cultivation.

Common names

No English common name specific to the species is attested in published sources (The World List of Cycads, Flora of Australia, PlantNET, Species+, Atlas of Living Australia), and no Aboriginal name is recorded. Gardeners and botanists use the scientific name.

In the wild

The species has a fairly wide range made up of scattered plants: according to the Flora of Australia, it grows on the central-western slopes and lower central tablelands of New South Wales, from Gilgandra to Grenfell and east to Mudgee and Capertee. The representative specimens come notably from the Dubbo–Mendooran road, the Bumbery Range east of Parkes, Airlie Creek east of Capertee, the Mudgee–Cassilis road and Weddin Mountain, west of Grenfell.

It lives in dry sclerophyll forest on sandy or stony country (Johnson, 1959; PlantNET). PACSOA describes isolated, scattered plants under a eucalypt canopy, sometimes with a Callitris understorey, on flat, dry ground inland, and on stony hillsides in dry forest near Mudgee, on the slopes of the Great Dividing Range. Johnson noted that in this species, as in Macrozamia heteromera, the total population is far from small, although the plants seldom form dense stands.

The climate is dry and contrasted. For Mudgee (454 m), PACSOA gives 668 mm of rain a year, spread fairly evenly between the seasons, a minimum of −3.2 °C (26.2 °F) reached at least once a week in July, a maximum of 35.5 °C (95.9 °F) reached at least once a week in January, and about 57 frost days a year. Cones, male or female, are usually solitary and appear very irregularly. No study of the pollination of the species has been published.

Conservation status and regulation

Macrozamia secunda is listed as Vulnerable (VU) on the IUCN Red List under criteria B2ab(iii,v): a small area of occupancy, fragmented or limited to few locations, with a continuing decline in habitat quality and in the number of plants. The assessment, by J.D. Bösenberg, is dated 18 September 2020 and was published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, GBIF and Crossref, checked on 26 October 2026). No listing under the federal Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act 1999 (EPBC) or in New South Wales appears in the Atlas of Living Australia (checked on 26 October 2026).

Like all Macrozamia, it has been listed in CITES Appendix II since 4 February 1977, with annotation #4 in the version adopted at CoP20, in force since 5 March 2026: live plants need an export permit, while seeds, pollen and seedlings grown in vitro in sterile containers are exempt. In the European Union it falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026 (Species+, checked on 26 October 2026). Check the regulations in force, and the customs and plant-health rules, before any purchase abroad.

In practice, buy seed-grown plants of known origin.

Growing Macrozamia secunda

No reliable source describes the cultivation of this species outside Australia: the advice below rests on its habitat and on the general rules for the genus.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Soil: very free-draining, sandy or gravelly, like the sandy or stony soils of its habitat.
  • Light: in the wild, under a light eucalypt canopy; a bright position suits it. Its tolerance of full sun outside Australia is not documented.
  • Water: about 670 mm of rain a year in its region, spread over the whole year; water during growth and let the soil dry between waterings. Avoid cold, waterlogged soil in winter.
  • Growth: in the wild, new leaves appear very rarely (PACSOA); expect a very slow plant. PACSOA reports that some enthusiasts think the smaller plants from Mudgee could grow larger in cultivation, while others believe they will stay small.

Container cultivation

No container culture has been described. A deep pot suits the underground stem; use a gritty, free-draining mix and water sparingly in winter. In a pot the plant loses the protection that the soil gives the stem.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

  • Australia: the Australian Botanic Garden Mount Annan lists two accessions of wild provenance numbered in 2023 (A2023-2432 and A2023-2434), according to its Garden Explorer. The Australian National Botanic Gardens (Canberra) and Burrendong Botanic Garden & Arboretum (Mumbil, New South Wales) also declare the species to BGCI.
  • United States: the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley declares it to BGCI. A BGCI declaration says that a garden has held the plant, not that a living specimen is there today.
  • United Kingdom: no garden declares the species to BGCI (checked on 26 October 2026).

No grower in the English-speaking world has published a cultivation result.

Propagation

Propagation is by seed. Plants are male or female: both sexes are needed and, in cultivation, hand-pollination is required to obtain fertile seed. In the wild, cones appear very irregularly. No germination data specific to the species have been published.

Pests and diseases

In the wild the leaves are often attacked by insects (PACSOA), without further detail on the species involved. No problem specific to the species is documented in cultivation; for Macrozamia grown in Europe, growers’ forums mainly mention scale insects and rot in cold, wet soil (see the genus page).

Cold hardiness

No cultivation report exists. In the wild, the species endures frequent frosts, with its stem protected by the soil.

Mediterranean and European experience

No outdoor cultivation has been reported in Europe, in the ground or in a pot. The species is known in European collections under glass: a paper published in 1984 in Delpinoa, the journal of the Naples Botanical Garden, lists Macrozamia secunda among the nine Macrozamia then grown in the garden’s glasshouses, a dated record that does not say whether the plant is still there. On the French Fous de palmiers forum, in March 2014, a grower reported the opinion of an Australian nurseryman specialising in cycads, who placed Macrozamia secunda at the top of the hardiest species of the genus, with those from around Coonabarabran and the Pilliga, without giving temperatures. On the Italian Tropicamente forum, in November 2009, a grower from Ravenna noted that seed was on offer from a specialist seller and supposed that the species “should” tolerate both heat and cold. Both are opinions, not trials.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No dated report exists on the English-language forums that can be read. The Bureau of Meteorology’s Mudgee Airport station, at 471 m, records about 45 days a year at or below 0 °C (32 °F) in the air and a lowest temperature of −7.7 °C (18.1 °F), on 12 July 2002 (records since 1990). These figures describe the region, where the plant withstands repeated frosts with an underground stem protected by the soil; they say nothing of what a plant in a pot or in wet soil would tolerate. In Britain and in the colder parts of the United States, a pot kept frost-free and dry in winter is the prudent choice.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data specific to Macrozamia secunda have been published, but the risk is well documented for the genus, and the plant should be treated as very toxic, especially its seeds and young leaves. Macrozamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a powerful poison of the liver; cattle that eat the young leaves can develop a locomotor disorder of the hindquarters, the “zamia staggers”, which McKenzie (2006) describes as irreversible, with no treatment (see the genus page).

Wear gloves to handle the seeds, keep them out of reach of children, dogs and livestock, and contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay in case of ingestion.

Traditional uses

No use specific to Macrozamia secunda is documented. Asmussen’s (2011) review of the eating of Macrozamia seeds by Aboriginal peoples does not mention this species (see the genus page).

Frequently asked questions

Why is it called Macrozamia secunda?

Because its leaflets are turned to one side. In botany the Latin secundus describes organs facing the same way: here the leaflets rise almost vertically on the upper side of the axis, which gives a keeled leaf.

How hardy is it?

It has not been tested. No cultivation report exists; in its habitat the plant endures about 57 frost days a year according to PACSOA, and the Mudgee Airport weather station has recorded −7.7 °C (18.1 °F). An Australian nurseryman, quoted on a French forum, ranks it among the hardiest Macrozamia, without figures.

How does it differ from Macrozamia spiralis?

By its size and its leaves. Macrozamia secunda is smaller, with sickle-shaped, blue-grey leaves often bent down near the tip, and leaflets rising at a very acute angle from one side. The two species replace each other from west to east.

Is it endangered?

Yes. The IUCN lists it as Vulnerable (assessment published in 2022). Its range is fairly large but made up of scattered populations, with a decline in habitat quality and in the number of plants.

Does it grow quickly?

No. In the wild new leaves appear very rarely, and plants often carry only three or four leaves. In cultivation, expect very slow growth.

Is it poisonous?

Probably, like all Macrozamia. No analysis specific to the species exists, but the seeds and young leaves of the genus are very toxic to people, pets and livestock.
