Encephalartos ituriensis is a large tree-like cycad endemic to north-eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, where it grows only on a few granite domes rising out of the Ituri forest, between 1,100 and 1,200 m. Described in 1990 by Paul Bamps, of the National Botanic Garden of Belgium, and Stanislas Lisowski, of the University of Poznań, this species of the genus Encephalartos forms trunks up to 6 m long, usually lying on the ground, crowned with straight, glossy dark green leaves 2 to 3 m long. Its description was corrected in 1998, when ripe female cones were found to measure about 55 cm, not 20 cm as first published. It has been listed as Vulnerable since 2022 because of its small number of mature plants. In cultivation it is grown mostly in containers, and young plants are easily lost to overwatering.

Key facts about Encephalartos ituriensis

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos ituriensis Bamps & Lisowski (1990), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15), Species+ and GBIF; description emended by Vorster and Gereau (1998)
  • Former names and synonyms: no synonym; the first collections had been provisionally named Encephalartos hildebrandtii
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Ituri forest cycad (Species+)
  • Natural range: north-eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo (Ituri forest), five localities known over about 160 km, between Nzaro and Nyankunde, at 1,100 to 1,200 m; a presence in Uganda, suggested in 1990 and still listed by Species+, is not accepted by The World List of Cycads
  • IUCN status: Vulnerable (VU, criterion D1), assessment by J. D. Bösenberg dated 5 March 2020 and published in 2022; previously Near Threatened (NT)
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I (whole genus, since 1977); Annex A in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: no measured value; the dated growing reports concern young plants in pots, overwintered under cover (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, exposed granite domes above the forest, with a dry-adapted flora and a very free-draining soil; in cultivation, bright light, a free-draining mix and very careful winter watering
  • Habit and adult size: trunk up to 6 m long and 40 to 50 cm thick, usually decumbent; leaves 2 to 3 m; female cones about 55 by 20 cm (Vorster and Gereau, 1998)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Kew (BGCI); Montgomery Botanical Center (accessions of 2002 and 2011), Fairchild, Lotusland, the Huntington, the San Diego gardens and others in the United States; Sydney, Melbourne (outdoor beds), Brisbane, Cairns, Adelaide and Townsville in Australia
  • Toxicity: seeds poisonous, as in all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos ituriensis

Look for a trunk that usually lies on the ground with only its tip turned up, a hemispherical crown of straight, glossy dark green leaves, rather soft leaflets with rolled-under margins and small teeth, and glaucous green cones that turn yellowish when ripe. The emended description by Vorster and Gereau (1998), based on plants seen in the wild, gives the following characters:

  • Trunk: unbranched but sometimes suckering at the base, up to 6 m long and 40 to 50 cm thick, usually decumbent, the tip turned up once it exceeds 2 m, covered with regularly arranged leaf bases; apex hairless except for a tawny down on the cataphylls
  • Leaves: numerous, spreading to ascending, straight, glossy dark green, 2 to 3 m long, without a petiole; base of the rachis covered with a short tawny down
  • Leaflets: about 80 pairs, gradually reduced to a series of prickles towards the base of the leaf; median leaflets narrowly oblong-lanceolate, with an acute but not pungent tip, not or only slightly sickle-shaped, 19 to 30 cm long and 2 to 3 cm wide, with (3–)4 to 6(–9) teeth evenly spread along both margins and about three crowded teeth near the base of the upper margin; margins rolled under, texture rather soft, leaflets not overlapping
  • Male cones: known only from the original description, narrowly ellipsoid, about 26 by 7 cm, on a thick stalk up to 16 cm long
  • Female cones: one to four, ovoid-cylindrical, about 55 by 20 cm, on a stalk about 10 cm long, glaucous green, then yellowish green when ripe, hairless; scales with a projecting outer face about 7 by 4.5 cm, smooth when fresh
  • Seeds: red fleshy coat when fresh, brown when dry; 37 to 39 mm by 21 to 28 mm including the coat

The original description by Bamps and Lisowski (1990) gave female cones of 20 by 12 cm and leaflets ending in a single spine. Vorster and Gereau showed that those cones were stunted or not fully developed, and that the plants from Nzaro, larger and more vigorous than those from Nduye, have longer leaflets and much larger cones, without these differences justifying a separate taxon.

Hybrids

No natural or garden hybrid of Encephalartos ituriensis is documented. Isolated on its granite domes, far from other species of the genus, it has no known neighbour to cross with in the wild. In collections, where several large African species may cone at the same time, hand pollination with pollen of known origin is the only way to obtain pure seed.

Possible confusion

The first collections, made by Lisowski, had been referred to Encephalartos hildebrandtii, from coastal Kenya and Tanzania. Bamps and Lisowski (1990) separated their new species from it, and from Encephalartos laurentianus, by the median leaflets: in those two species they end in two or more spiny teeth and are larger (up to 35 by 4.5 cm in Encephalartos hildebrandtii, 35 to 50 cm by 4 to 7 cm in Encephalartos laurentianus). They also considered probably mistaken the record of Encephalartos laurentianus from Ituri by Walter Robyns (1948), which was based on sterile material.

Vorster and Gereau (1998) refined these comparisons. The species growing closest is Encephalartos whitelockii, of western Uganda, from which the Rift Valley separates it: both have glaucous green cones that sometimes turn yellowish when ripe, long-stalked male cones, smooth, hairless female cone scales and leaflets that overlap little or not at all. Encephalartos whitelockii differs in its hard leaflets, without a cluster of teeth near the base of the upper margin, and in its more numerous male cones, up to eight together. The only other near-equatorial African species with soft-textured leaflets is Encephalartos laurentianus, from the Kwango valley, which is larger, with leaflets up to 50 by 5 cm, long-stalked female cones and a reddish down on the cones of both sexes.

When they described Encephalartos equatorialis from Uganda in 1995, Hurter and Glen also compared it with Encephalartos ituriensis, as both have stiff, toothed green leaves ending in a single spine. In their account, Encephalartos ituriensis has spreading, sickle-shaped leaflets with a soft, pliable blade, whereas Encephalartos equatorialis has hard, rigid, ascending leaflets that overlap strongly towards the leaf tip; the cones of Encephalartos ituriensis are greenish yellow when ripe, not dark green, and its male cones, mostly hanging, are about three times the size of those of Encephalartos equatorialis. Vorster and Gereau, three years later, described the leaflets of Encephalartos ituriensis as not or only slightly sickle-shaped.

The plates published by Goode (1989), a year before the species was described, should not be used as a reference: according to Vorster and Gereau, the plant illustrated grew in cultivation on the shore of Lake Kivu and its cones came from another plant, received from Zimbabwe without a locality.

Taxonomy

Paul Bamps (National Botanic Garden of Belgium, Meise) and Stanislas Lisowski (Adam Mickiewicz University, Poznań) described the species in March 1990 in the Memoirs of the New York Botanical Garden (57: 152–155). The type is a collection by Lisowski (no. 42909) made on 12 April 1976 on Mount Mukonza, near Nduye, at the edge of the forest, at 1,180 m; the holotype is kept at Meise (BR) and an isotype at Poznań (POZG). The other collections cited come from the same mountain (a male cone at 1,200 m in April 1976, a male plant on the rocks in August 1975) and from Mount Tatotudu, near Nduye (a young plant on a forested slope, at 1,100 m).

The authors tentatively referred other material from Ituri to the new species: a young leaf collected by Bequaert in 1914 in gallery forest near Irumu, a sterile collection from Nyankunde (1955) and photographs of a female cone about twice as long as Lisowski's. They also suggested that plants from Uganda, from the gorges of the Mpanga River, which Melville had placed in Encephalartos hildebrandtii, might belong to it. Those Mpanga plants were described in 1995 as a separate species, Encephalartos whitelockii.

In 1998 Piet Vorster (Stellenbosch University) and Roy Gereau (Missouri Botanical Garden) published in Adansonia (20(1): 211–216) an emended description, based on a specimen of the collection Lisowski 41057 and on the plants of Nzaro (Gereau et al. 5413), some thirty kilometres north-west of Nduye, which they considered conspecific. This description is now the reference. The World List of Cycads records no synonym.

Etymology and history

The epithet ituriensis refers to the Ituri forest, in north-eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, the only region where the species is known (Haynes, 2022, cited by The World List of Cycads).

The history of the species begins with Lisowski's collections of 1975 and 1976 around Nduye, some fifty kilometres north of Mambasa: he found groups of Encephalartos on granite domes emerging from dense forest dominated by Cynometra alexandri. It took fourteen years for the species to be described, then another eight years and new field observations for its ripe female cones to be known. Since 2015 it has been displayed at Rotterdam Zoo (Diergaarde Blijdorp), in the section devoted to the rainforest of Central Africa, near the okapis, according to the Dutch association of botanic gardens (NVBT).

Common names

In English the species is called the Ituri forest cycad (Species+, NVBT). No local vernacular name is documented in a verifiable source.

In the wild

Encephalartos ituriensis is known with certainty only from the Ituri forest. Vorster and Gereau (1998) record it from five localities, between 1,100 and 1,200 m, arranged in an arc over about 160 km, from around Nzaro in the north-west to around Nyankunde in the south-east, between 1° and 2° north; to protect it, they do not publish precise coordinates.

The surrounding forest is a mosaic of semi-evergreen moist forest, mixed or dominated by a single tree species. But the cycad lives outside the forest, a little higher, on exposed granite domes that carry a dry-adapted flora, with several plants isolated far from their main range and possibly endemic. At one locality it grows among the grasses of a savanna-like vegetation, a secondary formation degraded by cattle grazing (Vorster and Gereau, 1998).

The Ituri climate has a single dry season. Male cones are known only in April and female cones in March and April, at the end of the dry season and the start of the rains; Vorster and Gereau consider it likely that growth and seed production are timed by the rains, all the more so as the extremely well-drained sites are naturally dry. No data have been published on pollinators, seed dispersal or precise numbers.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos ituriensis is Vulnerable (VU) on the IUCN Red List, under criterion D1, which applies to species with fewer than 1,000 mature individuals. The current assessment, by J. D. Bösenberg, is dated 5 March 2020 and was published in 2022 (GBIF, Crossref). The table of category changes published by the IUCN for version 2022-1 records a move from Near Threatened (NT) to Vulnerable due to new information, not to a genuine deterioration. In 2003 the IUCN action plan already listed it as Near Threatened, with more than 1,000 plants and a slight decline. No published source details the threats to the species. The NVBT states that the species occurs in the Okapi Wildlife Reserve; the primary sources do not say so.

Regulation, according to Species+ (consulted through its public API on 26 October 2026):

  • CITES: Appendix I since 4 February 1977, like the whole genus; seeds are covered as well as plants;
  • European Union: Annex A, current list in Regulation (EU) 2026/1383; for artificially propagated plants from South Africa, a positive opinion of 20 June 2011 for plants with a caudex up to 7 cm in diameter, and a negative opinion above that size, confirmed on 7 February 2013; a negative opinion covering the whole genus, issued on 3 December 2010, had been lifted on 11 March 2011; Species+ also records trade suspensions concerning the Democratic Republic of the Congo (2014 and 2015), no longer in force.

These notes do not replace the official texts: check Species+ and the legislation in force before any purchase or import, and ask for documents proving that a plant was propagated in cultivation.

Growing Encephalartos ituriensis

Reliable sources on the cultivation of Encephalartos ituriensis are few: the dated reports found all concern young plants in pots, overwintered under cover, and they agree on one point: too much water, especially in winter, is the main danger. In the wild the species lives on exposed, extremely free-draining granite domes, in a region with a single dry season: a very free-draining mix, bright light and watering reserved for the growing season suit it.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

Planting in the open is documented in two mild climates, with no details of winter conditions. The Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria lists plants in outdoor beds at Melbourne Gardens (online census downloaded on 19 October 2026), and Jungle Music, a cycad nursery in Encinitas, California, writes on an undated page that quite a few enthusiasts grow the species successfully in its area. The nursery recommends full or part sun on the coast and inland, and filtered light or shade in desert areas; it calls the species tolerant of water and happy in a loamy soil, and a fast grower that soon makes a large, upright plant. These statements, made for adult plants in the ground in southern California, contrast with the experience of European growers with seedlings in pots (see below): neither source gives temperatures or dates.

Container cultivation

In Agen, south-west France, a grower reported on the French Fous de palmiers forum in December 2009 that he had lost several seedlings of Encephalartos ituriensis, and advised watering them very little. The same month, a grower from Belley (Ain) and Montpellier (Hérault) showed a seedling about eighteen months old producing its third leaf, although its root system had been almost entirely destroyed the previous winter; he had stopped watering it in that season. In November he had reported that a seedling had just made a leaf, in a glasshouse, after a year without growth. In the Deux-Sèvres, in western France, another grower showed a young plant in full growth in October 2010.

In Poland, a grower who had sown two Encephalartos ituriensis at the end of 2009 noted on the same forum in September 2013 that they grew much more slowly than his Encephalartos whitelockii of the same age, grown in the same pots and mix: they had only two or three leaves. In 2012 the new leaves of his young Encephalartos, put outside, had been damaged by wind as they formed; in 2013 only Encephalartos ituriensis was still affected. In February 2015 he regretted that his plants had produced new leaves in January, and that he had not overwintered them in a cooler room.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

The species is fairly widely held by botanic gardens. In the United Kingdom, the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, declares it to BGCI PlantSearch; the listing does not say where it is grown, but in Britain almost all cycads are kept under glass. In the United States, Montgomery Botanical Center in Coral Gables, Florida, holds two accessions, received in 2002 and 2011 according to their accession numbers; Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden lists it in its catalogue, and Ganna Walska Lotusland, the Huntington, Descanso Gardens, San Diego Botanic Garden, the San Diego Zoo and its Safari Park, the United States Botanic Garden in Washington and three Hawaiian gardens declare it to BGCI. In Australia it is listed in the online catalogues of the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney and of the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria (outdoor beds at Melbourne), and declared to BGCI by Brisbane Botanic Gardens Mount Coot-tha, the Cairns Botanic Gardens, the Botanic Garden of South Australia in Adelaide and James Cook University in Townsville. A BGCI listing shows that a garden has declared the species at some point, not that a plant is alive today.

Propagation

Seed is the only documented method. In Agen, a grower obtained a first germination in December 2008 after eight days, in a sealed box, on moist absorbent paper covered with about 1 cm of dry vermiculite, at 30 °C (86 °F) day and night (Fous de palmiers). In the Deux-Sèvres, germination had taken two to three weeks (Fous de palmiers, 2010); this grower pointed out that it depends on the maturity of the embryo and can take several months. Adult plants sometimes sucker at the base (Vorster and Gereau, 1998), but no published source describes the removal of suckers. Seeds are covered by CITES Appendix I as well.

Pests and diseases

No disease or pest specific to Encephalartos ituriensis is documented. In cultivation, the main risk observed is root rot in young plants watered during cool periods: one grower lost several seedlings, another saw the root system of a seedling almost destroyed in a single winter (Fous de palmiers, 2009).

Cold hardiness

No measured cold tolerance is documented for Encephalartos ituriensis, and no temperature record from its habitat, an equatorial region at middle altitude, has been published.

Mediterranean and European experience

No temperature reading has been published for a plant grown in France or elsewhere in Europe: the plants mentioned on the Fous de palmiers forum are young, in pots, and overwintered in a glasshouse or indoors; in Poland, they spend the winter in the house (2013–2015). As in other Encephalartos, survival in the cold reported for the genus often concerns young plants whose caudex is still underground, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil and sometimes by snow; an adult plant with an aerial trunk, as this species becomes, does not enjoy that protection. In practice, a large container kept frost-free and almost dry in winter remains the prudent choice, even on the Mediterranean coast, and the same applies to comparable climates in California or southern Australia.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No dated cold report has been published from these countries. The plants in outdoor beds at Melbourne and in southern California show that the species can be grown in the open in mild climates, but neither source says how much frost they have experienced. No botanical source gives a USDA zone for the species; the zone ratings found on some websites cite no measurement. In Britain it needs a heated glasshouse.

Toxicity and precautions

No study deals with the toxicity of Encephalartos ituriensis, but the risk is documented in other species of the genus, so it should not be considered harmless. Encephalartos, like other cycads, contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; these substances affect the liver, the digestive tract and the nervous system, and the seeds are the most dangerous part. The ASPCA reports vomiting, diarrhoea, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses that have eaten cycads.

Wear gloves to clean seeds, keep plants and seeds out of reach of children and pets, and if any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use of Encephalartos ituriensis is documented. Two publications of 2025 (Diambu and Çevik) studied fibres extracted from the plant as reinforcement for bio-based composite materials; their abstracts do not say where the plants used came from.

Frequently asked questions

Where does Encephalartos ituriensis grow?

Only in the Ituri forest, in north-eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo. It is known from five localities, on granite domes rising out of the forest, between 1,100 and 1,200 m.

Does it also grow in Uganda?

No, according to current data. In 1990 its authors suggested that plants from the Mpanga River in Uganda might belong to it, but these were described in 1995 as Encephalartos whitelockii. The World List of Cycads records it only from the Democratic Republic of the Congo, although Species+ still lists Uganda.

How big does it get?

Very big for a cycad: up to 6 m of trunk, usually lying down, and leaves 2 to 3 m long. Ripe female cones are about 55 cm long.

How can I tell it from Encephalartos whitelockii?

By the leaflets and the male cones. Those of Encephalartos ituriensis are rather soft and carry a small cluster of teeth near the base of the upper margin; those of Encephalartos whitelockii are hard and lack it, and that species bears up to eight male cones together.

Is it fast-growing?

It depends on the conditions. Dated reports from Europe show slow young plants: a Polish grower found his 2009 seedlings much slower than an Encephalartos whitelockii of the same age, and a French grower saw a seedling go a year without a new leaf. A Californian nursery, on the other hand, describes established plants in the ground as fast growers.

How should I water it?

Carefully, especially in winter. French growers lost seedlings or saw their roots destroyed by excess water, and advise watering young plants very little in the cool season.

Is it poisonous?

Yes, it should be treated as such, like all cycads. The seeds are the most dangerous part.
