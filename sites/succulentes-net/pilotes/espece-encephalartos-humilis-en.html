Encephalartos humilis is a small South African cycad endemic to the province of Mpumalanga, where it grows in rocky mountain grassland between Mbombela (Nelspruit), Machadodorp, Waterval Boven, Carolina and Lydenburg. This species of the genus Encephalartos, known in English as the dwarf cycad, is one of the smallest in the genus: its largely underground trunk rises only about 10 to 30 cm above the soil in the wild, and its leaves are 30 to 55 cm long. Described in 1951 by the South African botanist Inez Clare Verdoorn, it had previously been confused with Encephalartos lanatus and Encephalartos laevifolius. It is listed as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List.

Key facts about Encephalartos humilis

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos humilis I.Verd. (1951), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15)
  • Former names and synonyms: no synonym; before 1951, collections of the species were placed under Encephalartos lanatus or Encephalartos laevifolius
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: dwarf cycad (English), according to Species+ and PlantZAfrica
  • Natural range: South Africa, Mpumalanga (Lydenburg, Carolina, Machadodorp and Mbombela districts)
  • IUCN status: Vulnerable (VU A2acd+4acd; B1ab(iii,v)+2ab(iii,v)), assessment by J. D. Bösenberg published in 2022; also Vulnerable on the Red List of South African plants
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I since 4 February 1977; Annex A in the European Union; positive EU opinion on imports of artificially propagated plants from South Africa limited to plants with a caudex up to 7 cm in diameter (since 21 June 2017); wild collection banned in South Africa
  • Documented hardiness: no dated observation in cultivation and no measured value (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, steep grassy slopes facing east or north-east, often wedged between rocks, on sandy soil over sandstone (PlantZAfrica), but the type was collected on dolomite
  • Habit and adult size: trunk 30 to 50 cm long in all, about 12 cm of it above ground, 13 cm across (Verdoorn, 1951); up to 0.3 m above ground and 13 to 18 cm across according to PlantZAfrica; clumps formed by suckers; leaves 30 to 55 cm long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: the Huntington, the San Diego Botanic Garden and the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley (BGCI); no British or Australian garden declares it
  • Toxicity: not studied for the species; treat it as poisonous like all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos humilis

Look for its very small size: a low plant, almost hidden in the grass, which forms small clumps by suckering at the base and most often grows wedged between low rocks. The original description by Verdoorn (1951) gives the following characters:

  • Trunk: about 35 cm long in all (30 to 50 cm), of which about 12 cm is above ground in the wild (often more in cultivation: one plant observed reached 45 cm), 13 cm in diameter, usually tapering to a cone at the top, covered with a fine wool
  • Leaves: recurved, the leaflets forming a V, at first woolly and then almost hairless with a few patches of wool, 30 to 35 cm long in the field (30 to 55 cm in the Latin diagnosis)
  • Leaflets: narrowly linear, entire, ending in a small sharp point, the median ones 9 to 13 cm long and 4 to 6 mm wide, with about nine veins raised on the underside
  • Male cone: up to 15 cm long and 4 cm in diameter, with densely woolly, only slightly projecting scales
  • Female cone: unknown when the species was described

H. Basil Christian, who grew these plants, had noticed that the leaves do not emerge all together “like a bunch of asparagus” but one after another, with a very twisted petiole; Verdoorn notes that this character, perhaps accidental, can be seen on several plants of the species.

The PlantZAfrica account (Skhosana and Ntenga, 2024) completes this description. It gives a stem up to 0.3 m above ground and 13 to 18 cm in diameter, branching freely from the rootstock, greyish-green leaves 30 to 50 cm long on a petiole of 5 to 15 cm, covered at first with tangled hairs, and leaflets with a greyish, bluish or whitish waxy coating when young. The cones are greyish and densely covered with matted woolly hairs; male cones are 15 to 20 cm long and 4 to 5 cm across, and the seeds are orange-yellow, ovoid, 24 to 30 mm long and 20 to 25 mm wide. The figures it gives for the female cone, “circular, about 20–70 mm long and 90 mm in diameter”, are hard to interpret and are not confirmed by another source.

Hybrids

No hybrid of Encephalartos humilis is reported. In collections, cross-pollination with other Encephalartos remains possible: to obtain pure seed, isolate the female cones and control the origin of the pollen.

Possible confusion

Encephalartos humilis belongs to a small group of species with narrow, entire leaflets from north-eastern South Africa, together with Encephalartos lanatus and Encephalartos laevifolius. Verdoorn (1951) summarises their differences in the field:

  • Encephalartos lanatus, from the highveld around Middelburg: trunks of 1.5 to 4 ft (about 0.5 to 1.2 m), rarely 5 ft; the wool persists for a long time on the rachis; male and female cones thick and lastingly woolly;
  • Encephalartos laevifolius, from the rocky mountain ridges around Barberton: trunks of 5 to 11 ft (about 1.5 to 3.4 m); longer leaves that soon become hairless and slightly glaucous; cones becoming almost hairless;
  • Encephalartos humilis, from the grassy, rocky hills below the summits: trunk rising only a few inches above the ground; male cones much smaller (up to 15 cm long, against about 24 to 30 cm for the other two), with flatter scales.

According to R. A. Dyer, cited by Palmer and Pitman (1972), the number of veins beneath the median leaflets also helps: about nine in Encephalartos humilis, ten to twelve in Encephalartos laevifolius and ten to fourteen in Encephalartos lanatus; PlantZAfrica likewise describes the leaflets of Encephalartos humilis as nine-veined. Without cones, and especially on a young plant, identification remains difficult.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos humilis is an accepted name with no synonym (The World List of Cycads). Inez Clare Verdoorn published it in 1951 in Bothalia (volume 6, issue 1, pp. 220 and 241, plate 3), in a joint article of “Newly described species”. The type is R. A. Dyer's collection no. 4806, with a male cone, made on Schagen Hill near Nelspruit, “on dolomite with Aloe recurvifolia” according to the label cited by The World List of Cycads (holotype at the National Herbarium, Pretoria; isotypes at Grahamstown and Stockholm). Verdoorn also cites collections from Schoemanskloof, Rosehaugh and Mount Majuba, in the Berlin forest reserve.

The description of the species resolved an old confusion. In the Flora Capensis, Hutchinson and Rattray (1933) had united Encephalartos laevifolius and Encephalartos lanatus under the latter name. Henderson (1945) separated them again but still placed several collections from Rosehaugh, Schoemanskloof and Schagen under Encephalartos lanatus. Verdoorn (1951) showed that these plants formed a third species, and that the cone measurements given for Encephalartos lanatus in the Flora Capensis mixed all three. She also attributed to Encephalartos humilis older collections cited in Burtt Davy's flora of the Transvaal, such as that of Wilms along the Crocodile River.

Etymology and history

The epithet humilis means “humble” or “low” in Latin, a reference to the small size of the plant (Haynes, 2022, cited by The World List of Cycads; PlantZAfrica).

According to Verdoorn (1951), it was the collector H. Basil Christian who first suspected the existence of a third species in this group. He grew plants from the Krantz nurseries in the Schoemanskloof and had noticed that their trunks were more conical, their leaf scars smaller and their leaves smaller than those of neighbours of the other two species planted beside them for many years. The appearance of a male cone, “like a miniature” of those of the other two species, prompted the fieldwork that led to the description.

Common names

In English the species is called the dwarf cycad (Species+, PlantZAfrica), after its very short trunk. This descriptive name could apply to other small cycads and does not identify the species on its own. No Afrikaans or other vernacular name is attested in a verifiable source.

In the wild

Encephalartos humilis grows only in Mpumalanga, according to The World List of Cycads. PlantZAfrica places it in the mountains of the Lydenburg, Carolina, Machadodorp and Mbombela districts, in subalpine grassland, where it favours steep grassy slopes facing east or north-east, on sandy soils over sandstone and often between sandstone rocks. The label of the type specimen, however, records a site on dolomite, so the substrate varies between localities. Verdoorn (1951) describes it on grassy rocky hills below the summits, often hidden by the grass. The habitat receives heavy rainfall, mainly in summer, and dense mists, which according to PlantZAfrica stimulate coning. The altitude of the populations is not given in the sources.

The species lives in a fire-prone grassland. PlantZAfrica explains that it survives fire by keeping its buds protected and its stem underground, and resprouts after damage; it adds that its root system takes up nitrogen from the air, which helps it survive in nutrient-poor sandy soil. No study of its pollinators has been published; beetles are the main pollinators of cycads in general (PlantZAfrica).

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos humilis is listed as Vulnerable (VU) on the IUCN Red List, according to the 2022 assessment by Bösenberg cited by The World List of Cycads, under criteria A2acd+4acd; B1ab(iii,v)+2ab(iii,v): past and expected population decline and a small, fragmented range. The Red List of South African plants also lists it as Vulnerable (Bösenberg and Steyn, 2020, cited by PlantZAfrica). According to PlantZAfrica, the conversion of its habitat to pine and eucalyptus plantations has destroyed about 30 % of it, illegal removal of plants causes a continuing decline, and the lack of natural fires has reduced cone production and therefore reproduction; the species is protected in several nature reserves and conservancies. No population count has been published.

Regulation, according to Species+ (consulted through its public API on 25 October 2026):

  • CITES: Appendix I since 4 February 1977, like the whole genus; artificially propagated plants are treated as Appendix II specimens;
  • European Union: Annex A, current list in Regulation (EU) 2026/1383; for imports of artificially propagated plants from South Africa (source codes A and D), the opinion of the EU Scientific Review Group has been positive since 21 June 2017 for plants with a caudex up to 7 cm in diameter (it had been positive up to 15 cm since 20 June 2011);
  • South Africa: the collection of wild cycads has been banned throughout the country since February 2007 (TOPS regulations, replaced by Government Notice No. 371 of May 2012), according to SANBI documents on the genus.

The 7 cm limit is meant to allow only young plants, which are easier to link to nursery propagation than a large specimen. These notes do not replace the official texts: check Species+ and the legislation in force before any purchase or import.

Growing Encephalartos humilis

Published growing reports for Encephalartos humilis are almost non-existent. PlantZAfrica describes it as an extremely slow-growing, long-lived but sensitive species that cones infrequently, transplants poorly and is best grown under controlled conditions with frequent monitoring. Its habitat, open grassland among rocks under summer rain and mist, points to a very free-draining, sandy or gritty mix, plenty of light and water in the growing season.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

No planting in the ground outside South Africa is documented in a verifiable source. In cultivation the trunk rises further above the soil than in the wild: Verdoorn cites a cultivated plant of 45 cm. Its small size and short leaves suit a rock garden in a climate without hard frost, but no grower has published the result of such a planting.

Container cultivation

Its small size and short leaves make it well suited to container growing, in a deep pot and a very free-draining mix. On the Italian Tropicamente forum, a grower in Ravenna listed it in December 2010 among the “dwarf” Encephalartos suited to pots, without describing a plant of his own. No other container report has been published.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

Three institutions in the United States declare the species to BGCI PlantSearch: the Huntington in San Marino, the San Diego Botanic Garden in Encinitas and the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley. Montgomery Botanical Center lists no accession in its online catalogue, and no British or Australian garden declares the species. A BGCI listing shows that a garden has declared the species at some point, not that a plant is alive today. In Italy, the species does not appear in the published lists of the Botanic Garden of Naples (1993–1994) or the Orto Botanico of Messina (2009).

Propagation

Encephalartos humilis suckers at the base and naturally forms small clumps (Verdoorn, 1951). PlantZAfrica considers seed the best method. Remove the fleshy coat; since the embryo is still underdeveloped, store the seeds for a couple of months in cool conditions, then sow them in spring in sandy soil, barely covered, because burying them encourages rot. Place them in a misted glasshouse, a shaded area or a cold frame: they germinate best with high daytime and cool night temperatures, sometimes after weeks, sometimes after years. Pot the seedlings in an airy mix in narrow pots that allow the roots to develop, keep them in partial shade, since young plants tolerate neither drought nor excessive humidity, feed them and water regularly.

Because the species transplants poorly, vegetative propagation is difficult. PlantZAfrica nevertheless describes the removal of suckers in spring, when their stem is about 5 cm in diameter and before a new flush of leaves: cut them off with a very sharp knife, remove their leaves, treat both wounds with a fungicide and let them heal in conditions that prevent drying out, before planting in sandy soil and watering frequently. Seeds, like plants, fall under CITES Appendix I.

Pests and diseases

No disease or pest specific to Encephalartos humilis is described. As with other Encephalartos, the main risk in cultivation is root and stem rot in a cold, wet substrate, which a free-draining mix helps to avoid.

Cold hardiness

The cold hardiness of Encephalartos humilis is not documented: no dated trial has been published, in Europe or elsewhere. Some growers, and a South African nursery cited on a French forum, place it among the narrow-leaved Encephalartos reputed to be more cold-resistant, but without any measured value. The habitat is subalpine grassland in Mpumalanga with summer rain, but no published source gives minimum temperatures for its sites. As in other Encephalartos, an almost entirely buried trunk benefits from the warmth stored in the soil: that holds in the wild and for a young plant in the ground, but a potted plant, whose root ball freezes easily, does not enjoy that protection.

Mediterranean and European experience

No growing report has been published from France or Italy for any region. On the French Fous de palmiers forum, a member wrote in June 2011 that the species “has not been tested much” and is very rare in cultivation, and a grower in Agen listed it in January 2011 among the species he imagined to be hardy, without having tried it. These are opinions, not observations.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No dated cold report has been published from these countries. No botanical source gives a USDA zone for the species; the zone ratings found on some websites cite no measurement. In the absence of data, grow it in a container kept frost-free and protected from winter rain, in a cool glasshouse in Britain.

Toxicity and precautions

No study has dealt with the toxicity of Encephalartos humilis, but it should not be considered harmless: PlantZAfrica recalls that most parts of cycads are extremely poisonous, the toxicity of Encephalartos horridus was demonstrated as early as 1944 (Van der Walt), and Encephalartos, like other cycads, contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; these substances affect the liver, the digestive tract and the nervous system, and the seeds are the most dangerous part. The ASPCA reports vomiting, diarrhoea, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses that have eaten cycads.

Keep plants and seeds out of reach of children and pets, wear gloves to handle seeds and, if any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use specific to Encephalartos humilis is described. PlantZAfrica notes, for the genus as a whole, that the bark and stems of Encephalartos are harvested for traditional medicine markets and that their stems were once used as a source of starch in times of famine, and that the species is grown as an ornamental.

Frequently asked questions

Is Encephalartos humilis the smallest Encephalartos?

It is one of the smallest, but no published source formally ranks it first. In the wild its trunk rises only about 10 to 30 cm above the soil and its leaves are 30 to 55 cm long. Other dwarf species exist in the genus.

How can I tell it from Encephalartos lanatus?

By its size and its cones. Encephalartos lanatus forms a trunk 0.5 to 1.2 m tall and bears thick, very woolly cones; Encephalartos humilis stays almost buried and its male cones do not exceed 15 to 20 cm. Its leaflets also have fewer veins, about nine against ten to fourteen.

Is it frost-hardy?

Nobody knows. No trial has been published. It is reputed to be among the hardier narrow-leaved species, but without any measured value; grow it in a container and protect it from frost and winter rain.

Can I grow it in a pot?

Yes, its small size suits a pot. Use a deep pot and a very free-draining mix. PlantZAfrica describes it as sensitive and slow, best grown under controlled conditions with frequent checks.

Can it be imported into the European Union?

Yes, under conditions. The species is in CITES Appendix I and EU Annex A. The EU opinion is positive for imports of artificially propagated plants from South Africa, but only up to a caudex diameter of 7 cm, and CITES export and import permits are required.

Why is it threatened?

Because its range is small and fragmented and its habitat is shrinking. According to PlantZAfrica, plantations have destroyed about 30 % of its habitat, illegal collection continues and the lack of fire reduces coning.
