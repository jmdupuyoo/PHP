Encephalartos ghellinckii is a South African cycad of grassland and rocky slopes in KwaZulu-Natal and the north of the Eastern Cape, from 700 m up to about 2,400 m in the Drakensberg, where winters bring severe frost and snow. This species of the genus Encephalartos, described in 1867 by the French botanist Charles Lemaire, is recognised at once by its very narrow leaflets with rolled-under margins, unique among the cycads of southern Africa. It is listed as Vulnerable by the IUCN, and growers know it as one of the slowest cycads and one of the hardest to re-establish after transplanting.

Key facts about Encephalartos ghellinckii

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos ghellinckii Lem. (1867), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15)
  • Former names and synonyms: Encephalartos gracilis Mast. (1868), a synonym
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Drakensberg cycad (English); drakensbergbroodboom (Afrikaans); isidawu (Zulu); umguza (Xhosa), according to PlantZAfrica
  • Natural range: South Africa, KwaZulu-Natal and northern Eastern Cape, from 700 m to about 2,400 m
  • IUCN status: Vulnerable (VU), criterion C1, assessment published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I (whole genus, since 1977); Annex A in the European Union; positive EU opinion for artificially propagated plants from South Africa up to a caudex diameter of 15 cm; wild collection banned in South Africa
  • Documented hardiness: severe frost and snow in its habitat (PlantZAfrica); plants died outdoors in North Carolina in the winter of 2013–2014 at −15.6 to −12.8 °C (4 to 9 °F); no quantified European report (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, steep grassy slopes and rocky outcrops in full sun, mostly facing east; in cultivation, full sun or light shade and slightly acid soil (PlantZAfrica)
  • Habit and adult size: two forms, a highland “giant” form with stems up to 3 m tall and 30 to 40 cm thick, and a lowland “dwarf” form rarely over 1 m; clumps of up to three stems; leaves up to 1 m long (PlantZAfrica; Dyer, 1965)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: declared by the Chelsea Physic Garden in London, the Huntington, the UC Botanical Garden at Berkeley and Phipps Conservatory in the US, and Melbourne and Adelaide in Australia (BGCI)
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; treat it as poisonous like all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos ghellinckii

Look for very narrow, almost needle-like leaflets whose margins roll under: according to PlantZAfrica, this character is unique among southern African cycads and rules out confusion with any other species. Dyer (1965), in his account of the cycads of southern Africa, describes plants that are unbranched or branched from the base, with stems up to about 3 m tall, often leaning with age, 30 to 40 cm in diameter, and an open crown of brown wool; somewhat yellowish-green leaves up to about 1 m long, straight or spreading and incurved, sometimes twisted, densely woolly at first; median leaflets 8 to 14 cm long and 2 to 4 mm wide, pungent, with revolute margins; two to five densely woolly cones together, the male cones about 25 cm long and 6 to 7 cm across, the female cones up to about 22 cm by 14 cm; and golden-yellow seeds about 2.5 cm long. Henderson (1945) counted about 130 pairs of almost needle-shaped leaflets and noted that the male cone scales are distinctly stalked. The PlantZAfrica account (L. Zondi, Walter Sisulu National Botanical Garden, March 2020) distinguishes two forms:

  • Leaflets in both forms: dense, pointed, narrow, with rolled-under, untoothed margins, dark green to yellow-green above and slightly paler below; basal leaflets reduced to one or two spines
  • Highland or “giant” form: stems up to 3 m tall and 30 to 40 cm in diameter; the crown produces a large amount of golden-brown wool when coning and when new leaves appear; leaves compact and tidy, with leaflets 4 to 5 mm wide that touch without overlapping; new leaves covered in golden-brown hairs that persist until maturity
  • Lowland or “dwarf” form: thinner, reclining stems rarely more than 1 m tall and 25 to 35 cm thick; no golden-brown wool on the crown, whose bracts carry soft fine hairs; leaves open and untidy, twisted near the tip, with leaflets 2 to 3 mm wide spaced apart; new leaves covered in white wool that is later lost

Both forms sucker from the base, forming clumps of up to three stems, and bear one to five cones per crown that look stalkless because their short stalks are buried in wool. The cones keep a dense, persistent yellow woolly layer: male cones pale yellow, 25 to 30 cm long and 6 to 8 cm across, shedding pollen in early summer (November in South Africa); female cones yellowish, barrel-shaped and rounded at the top, 20 to 25 cm long and 12 to 15 cm across, smaller in the lowland form; seeds yellow to golden-yellow (PlantZAfrica). A division into “three forms” (giant, lowland and dwarf) that circulates on websites does not match the PlantZAfrica account, which describes only two.

Hybrids

No hybrid of Encephalartos ghellinckii is documented.

Possible confusion

With its rolled leaflets, an adult plant is easy to identify. The risk lies mainly with young plants sold under the name, which can be confused with other fine-leaved Encephalartos of the South African mountains, such as Encephalartos friderici-guilielmi, Encephalartos lanatus or Encephalartos cycadifolius. PlantZAfrica singles out Encephalartos cycadifolius, from the Eastern Cape, whose leaflets are not rolled and whose rachis eventually turns bright yellow and curves gradually upwards. Dyer (1965) adds that it could be confused with the cultivated sago palm, Cycas revoluta, which carries its seeds in a loose cluster rather than in compact, woolly cones. On the French Fous de palmiers forum, growers regularly compare these species and question the identity or origin of plants bought under this name; a plant with documented origin remains the only guarantee.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos ghellinckii is an accepted name. Charles Lemaire published it in 1867 in the Belgian horticultural journal L'Illustration horticole (14, miscellanies: 80–81), with a plate that appeared in the following volume (15: pl. 567); The World List of Cycads gives that plate as the type, a neotype marked with a query in its database. In his 1965 notes on the type specimens of South African Encephalartos, Dyer considered it probable that the type material had been sent from Natal by M. J. McKen (see Etymology and history).

Encephalartos gracilis Mast., published in 1868 in the Gardeners' Chronicle (25(14): 349), is a synonym. No other change of name is recorded. Dyer (1965) regarded the species as most nearly related to Encephalartos cycadifolius and Encephalartos friderici-guilielmi, two Eastern Cape species with similar woolly cones but broader leaflets.

Etymology and history

The epithet honours Édouard de Ghellinck de Walle, a nineteenth-century Belgian horticulturist and amateur botanist (Haynes, 2022), who, according to PlantZAfrica, had a fine private collection in Ghent. Lemaire, a French botanist who edited L'Illustration horticole from 1854 to 1870 for the Ghent nurseryman Ambroise Verschaffelt, described it from plants already in cultivation in Europe in the 1860s. Dyer (1965) sheds light on their origin: the register kept from 1865 to 1872 by M. J. McKen, curator of the Durban Botanic Garden, shows that he sent 670 Encephalartos overseas in seven years, including a consignment of 25 dispatched to Verschaffelt in Ghent on 22 February 1867; since the species is almost confined to Natal, Dyer thought it likely that McKen also supplied the type material of Encephalartos ghellinckii. The genus name comes from the Greek for “bread in the head”, after the starchy pith of the trunks, used as famine food (PlantZAfrica).

Common names

PlantZAfrica gives the English name Drakensberg cycad, the Afrikaans drakensbergbroodboom, the Zulu isidawu and the Xhosa umguza. The English name is also used by Species+ and by the cycad encyclopaedia of the Palm and Cycad Societies of Florida (archived 1998–2000). PlantZAfrica adds that in Zulu most cycads are called isqgiki somkhovu, a name linked to witchcraft beliefs (see Traditional uses).

In the wild

Encephalartos ghellinckii grows in KwaZulu-Natal and the north of the Eastern Cape (The World List of Cycads). According to PlantZAfrica:

  • its altitudinal range runs from 700 m in KwaZulu-Natal to about 2,400 m in the Drakensberg
  • the highland form grows at 1,100 to 1,800 m in the KwaZulu-Natal Drakensberg; the more widespread lowland form occurs lower down, near the Umkomaas River and in the north of the Eastern Cape
  • both forms grow on steep grassy slopes and rocky outcrops in full sun, preferably on east-facing slopes, and large plants often hang over the edge of cliffs above deep gorges
  • summers are mild to hot and winters very cold, with severe frost and snow
  • grass fires are common and play an important part in the production of cones and new leaves

Dyer (1965) places the species from the southern foothills of the Drakensberg, in the Eastern Cape near Flagstaff, northwards along the range to Mont-aux-Sources in Natal; Henderson (1945) recorded it as far south as Tabankulu in Pondoland and eastwards almost to the Natal coast. PlantZAfrica points out that very woolly cones, like those of this species, Encephalartos cycadifolius, Encephalartos friderici-guilielmi or Encephalartos lanatus, are poorly suited to wind pollination, and that current research attributes the pollination of cycads to insects attracted by the heat and scent of the cones. The pollinators of this species have not been identified.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos ghellinckii is listed as Vulnerable (VU) on the IUCN Red List (assessment by J. D. Bösenberg, published in 2022) under criterion C1: fewer than 10,000 mature plants with a continuing decline. According to PlantZAfrica, several subpopulations are declining; those of the lowland form, which are more accessible, are the most threatened and may be on the brink of extinction through poaching, whereas the highland form grows in inhospitable, less accessible terrain and is at lower risk. The main threat is the removal of plants from the wild, which could take more than 10 % of the remaining plants within thirty years. The species is also sold in the traditional medicine (muthi) markets of Durban and Johannesburg (Cousins and colleagues, cited by PlantZAfrica), as the 2017 Biodiversity Management Plan for Encephalartos (Government Gazette No. 40793) also notes.

Like the whole genus, it has been listed in CITES Appendix I since 4 February 1977 and, in the European Union, in Annex A of the wildlife trade regulations (current list: Regulation (EU) 2026/1383). According to Species+, consulted in October 2026, the EU Scientific Review Group has held a positive opinion since 20 June 2011 for imports from South Africa of artificially propagated plants with a caudex up to 15 cm in diameter, and a review covering the whole genus was under consultation in September 2026. Collecting wild cycads has been banned throughout South Africa since 2007 (Government Notice No. 371 of 2012 for the current text). Check the current rules and ask for proof of cultivated origin before any purchase.

Growing Encephalartos ghellinckii

It is one of the slowest cycads and one of the hardest to re-establish. According to PlantZAfrica, the leaves of large specimens die soon after transplanting unless removed beforehand; even when moved successfully, a plant whose leaves and roots have been cut can take up to five years to produce a new set of leaves, and plants in cultivation rarely cone. The same source recommends slightly acid soil in full sun or light shade, and notes that the species tolerates frost and snow.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

A grower in Corsica reports on the Fous de palmiers forum (October 2013) that he planted a very young plant in the ground in the north of the island, where it grew slowly but without difficulty; left alone for more than eight years and watered only by rain, it survived. A grower in Agen, south-western France, wrote in November 2013 that the species takes badly to transplanting and that one of his plants had been marking time for two years. On the Italian Tropicamente forum, a grower in Ravenna opened a discussion in May 2012, illustrated with habitat photographs, reporting the common opinion that the highland form “should be” the hardiest Encephalartos but also the hardest to grow and averse to humid climates; no one replied with a growing experience. For readers in comparable Mediterranean climates, these reports show only that the species can live for years outdoors in a mild, dry site if it is left undisturbed.

Container cultivation

The Corsican grower later moved his plant into a pot with a large root ball: it lost all its leaves, produced nothing for four years and then made one leaf a year, in June. This is in line with the PlantZAfrica warning about transplanting: a container plant should be potted on with its root ball intact and moved as little as possible.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

The species is rare in public collections. The Chelsea Physic Garden in London, the Huntington and the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley in California, Phipps Conservatory and Botanical Gardens in Pittsburgh, the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria in Melbourne and the Botanic Garden of South Australia in Adelaide declare it to BGCI PlantSearch, which shows that a garden has declared the species at some point, not that a plant is alive today; the current census of the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria does not list it. Montgomery Botanical Center's online catalogue does not list it either. The Juniper Level Botanic Garden in North Carolina grew it outdoors until it was lost in the winter of 2013–2014 (see Cold hardiness).

Propagation

Encephalartos ghellinckii is propagated from seed and suckers (PlantZAfrica):

  • sow the seed in river sand on a heated bench at 24 to 28 °C (75.2 to 82.4 °F); germination starts after three to six weeks, but some seeds take longer, especially without heat
  • remove suckers in early spring (July to September in South Africa), during dormancy, once they reach about 8 cm in diameter: dig away the soil, cut the sucker off cleanly at its point of attachment with a sharp, clean saw, chisel or knife, and treat both cut surfaces with fungicide, sulphur or a tree sealant
  • plant suckers of at least 25 cm with their own roots straight into the ground; leave smaller or rootless ones to callus for a few weeks in a cool, dry place before potting

A member of the Fous de palmiers forum notes that in this species, as in other fine-leaved Encephalartos, unfertilised ovules do not develop, so that well-formed sterile seeds would be rare; no published source confirms this.

Pests and diseases

No disease or pest specific to the species is documented. The main risk in cultivation, as with other Encephalartos, is rot in cold, wet soil, made worse by the species' slow recovery after any root disturbance.

Cold hardiness

The species tolerates severe frost and snow in its habitat (PlantZAfrica), but no minimum temperature has been measured in the wild, and the only quantified result in cultivation is a failure. In Encephalartos, survival in the cold often concerns young plants whose caudex is still underground, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil and, in the mountains, by snow; adults with an aerial trunk have no such protection.

Mediterranean and European experience

No dated cold report with a temperature exists for the species in France or Italy. The only long-term in-ground report comes from northern Corsica (see Outdoor and in-ground cultivation), where a plant lived for more than eight years with no care but without any temperature data. Rankings posted on forums that place Encephalartos ghellinckii among the hardiest Encephalartos, with the other fine-leaved species, are opinions based on its habitat, not measurements.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

In Raleigh, North Carolina, the Juniper Level Botanic Garden listed Encephalartos ghellinckii among the cycads that did not survive outdoors, unprotected and unmulched, in the winter of 2013–2014, when its lows were between 4 and 9 °F (−15.6 to −12.8 °C) depending on the site (article of 7 August 2014); the size of the plants is not given. In the Drakensberg the highland form grows up to about 2,400 m, with very cold winters, severe frost and snow (PlantZAfrica), but no source gives a minimum temperature. USDA zones proposed by some websites are not based on any cited measurement. In Britain, botanic gardens grow almost all their cycads under glass, and no outdoor trial of this species is documented.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data have been published for Encephalartos ghellinckii, but the risk is documented in the genus: the toxicity of Encephalartos horridus was demonstrated as early as 1944 (Van der Walt), and Encephalartos, like other cycads, contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; the seeds are the most dangerous part. The ASPCA reports vomiting, diarrhoea, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses that have eaten cycads.

Treat the plant as poisonous: wear gloves to handle seeds and keep plants and seeds away from children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

According to PlantZAfrica, the bark and stems of Encephalartos ghellinckii are used in traditional medicine and traded at muthi markets in Durban and Johannesburg; the plant is also traded as a garden ornamental, and both practices, now illegal, contribute to the decline of wild plants. The same source records a Zulu belief about cycads in general: planted in front of the gate of a homestead, a cycad protects it from evil spirits, since the zombie (umkhovu) used in witchcraft will sit on top of the cycad, called isqgiki, “chair”.

Frequently asked questions

Is Encephalartos ghellinckii frost-hardy?

It tolerates frost and snow in the Drakensberg, but its limit is unknown. No minimum temperature has been measured in the wild, and in North Carolina plants in the ground died at −15.6 to −12.8 °C (4 to 9 °F). Outside mild areas there are no data to recommend it for planting out.

How can I recognise it?

By its very narrow leaflets with margins rolled under, a character unique among southern African cycads according to PlantZAfrica. Young plants nevertheless remain hard to identify without a reliable label.

Why is it so difficult to grow?

Because it resents transplanting. A plant that has been moved can go up to five years without new leaves, as PlantZAfrica notes and growers in France confirm. It also grows very slowly and rarely cones in cultivation.

Can it be transplanted?

As little as possible. If unavoidable, keep a large root ball and expect a long period without leaves: a grower in Corsica waited four years for the first new leaf.

What are the two forms?

A highland or “giant” form of the KwaZulu-Natal Drakensberg, with stems up to 3 m and golden-brown wool, and a more widespread lowland or “dwarf” form, rarely over 1 m, with thinner leaflets and white wool on young leaves. The lowland form is the more threatened.

Can I buy one legally?

Yes, if it is a nursery-propagated plant. The species is in CITES Appendix I and EU Annex A, but the EU accepts imports from South Africa of artificially propagated plants with a caudex up to 15 cm. Ask for the permits and proof of origin.

Is it poisonous?

It must be assumed so. No study concerns this species, but Encephalartos seeds contain toxins dangerous to people and animals.
