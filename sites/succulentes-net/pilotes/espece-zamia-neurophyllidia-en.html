Zamia neurophyllidia is a cycad of Central America, found on the Caribbean slope from southern Nicaragua through Costa Rica to western Panama, in lowland rainforest. This species of the genus Zamia, described by Dennis W. Stevenson in 1993 and long included in Zamia skinneri, has large, thin leaflets deeply pleated between the veins, which give it its name. It belongs to the Zamia skinneri complex and is one of the few Zamia that the IUCN rates as Least Concern.

Key facts about Zamia neurophyllidia

  • Accepted name: Zamia neurophyllidia D.W.Stev. (1993), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and by GBIF; the World Checklist of Vascular Plants (WCVP), on which POWO is based, also accepts it (consulted through GBIF on 31 October 2026)
  • Former names and synonyms: no synonym; long treated as a dwarf form of Zamia skinneri
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales; Zamia skinneri complex
  • Common names: no attested vernacular name in English or in Spanish; a seed merchant sells it as “Corrugated Zamia”, a trade name
  • Natural range: Nicaragua (Río San Juan, Atlántico Sur), Costa Rica and Panama (Bocas del Toro), on the Caribbean slope
  • IUCN status: Least Concern (LC), assessment published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (the whole genus, since 1977; seeds exempt under annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: no measurement (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, lowland rainforest, sometimes flooded, and forested hills near Changuinola (Panama); no published cultivation trial
  • Habit and adult size: trunk up to 2 m (commonly 1.5 m); leaves 0.6 to 1.8 m long with 6 to 11 pairs of pleated leaflets (Taylor and colleagues, 2008)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Montgomery Botanical Center (Florida) holds two accessions; Fairchild lists it in its online catalogue; other American gardens and the Cairns Botanic Gardens (Queensland) declare it to BGCI; no British record
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; treat it as poisonous (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Zamia neurophyllidia

It is recognised by its broad, elliptic, thin leaflets, deeply grooved between the veins so that they look pleated, by its green new leaves and by its rather slender, usually solitary trunk. Two descriptions exist, and they do not fully agree.

The original description (Stevenson, 1993) gives a trunk no more than 60 cm tall and 6 to 12 cm across, 3 to 10 leaves 0.5 to 1 m long, a petiole up to 30 cm, and 5 to 8 (rarely 10) pairs of elliptic leaflets, the median ones 12 to 20 cm long and 6 to 10 cm wide (5 to 8 cm in the Latin diagnosis). The male cones, cream to fawn and cylindrical, measure 5 to 8 cm by 1 to 2 cm; the female cones, brown and shortly stalked, ovoid-cylindrical, 10 to 15 cm by 3 to 4 cm; the seeds are ovoid, 1 to 1.5 cm long, with a red sarcotesta (the fleshy outer seed coat).

In 2008 Taylor, Haynes and Holzman showed that these figures had partly been taken from plants of other regions, since assigned to other species, and that the holotype itself exceeds them (median leaflets 26.5 to 27.5 cm long; an immature female cone 19 cm by 5 cm, on a stalk 6 cm long that is anything but short). Their new description, based on three populations close to the type locality near Changuinola (Bocas del Toro), gives:

  • Trunk: up to 2 m (commonly 1.5 m), 5 to 12 cm in diameter, usually solitary and thinner than in the other species of the complex.
  • Leaves: 61 to 180 cm long, up to 22 per crown (11.5 on average), with 6 to 11 pairs of leaflets (8 on average), bright green and hairless when they emerge, then a glossy mid-green; petiole 25.5 to 92 cm long.
  • Leaflets: elliptic to oblong-elliptic, pleated between the veins, with a toothed margin; the median ones measure 20 to 31.3 cm by 6.1 to 10.5 cm.
  • Female cones: solitary, cylindrical to globose, upright, 13.2 to 20 cm by 5.7 to 7.2 cm, covered in fawn down that turns mid-brown, on a stalk 7.5 to 15.5 cm long.
  • Seeds: ovoid to globose, 1.8 to 2.5 cm by 0.9 to 1.5 cm, with a bright red sarcotesta, from 120 to more than 220 per cone.
  • Male cones: not yet observed in these populations in 2008.

On the French forum Fous de palmiers, an amateur who saw the species in its habitat in Costa Rica described in January 2013 trunks up to 1.5 m, leaves of 2 m and leaflets up to 30 cm by 12 cm; he also noted (August 2013) that plants growing in sun look very different from those in shade. These reports agree with the 2008 description.

Hybrids

No hybrid has been described or published. On the Fous de palmiers forum, an amateur who had observed the Zamia of Bocas del Toro considered it “very likely”, in October 2015, that Zamia nesophila hybridises with Zamia neurophyllidia on the mainland; no publication confirms this. The idea, sometimes put forward, of a “complex of hybrid species” uniting Zamia neurophyllidia and Zamia skinneri could not be traced to a verifiable source.

Possible confusion

The oldest confusion is with Zamia skinneri, of which Zamia neurophyllidia was long thought to be a mere dwarf form. Both have green new leaves and broadly elliptic, deeply veined leaflets. According to Stevenson (1993), Zamia neurophyllidia stays smaller as an adult; the chromosome number (2n = 18 against 22) has also been put forward to separate them, but Taylor and colleagues (2008) consider this argument weak, because the published counts were made on plants from Costa Rica, not on the Panamanian populations of the two species.

Several populations once placed under Zamia skinneri have since been described as species in their own right: in 2008 Taylor, Haynes and Holzman described Zamia imperialis, Zamia hamannii and Zamia nesophila. According to the same Fous de palmiers contributor (December 2014), Zamia skinneri, Zamia nesophila and Zamia neurophyllidia produce green new leaves, whereas Zamia hamannii and Zamia imperialis produce brown ones. Without a known provenance, telling these species apart remains difficult.

Taxonomy

The World List of Cycads and GBIF accept the name Zamia neurophyllidia; the World Checklist of Vascular Plants (WCVP), on which POWO is based, also accepts it (consulted through GBIF on 31 October 2026). The species was described in 1993 by Dennis W. Stevenson in the journal Brittonia (45(1): 10, fig. 5), in a study of the Zamiaceae of Panama. The holotype (no. 3078), collected on 18 January 1980 in the province of Bocas del Toro, is kept in the herbarium of the University of Panama (PMA), with an isotype in New York (NY); the published sources disagree on the name of the collector.

Stevenson separated the species from Zamia skinneri, of which it was thought to be a dwarf variety. He saw it as a reduced version of that species: at the type locality and around it, the plants had kept this small size as adults for nearly thirty years, and he treated it as a local endemic, sister to Zamia skinneri, adapted to a wet lowland habitat (Stevenson, 1993).

In 2008 Taylor, Haynes and Holzman put forward a hypothesis of “artificial dwarfism”: near the type locality, local people have long cut the stems of cycads to use their mucilage as glue, and most plants bore machete marks; in January 2008, in the best-preserved forest, plants with trunks over 1.5 m were common. They nevertheless concluded that the plants of the Changuinola area, though not dwarf, differ enough from Zamia skinneri, as they redefined it, to form a distinct species, whose true range remains to be established.

Etymology and history

The epithet is formed from the Greek neuro-, “nerve”, and phyllon, “leaf”, in reference to the strongly pleated leaflets (Haynes, 2022, as given by The World List of Cycads).

The history of the species is bound up with that of the Zamia skinneri complex, long treated as a single variable species. Described in 1993, it was redefined in 2008 from populations close to its type locality, at the same time as three new species were split from Zamia skinneri.

Common names

No vernacular name is attested, in English or in Spanish. The seed merchant Rare Palm Seeds lists the species as “Corrugated Zamia”, a descriptive trade name with no traditional use behind it.

In the wild

The World List of Cycads places the species in Nicaragua (Río San Juan, Atlántico Sur), Costa Rica and Panama (Bocas del Toro), on the Caribbean slope. According to the original description, it grows between 100 and 200 m above sea level, in rainforest that is sometimes flooded (Stevenson, 1993). The altitude of 0 to 1,000 m sometimes quoted appears in no published source.

The populations near the type locality, studied in 2007 and 2008, occupy steep hills covered with primary tropical forest along the Changuinola river and its tributaries, under a tropical climate with fairly even rainfall all year round; young plants and seedlings are common there. Nearly ripe female cones were seen in October and opening cones in January; pollination is thought to be carried out, as in the other Zamia of Panama, by beetles of the genus Pharaxonotha (Taylor and colleagues, 2008). These observations concern only the Changuinola area. In Costa Rica, the amateur quoted above found in January 2013 a population at the end of its fruiting season, with all the cones open and the seeds already germinating inside them.

Conservation status and regulation

Zamia neurophyllidia is rated Least Concern (LC) by the IUCN, in an assessment by J.D. Bösenberg published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads); GBIF gives the same category. In 2003 the IUCN cycad action plan rated it Near Threatened (NT), with 4,000 to 5,000 plants known, a slight decline due to habitat destruction and populations present in protected areas. In 2008 Taylor and colleagues recalled that the 2006 Red List rated it Near Threatened and proposed raising it to Vulnerable (VU A3c), because a hydroelectric dam project in the Changuinola area could affect several populations.

Like all species of the genus except Zamia restrepoi, Zamia neurophyllidia falls under CITES Appendix II, where Zamia have been listed since 4 February 1977. International trade in the plants is therefore allowed, but requires an export permit from the country of origin. The listing carries annotation #4: seeds, pollen, seedlings and tissue cultures obtained in vitro and transported in sterile containers are not covered by CITES. In the European Union the species falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, whose current list is set by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026.

Species+ has a page for the species, which confirms this status (consulted on 11 October 2026). This information does not replace the official texts: check the regulations in force before buying or exchanging plants, and ask the seller for proof of origin.

Growing Zamia neurophyllidia

No growing guide specific to the species has been published. The French specialist nursery Cycadales.eu (2023) places it among the lowland forest Zamia of Central and South America, which it considers “difficult” outside the tropics, while noting that Zamia neurophyllidia seems to tolerate fairly wide swings in temperature, unlike the closely related Zamia imperialis. For this group it recommends:

  • Compost and water: a free-draining compost, neutral to acid; water that is also neutral to acid.
  • Temperatures: nights around 22 °C (71.6 °F), brief dips to about 15 °C (59 °F) being tolerated; days of 28 to 30 °C (82.4 to 86 °F), most species coping with 34 to 35 °C (93.2 to 95 °F).
  • Air humidity: high, 70 to 90 %, for best growth.
  • Where to grow it: a glasshouse, a conservatory, a bright heated room or even a growth chamber.

In Singapore, where the Singapore Botanic Gardens declare the species to BGCI, the National Parks Board’s Flora & Fauna Web describes it as a slow-growing plant for semi-shade, in moist but well-drained soil, propagated by seed; its description follows the concept of the species before the 2008 revision (Jones, 2002).

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

No outdoor cultivation has been documented with dates, temperatures and results. The species is a plant of tropical lowland rainforest; outside the frost-free humid tropics, nothing suggests it can be grown in the open all year. The British nursery Trebrown gives it a USDA zone 10b rating, its own commercial classification, without any published observation behind it.

Container cultivation

In the UK and in other temperate climates it is a plant for a heated glasshouse, a conservatory or a bright, warm room, in the conditions that Cycadales.eu recommends for the group: night temperatures around 22 °C (71.6 °F), high air humidity and a free-draining, acid to neutral compost. No grower’s report gives the results of such cultivation over several years.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In short: present in several American collections, declared by one Australian garden, no British record.

  • United States: the online catalogue of Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida) lists two accessions, dating from 1986 and 2008 according to their accession numbers (consulted on 24 October 2026); Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden lists the species in its online catalogue and in BGCI. The Huntington (San Marino, California), Naples Botanical Garden (Florida), the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley, Marie Selby Botanical Gardens (Sarasota, Florida), the National Tropical Botanical Garden (Kalāheo, Hawaii), the Botanical Garden at the University of Hawaii at Hilo, the Four Seasons Resort on Lāna‘i and the US National Plant Germplasm System also declare it to BGCI.
  • Australia: the Cairns Botanic Gardens (Queensland) declare it to BGCI.
  • United Kingdom: no record in BGCI or in the online catalogues of British gardens (Edinburgh, Cambridge, Chelsea Physic Garden) checked on 19 October 2026.

A BGCI declaration shows that a garden held the plant at some point, not that it is alive today, and the sources do not say whether these plants grow outdoors or under glass. Because the species was redefined in 2008, plants accessioned earlier under this name may belong to other species of the Zamia skinneri complex.

Propagation

The plant is dioecious: a male and a female plant are needed and, in cultivation, hand pollination, as the grower in Pedara (Sicily) was preparing to attempt in 2007. No germination data specific to the species have been published; according to the Fous de palmiers contributor, the seeds collected in Costa Rica in January 2013 were already germinating in the cone. Seeds are exempt from CITES (annotation #4), but make sure they come from cultivated plants.

Pests and diseases

In the wild populations studied near the type locality, no notable pest or disease was observed (Taylor and colleagues, 2008). In cultivation, no data specific to the species exist; for the Zamia of this group, Cycadales.eu mainly warns of root rot in a compost that is too wet in cool weather.

Cold hardiness

There is no measurement of cold tolerance for Zamia neurophyllidia. It is a species of tropical lowland rainforest, and nothing suggests that it tolerates frost.

Mediterranean and European experience

No in-ground cultivation has been reported in France, and no dated frost episode concerns it. On the Fous de palmiers forum, the amateur who observed it in Costa Rica judged it “very unlikely”, in January 2013, that it would survive below 0 °C (32 °F), and advised against it in France except in a very favoured position, because its need for humidity combines poorly with cold: this is an opinion, not an observation. In Italy the species appears in the list of cycads of the “Pietro Castelli” botanic garden in Messina, Sicily (page dated 23 February 2009), without any detail of how it is grown, and on the Tropicamente forum a grower in Pedara, near Catania, reported in October 2007 a male and a female plant coning at the same time, without saying whether they were in the ground or in pots. Neither mention gives any temperature. The Jardin zoologique tropical at La Londe-les-Maures, in the Var, has published no observation of this species.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No dated growing report has been published for any of these countries.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity study specific to Zamia neurophyllidia has been published. The risk is, however, documented in the genus and in other cycads: Zamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, and for Zamia furfuracea the ASPCA cites cycasin and BMAA, a neurotoxic amino acid, with vomiting, bloody diarrhoea and liver damage in dogs, cats and horses; the seeds are the most dangerous part. In the absence of evidence to the contrary, treat the plant as poisonous: keep seeds and leaves out of reach of children and animals, and wear gloves to clean seeds. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet straight away.

Traditional uses

No use specific to the species is documented, since it was long confused with Zamia skinneri and its relatives. Near the type locality, however, local people have long cut the stems of these cycads to use the mucilage that oozes from them as glue (Taylor and colleagues, 2008).

Frequently asked questions

Is it a palm?

No. Despite its trunk and foliage, Zamia neurophyllidia is a cycad (Zamiaceae), a gymnosperm that reproduces by cones, with no close relationship to palms.

Where does it grow?

On the Caribbean slope of Central America, from southern Nicaragua through Costa Rica to Panama (Bocas del Toro), in lowland rainforest; the original description gives 100 to 200 m above sea level.

What does its name mean?

It comes from the Greek neuro-, “nerve”, and phyllon, “leaf”, for its leaflets, strongly pleated between the veins.

How can I tell it from Zamia skinneri?

Not easily. The original description made it smaller, and published counts give it 18 chromosomes against 22; but in 2008 it was shown that it can exceed 1.5 m and that the chromosome argument is weak. Without a known provenance, the distinction remains tricky.

Is it endangered?

No, according to the IUCN, which rates it Least Concern (2022). In 2008, however, botanists proposed rating it Vulnerable because of a dam project in the Changuinola area.

Can I grow it in the UK?

Only under glass. No cold data exist; it is a tropical lowland forest species, to be kept in a heated glasshouse, a conservatory or a bright, warm room with humid air.

Is it poisonous?

It must be assumed so. No analysis exists for the species, but the cycads studied contain serious toxins, especially in their seeds.
