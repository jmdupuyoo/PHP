Zamia loddigesii is a cycad of the Atlantic slope of Mexico, widespread on the coastal plain of the Gulf of Mexico from Tamaulipas to southern Veracruz and also recorded in Hidalgo, Oaxaca, Tabasco and Chiapas. This species of the genus Zamia has an underground stem and a few upright leaves with tough, lance-shaped leaflets. Described in 1843 from plants grown in Europe, it is the Mexican Zamia with the most tangled naming history, and in 2024 part of its populations was transferred to Zamia lawsoniana. The IUCN lists it as Near Threatened; in Hidalgo it is linked to beliefs about the origin of maize.

  • Accepted name: Zamia loddigesii Miq. (1843), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and by GBIF; POWO could not be consulted directly
  • Former names and synonyms: among others Zamia galeottii de Vriese (1845), Zamia leiboldii Miq. (1847), Zamia mexicana Miq. (1861), Zamia sylvatica Chamb. (1926), Palmifolium loddigesii (Miq.) Kuntze (1891); Zamia lawsoniana, treated as a synonym from 1995, was reinstated in 2024
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: no English name; in Mexico teocinte, teocintle, camotillo and amigo del maíz
  • Natural range: Mexico (Chiapas, Hidalgo, Oaxaca, Tabasco, Tamaulipas, Veracruz), from 20 to 1,200 m above sea level
  • IUCN status: Near Threatened (NT), criterion A4ac, assessment published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (the whole genus, since 1977; seeds exempt under annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union; in Mexico, “threatened” under standard NOM-059-SEMARNAT-2010
  • Documented hardiness: no verified data; a Californian nursery reports survival down to freezing point with some leaf damage (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, also in disturbed ground, including fallow maize fields; no growing recommendations published for the species
  • Habit and adult size: underground stem; 2 or 3 leaves (sometimes 4), 45 to 95 cm long

  • Toxicity: poisonous, like all cycads; badly prepared stems can cause serious poisoning (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Zamia loddigesii

It is a Zamia without an above-ground trunk, with a few upright or spreading leaves and lance-shaped, leathery leaflets toothed towards the tip; it is told from related species mainly by the width of its leaflets and by its cones. The species varies a great deal from one population to another, however (Limón and colleagues, 2016). The measurements below come from the comparison published in 2024 by Nicolalde-Morejón and colleagues, based on plants measured in the wild:

  • Leaves: 2 or 3, sometimes 4, upright or spreading, light green when they emerge, 45 to 95 cm long and 30 to 41 cm wide; greyish-white hairs on the petiole; petiole and rachis straight, the petiole carrying 40 to 60 stout prickles.
  • Leaflets: 12 to 23 pairs, lance-shaped and leathery; the median leaflets measure 16 to 26 cm by 1.8 to 3.1 cm and carry 13 to 18 teeth.
  • Male cones: 1 to 6 per stem tip, 8 to 14 cm long and 1.8 to 3.5 cm wide, on a stalk 6 to 10 cm long.
  • Female cone: ellipsoid to cylindrical, light brown (cream or yellowish according to the key in the same paper), 12 to 16 cm long and 4 to 6 cm wide, on a stalk 5 to 10 cm long, with 40 to 80 fertile scales.

In 2011 Nicolalde-Morejón and colleagues described the “Zamia loddigesii complex” as the hardest group to identify among Mexican Zamia: leathery, linear-lanceolate to oblanceolate leaflets, an underground stem, and the highest chromosome numbers in the genus in Mexico. Jungle Music, a Californian cycad nursery, likewise notes how variable it is in leaf and leaflet shape, with small, often underground stems 2 to 4 in (5 to 10 cm) across, green leaves 2 to 3 ft (60 to 90 cm) long and stems that sucker to form several heads.

Hybrids

No confirmed natural hybrid and no garden hybrid has been documented. In the description of Zamia grijalvensis (2012), the authors considered that Zamia loddigesii might be one of the parents of this species from Chiapas; the hypothesis rested on a few chromosome counts and has not been confirmed. A hybrid origin, with Zamia loddigesii among the possible parents, had also been suggested for Zamia katzeriana; the transcriptome data of Lindstrom and colleagues (2024) show that it is not close to Zamia loddigesii.

The idea that Zamia loddigesii is itself a stabilised hybrid between Zamia furfuracea and Zamia spartea circulates among growers: a member of the French Fous de palmiers forum put it forward in 2010 and 2014. No publication supports it, and the 2024 phylogeny (see Taxonomy) does not bear it out.

Possible confusion

Confusion is frequent, because the variability of Zamia loddigesii overlaps that of several related species. The key published in 2024 separates:

  • Zamia lawsoniana, from southern Veracruz, northern Chiapas and Tabasco: linear leaflets no more than 1.46 cm wide, petiole and rachis slightly twisted, cones on very long stalks (up to 32 cm in males). Some Zamia loddigesii from disturbed ground have narrow leaflets, but their rachis and petiole remain straight.
  • Zamia prasina, from the Yucatán peninsula (Mexico, Belize, Guatemala): similar leaflets but brown female cones; in Tabasco (Macuspana), some populations are intermediate between the two species (Limón and colleagues, 2016).
  • Zamia spartea, from the Isthmus of Tehuantepec, and Zamia stenophyllidia, from Michoacán: narrow, linear leaflets.
  • Zamia inermis, from central Veracruz: leaflets with entire margins.
  • Zamia furfuracea, the cardboard palm of gardens: obovate or oblanceolate, thick, keeled leaflets.

In 2011 Nicolalde-Morejón and colleagues grouped Zamia loddigesii, Zamia paucijuga and Zamia polymorpha (now Zamia prasina) in the Zamia loddigesii complex, which in their view needed population studies with molecular data to be clarified. The confusion also exists in the trade: on the Fous de palmiers forum, a grower reported in November 2009 that seed bought as Zamia loddigesii had given him Zamia furfuracea.

Taxonomy

Zamia loddigesii is accepted by The World List of Cycads and by GBIF; POWO, the site’s nomenclatural authority, could not be consulted directly. The species was published in 1843 by the Dutch botanist Friedrich Anton Wilhelm Miquel in the Tijdschrift voor Natuurlijke Geschiedenis en Physiologie (volume 10, pages 72 to 73), from plants grown in Europe.

For a long time no herbarium specimen that could serve as the type was known, and Stevenson and Sabato (1986) had typified the name on the description itself. A cultivated specimen was later found at Utrecht, the collection Van Houtte 3374, prepared before publication and matching Miquel’s description: it was designated as lectotype in the revision of the Zamia of Mega-Mexico (Nicolalde-Morejón and colleagues, 2009).

The name has gathered many synonyms: twelve related names according to Nicolalde-Morejón and colleagues (2011), fourteen names and varieties in The World List of Cycads, and not twenty-six as sometimes stated. They range from Zamia galeottii and Zamia leiboldii (1840s) to Zamia mexicana Miq. (1861), not to be confused with Ceratozamia mexicana, and Zamia sylvatica (1926).

The reinstatement of Zamia lawsoniana (2024)

In 2024 Fernando Nicolalde-Morejón, Lilí Martínez-Domínguez, Marlon Aramis González-Aguilar and Dennis Wm. Stevenson reinstated Zamia lawsoniana Dyer, described in 1884 and treated as a synonym of Zamia loddigesii since 1995: the populations of the border area between south-eastern Veracruz, north-eastern Chiapas and Tabasco, with narrow leaflets and cones on very long stalks, now belong to that species. In practical terms, a plant labelled Zamia loddigesii from Tabasco or southern Veracruz may today be a Zamia lawsoniana.

The same paper places Zamia loddigesii in the Mesoamerican “Furfuracea” clade of the phylogeny of Calonje and colleagues (2019). The transcriptome-based study of Lindstrom and colleagues (2024), which sampled a plant from Veracruz, puts it in a “loddigesii-furfuracea complex” that also includes Zamia paucijuga, the Zamia of the Pacific coast.

Etymology and history

The epithet honours Joachim Conrad Loddiges (1738–1826), a London nurseryman who specialised in exotic plants and was a correspondent of Miquel (Haynes, 2022, as quoted by The World List of Cycads). Like many nineteenth-century cycads, the species was described from European nursery plants before being studied in the wild; even its lectotype is a cultivated plant.

Common names

There is no English name. In Mexico, the IUCN Red List, as relayed by GBIF, records the Spanish names teocinte, teocintle, camotillo and amigo del maíz (“friend of maize”). Among the Nahua of the Huasteca, in northern Hidalgo, teocintle means “sacred” or “ancestral maize cob” (Bonta and colleagues, 2019). Beware of the homonym: “teosinte” also refers to the wild relatives of maize, grasses of the genus Zea.

In the wild

Zamia loddigesii in the broad sense is widespread along the coastal plain of the Gulf of Mexico, from Tamaulipas to southern Veracruz (Nicolalde-Morejón and colleagues, 2024). The World List of Cycads and the 2014 review of Mexican cycads record it in six states (Chiapas, Hidalgo, Oaxaca, Tabasco, Tamaulipas and Veracruz), from 20 to 1,200 m, in the biogeographical regions of the Gulf of Mexico and the Sierra Madre Oriental. Since the reinstatement of Zamia lawsoniana in 2024, some populations of Tabasco and southern Veracruz no longer belong to it, and its exact range has yet to be updated. The World List of Cycads does not list Belize, where the closely related Zamia prasina grows.

It is one of the most collected Mexican Zamia (about 80 herbarium collections in 2011), because it lives in easily accessible areas. It also grows in disturbed ground: in Hidalgo its leaves come up from underground stems in maize fields left fallow and burnt (Bonta and colleagues, 2019).

Conservation status and regulation

The species is listed as Near Threatened (NT, criterion A4ac) on the IUCN Red List, in the assessment by J.D. Bösenberg published in 2022 and cited by The World List of Cycads; GBIF gives the same category (the Red List itself could not be consulted). In Mexico it is listed as “threatened” under standard NOM-059-SEMARNAT-2010 (Nicolalde-Morejón and colleagues, 2014).

A genetic study of four populations (González-Astorga and colleagues, 2006) found relatively high genetic diversity compared with other cycads and tropical trees, but also a loss of alleles linked to the isolation of populations fragmented by habitat change; the authors recommended sanctuaries, protected areas and less land clearance.

Like all species of the genus except Zamia restrepoi, Zamia loddigesii falls under CITES Appendix II, where Zamia have been listed since 4 February 1977. International trade in the plants is therefore allowed, but requires an export permit from the country of origin. The listing carries annotation #4: seeds, pollen, seedlings and tissue cultures obtained in vitro and transported in sterile containers are not covered by CITES. In the European Union the species falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, whose current list is set by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026.

Growing Zamia loddigesii

No reliable source publishes growing recommendations specific to the species or verified cultivation data; the advice that circulates online (composts, light, feeding, minimum temperatures) cites no source. Two specialist nurseries give advice drawn from experience.

The French specialist nursery Cycadales.eu places Zamia loddigesii among the Zamia of Mexico, Guatemala, El Salvador and Honduras, of “moderate” difficulty: in its view, these species mostly come from climates with a marked dry season, tolerate dry air and a fairly dry compost in winter, and are not particular about the nature of the compost as long as it drains well, with a preference for slightly limy conditions. It advises 5 to 10 °C (41 to 50 °F) in winter, with daytime rises to 20 to 25 °C (68 to 77 °F), and in summer 15 to 20 °C (59 to 68 °F) at night and 25 to 30 °C (77 to 86 °F) by day, with at least four to six months of summer temperatures (Cycadales.eu, 2023). In California, Jungle Music says the species prefers filtered light, or possibly full sun near the coast, and well-drained soil. These are growers’ recommendations for a group of species or from a nursery, not observations published on the species.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

No documented in-ground result has been published, in Europe or in the English-speaking world. Jungle Music’s remarks on cold (see Cold hardiness) suggest that planting out is conceivable only in the mildest, practically frost-free gardens, such as coastal southern California, where the nursery is based; this is a deduction, not a published result.

Container cultivation

In the UK and other climates with frosty winters, grow it in a pot in a free-draining compost, kept frost-free in winter and on the dry side, as Cycadales.eu advises for the Mexican group, and give it a warm summer, outdoors or under glass.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In short: widely held in American collections, present in a few Australian gardens and, under glass, at Kew.

  • United States: Montgomery Botanical Center, in Coral Gables (Florida), lists 30 accessions in its online catalogue, from 1983 to 2006, most of them from 2000 according to the garden’s numbering (consulted on 24 October 2026). Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden lists it in its online Garden Explorer (inventory of 19 October 2026). Seventeen American gardens declare it to BGCI, among them the Huntington, Lotusland, San Diego Botanic Garden, Descanso Gardens and UC Berkeley in California, Naples, Marie Selby and Miami Beach Botanical Garden in Florida, Honolulu and the National Tropical Botanical Garden in Hawaii, and, in cities with cold winters where it can only be kept under glass, Brooklyn Botanic Garden, Lincoln Park Conservatory (Chicago) and Como Park Zoo and Conservatory (St Paul).
  • United Kingdom: the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, declares it to BGCI; no other British record is documented.
  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney lists it in its Garden Explorer (inventory of 19 October 2026); the Botanic Garden of Adelaide, Noosa Botanic Gardens, Cairns Botanic Gardens and the System Garden of the University of Melbourne declare it to BGCI.

A BGCI declaration shows that a garden held the plant at some point, not that it is alive today, and plants grown under this name before 2024 may belong to Zamia lawsoniana. The British nursery Trebrown gives it a USDA zone 9b rating, its own commercial classification.

Propagation

The species is dioecious: a male and a female plant are needed to obtain seed and, in cultivation, hand pollination. In the wild a single male plant can carry many cones, up to six per stem tip according to the 2024 measurements. Clean the seeds of their flesh, wearing gloves, and sow them fresh. Jungle Music notes that the stems sucker to form several heads, which in principle allows careful division. No verified data on germination time have been published.

Pests and diseases

No pest or disease specific to the species has been documented. In cultivation, Zamia are mainly at risk from stem rot in a cold, waterlogged compost, and from scale insects, including the cycad aulacaspis scale (Aulacaspis yasumatsui).

Cold hardiness

No verified measurement exists of the cold resistance of Zamia loddigesii. The USDA zone 9b sometimes quoted, and the −3 to −4 °C (26.6 to 24.8 °F) sometimes given, rest on no dated observation. The species lives from sea level to 1,200 m, in a climate with a marked dry season, and its stem is underground.

Mediterranean and European experience

No in-ground cultivation has been reported in France, and no dated frost episode concerns it. On the Fous de palmiers forum, messages mentioning “loddigesii” almost all concern the palm Latania loddigesii; the few messages on the Zamia (2009–2014) deal with identification and sowing, without any cold data (search of 13 October 2026). In Italy, the cycad page of the Messina botanic garden (2009) lists it in its collection, and a photograph of the Palermo botanic garden published on the Tropicamente forum the same year bears its name; neither says whether the plants grow outdoors or what they went through in winter, and the identification is not verified. The Jardin zoologique tropical at La Londe-les-Maures, in the Var, has published no observation of this species.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

The only English-language indication found comes from Jungle Music, in Encinitas (California): the nursery writes that the species can survive temperatures down to freezing point with some leaf damage, and that buried stems escape damage. This is a nursery’s general remark, without a date, place or measured temperature. Trebrown’s zone 9b rating is a commercial classification, not a measured result. In the UK it belongs under glass in winter.

Toxicity and precautions

The plant is poisonous. Zamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, and cycads also contain BMAA, a neurotoxic amino acid; for Zamia furfuracea the ASPCA reports vomiting, bloody diarrhoea and liver damage in dogs, cats and horses, the seeds being the most dangerous part. For the cycads of Mexico and northern Central America, Bonta and colleagues (2019) report that poorly detoxified starch causes symptoms ranging from stomach ache and vomiting to death, and cite a death attributed to eating Zamia stems. Keep the plant out of reach of children and animals and wear gloves to clean the seeds. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet straight away.

Traditional uses

Zamia loddigesii is one of the Mexican cycads best documented by ethnobotany (Bonta and colleagues, 2019). In the Huasteca of Hidalgo, the Nahua still believe that Zamia loddigesii and Ceratozamia fuscoviridis are ancestors of maize. When maize is sown, the teocintles that come up again in fallow fields are left in place, because they are thought to give the maize vigour, resistance to pests and bigger cobs. A male plant covered in cones evokes the local maize deity, Chicomesintli (“seven cobs”) or Chicomexochitl (“seven flowers”).

The starch of Zamia, taken from the stems and not the seeds, is now only very rarely eaten in Mexico and northern Central America (Bonta and colleagues, 2019). Eating it requires lengthy detoxification; do not attempt any preparation.

Frequently asked questions

How does Zamia loddigesii differ from Zamia furfuracea?

By its leaflets. Those of Zamia furfuracea are obovate or oblanceolate, thick and stiff like cardboard; those of Zamia loddigesii are lance-shaped, thinner and toothed. Both species are Mexican and often confused in the trade, even in seed lots.

What changed with Zamia lawsoniana?

In 2024 the populations of southern Veracruz, northern Chiapas and Tabasco, with narrow leaflets and cones on very long stalks, were transferred to Zamia lawsoniana, reinstated as a species. Zamia loddigesii remains the species of the Gulf coastal plain, from Tamaulipas to Veracruz.

Why does it have so many synonyms?

Because it varies a great deal between populations and because, in the nineteenth century, new species were described from plants grown in Europe. The World List of Cycads now lists fourteen, names and varieties included.

Is teocintle the same as the teosinte of maize?

No. In Mexico, teocintle or teocinte refers to Zamia loddigesii and other cycads; teosinte is the wild relative of maize, a grass of the genus Zea. The Nahua of Hidalgo nevertheless regard the teocintle as an ancestor of maize.

Is it a hybrid?

Nothing proves it. The idea of a stabilised hybrid between Zamia furfuracea and Zamia spartea circulates among growers, but no publication supports it.

Is it frost-hardy?

Not with any certainty: no verified data exist. A Californian nursery reports survival down to freezing point with some leaf damage; in the UK, grow it in a pot and keep it frost-free in winter.

Is it poisonous?

Yes. Like all cycads it contains dangerous toxins, especially in the seeds; even the stem, once eaten, requires lengthy detoxification.
