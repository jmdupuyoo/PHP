Zamia skinneri is a large, trunk-forming cycad of the Caribbean coast of Panama, known only from the province of Bocas del Toro, where it grows in the understorey of lowland tropical rainforest. This species of the genus Zamia carries leaves more than 2 m long, with broad leaflets pleated between the veins. Described in 1851 from the notes and drawing of the Polish collector Józef Warszewicz, it gave its name to a whole group of pleated-leaved Central American Zamia: for more than a century the name covered several different plants, until a revision published in 2008 restricted it to the Bocas del Toro plants whose young leaves emerge green. The IUCN rates it Endangered (2022 assessment).

Key facts about Zamia skinneri

  • Accepted name: Zamia skinneri Warsz. (1851), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15); Taylor and colleagues (2008), Species+ and GBIF write “Warsz. ex A.Dietr.”; the World Checklist of Vascular Plants (WCVP), on which POWO is based, also accepts it (consulted through GBIF on 31 October 2026)
  • Former names and synonyms: Aulacophyllum skinneri (Warsz.) Regel (1876), Palmifolium skinneri (Warsz.) Kuntze (1891); before 2008 the name was also applied to plants now placed in other species (see Taxonomy)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: no English name attested; “cebolla roja” in Spanish (Species+)
  • Natural range: Panama, province of Bocas del Toro (mainland Caribbean coast), from sea level to 400 m
  • IUCN status: Endangered (EN), criteria A2cd+4cd, 2022 assessment; Taylor and colleagues had proposed Near Threatened (NT) in 2008
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (seeds exempt under annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: no measurement; a lowland rainforest plant, to be kept warm all year (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, the understorey of humid tropical forest, often on steep slopes; in cultivation, a free-draining, neutral to acid compost (group advice from the nursery Cycadales.eu)
  • Habit and adult size: trunk 2.4 m tall or more and 7.5 to 20 cm in diameter; leaves 1.06 to 2.27 m long, with 4 to 10 pairs of leaflets up to 51 cm long (Taylor and colleagues, 2008)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Montgomery Botanical Center (Florida) holds seven accessions; a dozen American gardens and two Australian ones declare the name to BGCI, though some of these plants may belong to other species of the complex; no British record
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; treat it as poisonous, like all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Zamia skinneri

The first thing to look at is the colour of the new leaves: in Zamia skinneri they emerge bright green and hairless, with no red or brown tint. The description below comes from the revision by Taylor, Haynes and Holzman (2008), based on more than a hundred plants from three populations near Chiriquí Grande, in the province of Bocas del Toro:

  • Habit: a shrub or small tree with an upright, usually solitary trunk 2.4 m tall or more and 7.5 to 20 cm in diameter; in some populations the trunks stay small.
  • Leaves: 106 to 227 cm long, up to 27 per crown (9.6 on average), with 4 to 10 pairs of leaflets (7 on average); they emerge bright green and hairless and turn mid-green and glossy.
  • Petiole: 48 to 110.5 cm long, mid-green; on average about as long as the rachis.
  • Leaflets: elliptic to oblong-elliptic, pleated between the veins on the upper surface, with a saw-toothed margin; those in the middle of the leaf measure 25.5 to 51.4 cm by 7 to 16.6 cm, about four times as long as wide.
  • Male cones: single or in groups of 2 to 6, 10 to 20 cm long and 2 to 2.7 cm wide, covered in golden-russet to brownish-yellow down, on a stalk 4.5 to 8 cm long.
  • Female cones: solitary, covered in brownish-yellow to tawny down when they emerge; close to receptivity they measure 8 to 12 cm by 4 cm, on a stalk 3 to 6 cm long. The authors never saw a ripe female cone, and the seeds have not been described.

The original description (Dietrich, 1851), written from Warszewicz’s notes and drawing and translated by Taylor and colleagues, gave an upright trunk of 4 to 6 feet (1.2 to 1.8 m), thicker at the base, 6 to 9 leaves slightly shorter than the trunk, a petiole armed with strong spines, opposite leaflets a foot and a half long (45 cm) and half a foot wide (15 cm), elliptic-lanceolate, almost entire but sharply toothed towards the tip, and a single barrel-shaped cone “with green scales on an orange ground”. The plants of Chiriquí Grande match these figures well: a trunk of 1.83 m and middle leaflets of 44 by 16 cm.

Jay Vannini (2019), a collector who grows the species, adds that mature plants usually carry five or six pairs of leaflets and that the new leaves are a glossy emerald green; he describes the plants as normally short-trunked, while noting that some quite tall trunks have been photographed in the wild.

No chromosome number has been published for Zamia skinneri in its present sense. The counts published under this name (2n = 22 for plants from central Panama, 2n = 18 for plants from Costa Rica) refer to plants now placed in other species (Taylor and colleagues, 2008).

Hybrids

No hybrid of Zamia skinneri has been documented by a study. Schutzman (2004), cited by Taylor and colleagues (2008), had suggested that Zamia neurophyllidia and Zamia skinneri formed a hybrid complex. On the French forum Fous de palmiers, a member who has seen the species in the wild considered a local hybridisation with Zamia imperialis, a species with a fairly wide range, likely in December 2014, and mentioned a possible hybrid swarm with Zamia hamannii: these are the views of an experienced amateur, not verified findings.

Possible confusion

Zamia skinneri belongs to a group of pleated-leaved Panamanian Zamia that were only separated in 2008. The key by Taylor and colleagues (2008) tells them apart as follows:

  • Zamia dressleri: an underground stem and leaves usually borne singly;
  • Zamia imperialis, from central Panama: usually 3 leaves, middle leaflets 27 to 75 cm long and 6.5 to 21 cm wide, new leaves dark red then bronze, maturing dull dark green, never bright green;
  • Zamia hamannii, from the island of Escudo de Veraguas: new leaves orange-red to pink, covered in silvery down;
  • Zamia nesophila, from the islands of the Bocas del Toro archipelago, and Zamia neurophyllidia, from inland parts of the same province: new leaves green as in Zamia skinneri, but middle leaflets shorter (38 cm at most) and proportionately broader (length no more than 3.6 times the width), 5 to 12 pairs, and a petiole usually shorter than the rachis.

In cultivation, the commonest confusion concerns the colour of the new leaves. For decades the large plants of central Panama with red new leaves were called “Zamia skinneri”, sometimes “true skinneri”; they are now Zamia imperialis. Vannini (2019) recalls that this species was formerly well known in the trade as “Zamia skinneri red emergent”. On the Fous de palmiers forum, the member quoted above pointed out in December 2014 that Zamia skinneri, Zamia nesophila and Zamia neurophyllidia produce green new leaves, whereas Zamia hamannii and Zamia imperialis produce brown or red ones. He thought that a plant grown indoors by another member, raised from seed bought in 2010 as Zamia skinneri, whose new leaves passed through a light brown stage, was probably Zamia imperialis. A label alone is therefore not enough.

Outside Panama, Zamia amplifolia, from Colombia, has similar pleated leaflets and has been mistaken for Zamia skinneri (Whitelock, 2002, cited by Taylor and colleagues, 2008).

Taxonomy

Zamia skinneri is a name accepted by The World List of Cycads, which attributes it to Warszewicz alone; Taylor and colleagues (2008), Species+ and GBIF write “Warsz. ex A.Dietr.”. The World Checklist of Vascular Plants (WCVP), on which POWO is based, also accepts it (consulted through GBIF on 31 October 2026). The species was published in 1851 by Albert Dietrich in the Allgemeine Gartenzeitung (volume 19, page 146), together with Zamia lindleyi, from the notes and drawing of Józef Warszewicz, who had travelled through “Veraguas” in 1850. As no herbarium specimen exists, Stevenson and Sabato (1986) chose the drawing published by Dietrich as the lectotype.

The World List of Cycads recognises two synonyms: Aulacophyllum skinneri (Regel, Gartenflora 25: 143, 1876), from the genus Aulacophyllum in which Regel grouped the Zamia with pleated leaflets, and Palmifolium skinneri (Kuntze, Revisio generum plantarum 2: 803, 1891).

The Zamia skinneri complex and the 2008 revision

The history of the name is long and confused. Taylor and colleagues (2008) list more than a century of conflicting interpretations: the name was applied to plants from Guatemala or southern Nicaragua to central Panama, and the large plants of central Panama with red new leaves were regarded as the “true skinneri”. In 1993 Stevenson had already described Zamia neurophyllidia for part of these plants. Their revision, based on fieldwork carried out from 2004 to 2008, concluded that:

  • in 1850 “Veraguas” meant the whole western half of Panama, so the type locality remains open, but the simplest explanation is that Warszewicz saw the abundant plants of the hills near Chiriquí Grande, on the trail that led to the pass where Zamia lindleyi grows;
  • the living plant Warszewicz sent in 1850 to the English collector James Yates had “bright green and shining” leaves, incompatible with the red-flushing plants;
  • the lithograph in the Botanical Magazine (Hooker, 1861), reproduced in 1875 by Van Houtte, closely resembles the plants of Chiriquí Grande;
  • the name must therefore be kept for the green-flushing plants of the mainland coast of Bocas del Toro; the red-flushing plants were described in the same work as Zamia imperialis, together with two new island species, Zamia hamannii and Zamia nesophila.

In practice, many data published under the name Zamia skinneri before 2008 concern other plants: the ecological studies by Clark and Clark at La Selva Biological Station, in Costa Rica, or the census by Osborne (1995), which counted about 2,000 plants in the wild. In 2006 Michael Calonje observed very similar populations on the Caribbean coast of south-eastern Costa Rica, near the Panamanian border; whether they belong to Zamia skinneri has yet to be established. Vannini (2019) likewise considers that the species may extend into the forests of extreme south-eastern Costa Rica.

On the phylogenetic side, Caputo and colleagues (2004), as cited by Taylor and colleagues, placed Zamia skinneri and Zamia neurophyllidia in a “pseudoparasitica clade” with Zamia pseudoparasitica, Zamia acuminata and Zamia obliqua; these results predate the 2008 revision and the description of Zamia imperialis, Zamia hamannii and Zamia nesophila. The study by Lindstrom and colleagues (2024), which included a plant from Bocas del Toro, takes Zamia skinneri as an example of deeply pleated venation and shows that this character arose several times independently in the genus, mainly in the clade of the isthmus (Costa Rica and Panama) and in that of the western Andes.

Etymology and history

The epithet honours George Ure Skinner (1804–1867), a Scottish amateur botanist and plant collector active in Central America (Haynes, 2022, as given by The World List of Cycads).

According to Dietrich (1851), Warszewicz brought back from Central America seeds and living trunks of this species and of Zamia lindleyi, which he distributed in England and Germany; the seeds, although they looked fresh, did not germinate. Dietrich also reports that Warszewicz had found the two species growing together in the cordilleras of Veraguas. James Yates, who owned one of the largest cycad collections in Europe, received in 1850 a plant with a trunk 30 cm long and 7.6 cm in diameter, whose twelve leaves had been cut off; it produced three new leaves in 1852 and four in 1853 (Seemann, 1857, as cited by Taylor and colleagues, 2008). In 1861 the Botanical Magazine published a colour plate of a male plant bearing cones, grown at Kew and received from a Berlin nurseryman.

Common names

No English name is attested. Species+ gives the Spanish name “cebolla roja” (“red onion”), without explaining its origin.

In the wild

According to The World List of Cycads, Zamia skinneri lives only in Panama, in the province of Bocas del Toro. Taylor and colleagues (2008) described it as common and widespread, in many large populations along the mainland coast of the province: mainly a lowland species, often growing on steep slopes, in primary and secondary humid tropical forest, from sea level to 400 m. Vannini (2019), on the other hand, writes that true Zamia skinneri appears to be relatively uncommon in nature. Taylor and colleagues regard as an error the altitude of more than 2,100 m reported by Dietrich, since no Panamanian cycad grows above 1,500 m and no pleated-leaved Zamia above 750 m. Species+ still lists Colombia, Costa Rica and Panama, following the old broad concept of the name.

The climate of the region is tropical, with fairly regular rain all year round. A Fous de palmiers member who has seen the species in its habitat reported in February 2014 that the plants grew in a very steep ravine, under very tall trees, with very little light.

Cones probably appear around May or June; male cones shed their pollen and female cones become receptive in September and October. Coning seems infrequent: no ripe female cone had been seen. The Zamia of Panama are thought to be pollinated by small beetles of the genus Pharaxonotha (Erotylidae). No visible pest or disease was noted in the wild (Taylor and colleagues, 2008).

Conservation status and regulation

Zamia skinneri is rated Endangered (EN) on the IUCN Red List under criteria A2cd+4cd, in an assessment by J.D. Bösenberg published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads; category confirmed by the Red List data relayed by GBIF, consulted on 31 October 2026). These criteria correspond to a population decline of at least 50 % over three generations, observed or expected, linked to habitat degradation and exploitation. In 2008 Taylor and colleagues were still proposing Near Threatened (NT): the species was common, but many populations were already suffering from habitat destruction. GBIF nevertheless shows Zamia skinneri Warsz. ex A.Dietr. as Not Evaluated (NE), because the IUCN assessment is attached there to the spelling Zamia skinneri Warsz.: a difference in author citation, not a different assessment.

Like all Zamia except Zamia restrepoi, Zamia skinneri falls under CITES Appendix II, where the genus has been listed since 4 February 1977. The listing carries annotation #4 (version from CoP20, in force on 5 March 2026): seeds, pollen, seedlings and tissue cultures obtained in vitro and transported in sterile containers, as well as cut flowers of artificially propagated plants, are not covered by CITES. International trade in the plants themselves, on the other hand, requires an export permit. In the European Union the species falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, whose current annexes are set by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026. Species+ names the species and confirms this status (consulted on 13 October 2026).

This general information does not replace the official texts: check the regulations in force before buying or exchanging plants, ask the seller for proof of origin and prefer plants raised from seed in cultivation.

Growing Zamia skinneri

No growing advice specific to Zamia skinneri in its present sense has been published in a scientific source. Simon Lavaud, of the specialist nursery Cycadales.eu in Montpellier (southern France), places the species among the lowland forest Zamia of Central and South America, which he considers difficult to grow outside the tropics: European climates are too cool and too dry for them, even in summer, and he recommends a glasshouse, a conservatory, a bright heated room or even a growth chamber (Lavaud, 2023). For this group he gives:

  • a free-draining, neutral to acid compost, and neutral to acid water;
  • at night, 22 °C (71.6 °F), with brief dips to about 15 °C (59 °F) tolerated;
  • by day, 28 to 30 °C (82.4 to 86 °F), up to 34–35 °C (93.2–95 °F) for most species;
  • air humidity of 70 to 90 % for best growth.

The Fous de palmiers member who saw the species in Panama considered in February 2014 that light is not the main factor for this plant of deeply shaded understorey: temperature and humidity matter most, and plants often suffer from poor air circulation; in tropical conditions they would reach maturity at about 4 to 6 years. These are views from experience, not measurements.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

No outdoor cultivation outside the tropics has been documented. Vannini (2019) recommends light to medium shade for the cycads of Central America and Panama, most of which benefit from constant warmth and high humidity. A frost-free, humid tropical climate is the only one in which in-ground cultivation can be considered, which is a deduction from the habitat rather than a tested result. The San Diego nursery Jungle Music states that in southern California the species needs a glasshouse, as it does not tolerate frost; its description, however, also covers red-flushing plants, so it may refer in part to Zamia imperialis.

Container cultivation

Vannini (2019) considers Zamia skinneri an exceptionally fine ornamental species, very well suited to long-term pot culture for growers who have a little warm, shaded space; he grows a mature plant in his collection in Guatemala. In a cool climate this means a heated glasshouse or a bright, warm room all year, with a compost that stays slightly moist and air that is as humid as possible.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In short: the name is present in many American collections and in two Australian gardens, but true Zamia skinneri is rare in cultivation (Vannini, 2019); no British record.

  • United States: the online catalogue of Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida) lists seven accessions, five from 2001, one from 2005 and one from 2011 according to their accession numbers (consulted on 24 October 2026). Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden (Miami), the Huntington (San Marino, California), San Diego Botanic Garden (Encinitas, California), Marie Selby Botanical Gardens (Sarasota, Florida), Miami Beach Botanical Garden, the United States Botanic Garden (Washington), Longwood Gardens (Pennsylvania), Atlanta Botanical Garden, Fort Worth Botanic Garden, Denver Botanic Gardens and Honolulu Botanical Gardens declare it to BGCI.
  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney and the Cairns Botanic Gardens (Queensland) declare it to BGCI.
  • United Kingdom: no record in BGCI or in the online catalogues of British gardens (Edinburgh, Cambridge, Chelsea Physic Garden) checked on 19 October 2026.

A BGCI declaration shows that a garden held the plant at some point, not that it is alive today, and the sources do not say whether these plants grow outdoors or under glass. Plants acquired under this name before 2008, or raised from seed sold as Zamia skinneri, may belong to Zamia imperialis or to another species of the complex.

Propagation

Like all Zamia, the species has male and female plants and is propagated from seed; a plant of each sex is needed to obtain seeds in cultivation. No germination data specific to the species have been published. Given the risk of confusion with Zamia imperialis and with possible hybrids, buy seeds of documented origin. Seeds are exempt from CITES permits; make sure they come from cultivated plants.

Pests and diseases

In the wild, Taylor and colleagues (2008) saw no visible pest or disease. For the group the species belongs to, the nursery Cycadales.eu notes that in poor conditions, too cool and too dry, plants grow little, are weak and are at risk of root rot, and that reducing watering in winter, a useful remedy for species from climates with a dry season, works poorly for those from the humid tropics (Lavaud, 2023). Where zamias grow wild, Vannini (2019) reports that caterpillars of Eumaeus butterflies can be persistent pests of new leaves, and that slugs can destroy emerging leaves in a single night, in the glasshouse as in the garden. Cultivated Zamia can also host scale insects.

Cold hardiness

There is no measurement of cold tolerance for Zamia skinneri. The species lives in lowland tropical forest with fairly regular rain all year, and the nursery Cycadales.eu gives for its group a night temperature of 22 °C (71.6 °F), with brief dips to about 15 °C (59 °F) tolerated (Lavaud, 2023).

Mediterranean and European experience

No result of in-ground cultivation has been published in France, and no dated frost episode concerns the species. Simon Lavaud notes that in Montpellier, even in July and August, night-time minima are around 19 °C (66.2 °F), at the limit for the species of this group, and that the air there is much drier than in their habitat (Lavaud, 2023). On the Fous de palmiers forum, the member quoted above considered planting out “complicated” in December 2014, as these species hate cold. Even in a Mediterranean climate, then, Zamia skinneri is a plant for a warm glasshouse or a heated room all year. The Jardin zoologique tropical at La Londe-les-Maures, in the Var, has published no observation of this species.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No cold record has been published for the species, on forums or in botanic gardens. The World List of Cycads cites an article by G. Holzman (2009) on growing the Zamia skinneri complex in Hawaii, which has not been seen.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity study specific to Zamia skinneri has been published. The risk is, however, well documented in cycads: Zamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, and for Zamia furfuracea the ASPCA cites cycasin and BMAA, a neurotoxic amino acid, with vomiting, bloody diarrhoea and liver damage in dogs, cats and horses; the seeds are the most dangerous part. In the absence of evidence to the contrary, treat the plant as poisonous: keep seeds and leaves out of reach of children and animals, and wear gloves to clean seeds. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet straight away.

Traditional uses

No use specific to Zamia skinneri is documented.

Frequently asked questions

Are the new leaves of Zamia skinneri red?

No. They emerge bright green and hairless. The large Panamanian Zamia with red new leaves, long sold as Zamia skinneri, are now Zamia imperialis (Taylor and colleagues, 2008).

Where does it grow?

Only in Panama, in the province of Bocas del Toro, on the mainland Caribbean coast, from sea level to 400 m, in humid tropical forest. Neighbouring populations in south-eastern Costa Rica may belong to it, but this has not been confirmed.

How big does it get?

The trunk exceeds 2.4 m, the leaves reach 2.3 m and the middle leaflets up to 51 by 16.6 cm. In some populations, however, the trunks stay small.

How can I tell it from Zamia neurophyllidia?

By the leaflets. Both species have green new leaves, but Zamia skinneri has longer middle leaflets (25.5 to 51.4 cm), narrower in proportion, no more than 10 pairs, and a petiole as long as the rachis. The chromosome number, often quoted, does not separate them: no count concerns Zamia skinneri in its present sense.

Is it endangered?

Yes. The IUCN rates it Endangered (2022), for a population decline linked to habitat loss and exploitation, although it was still considered common in 2008. It falls under CITES Appendix II.

Can I grow it in the UK?

Only in warmth: a heated glasshouse or a warm room all year, with high humidity. No in-ground cultivation is documented outside the tropics, and even a Montpellier summer is considered a little cool at night for its group.

Is it poisonous?

It must be assumed so. No analysis exists for the species, but the cycads studied contain serious toxins, especially in their seeds.
