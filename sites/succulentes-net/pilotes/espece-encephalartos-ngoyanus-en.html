Encephalartos ngoyanus is a small cycad with an almost entirely underground stem, growing on rocky grassy slopes and forest margins in northern KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa, and in neighbouring Eswatini and southern Mozambique. This species of the genus Encephalartos was described in 1949 by Inez Verdoorn from plants found near the Ngoye Forest in Zululand, which gave it its name. Some collections of the plant had until then been placed in another dwarf species, Encephalartos caffer, now spelt Encephalartos afer. It usually carries only a few soft, dark green leaves around 60 cm to 1.25 m long, and is assessed as Vulnerable by the IUCN (2022). It is rarely grown, and its cold hardiness is undocumented beyond a tolerance of light frost.

Key facts about Encephalartos ngoyanus

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos ngoyanus I.Verd. (1949), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15), IPNI, GBIF and Species+
  • Former names and synonyms: no synonym; some collections were formerly filed under Encephalartos caffer (now Encephalartos afer)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Ngoye dwarf cycad (PlantZAfrica, Species+), Ngoye cycad (Species+)
  • Natural range: South Africa (northern KwaZulu-Natal, from the Ngoye Forest to Mkhuze, Ubombo and Jozini), Eswatini, Mozambique (Maputo province)
  • IUCN status: Vulnerable (VU B1ab(iii,v); C2a(i)), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I since 1977, like the whole genus; Annex A in the European Union, with a positive EU opinion for artificially propagated plants from South Africa up to 7 cm of caudex and a negative opinion above that size
  • Documented hardiness: no measured value; “will tolerate light frost” according to PlantZAfrica (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, rocky slopes in open grassland and forest margins; in cultivation, dappled light and a well-drained soil, without overwatering (PlantZAfrica)
  • Habit and adult size: stem underground or emerging by about 20 cm, about 20 cm in diameter; three to seven leaves of about 60 cm according to the original description, five to ten leaves of 0.6 to 1.25 m according to PlantZAfrica
  • In the UK, US and Australia: declared by the Huntington, Lotusland, the National Tropical Botanical Garden and the University of Hawaii at Hilo (BGCI); no British or Australian garden declares it
  • Toxicity: not studied for the species; documented risk in the genus (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos ngoyanus

Look for a small crown of slender, soft leaves with a thin, woolly petiole and rachis, rising from a stem that barely shows above the ground. Verdoorn's original description (1949) gives the following characters:

  • Stem: mainly underground, sometimes with a dome of about 20 cm visible, about 20 cm in diameter at ground level; roots fine
  • Leaves: three to seven, about 60 cm long (up to 120 cm in shade); petiole 10 to 30 cm long; petiole and rachis covered with silky, off-white woolly hairs that are gradually shed
  • Leaflets: linear-lanceolate, 7 to 8 cm by 9 to 11 mm (up to 12 cm by 1.5 cm in shade), inserted in an open V, with a pungent tip, finely hairy then hairless, often with one to three teeth on the lower margin; towards the base of the leaf they become gradually smaller, rarely down to mere prickles
  • Male cone: roughly cylindrical, about 20 cm by 4 to 5 cm
  • Female cone: pale olive green becoming yellow when ripe, shortly stalked, about 23 cm by 10 cm
  • Seeds: scarlet, about 2.7 by 2 cm

The PlantZAfrica fact sheet (Lungisani Zondi, 2021) adds that the stem, usually single, can reach 30 cm long and 20 cm in diameter, that plants with up to five stems have been recorded, and that strong contractile roots pull the stem back into the ground, except in rock crevices, where the stem is forced above the rock and then produces rare suckers. Its measurements partly differ from Verdoorn's: five to ten leaves of 0.6 to 1.25 m, dark green above and paler beneath, leaflets 7 to 12 cm by 9 to 13 mm with entire margins, the basal ones reduced to one or two spines, one cone per plant in both sexes, light green becoming yellow, 20 to 25 cm by 10 to 12 cm, the male egg-shaped and the female pear-shaped, and bright red seeds of 29 by 18 to 20 mm. Jungle Music, a nursery in southern California, describes plants with eight to ten leaves of up to about four feet (1.2 m), leaflets bright shiny green when emerging, later dull dark or olive green, and leaflets and petiole unarmed and soft to the touch. Leaf number and size clearly vary with age, exposure and source; the toothed lower margin of the leaflets noted in the protologue is not mentioned by the later sources.

Hybrids

No hybrid of Encephalartos ngoyanus is described. In collections, where different species cone side by side and hand pollination is common, seed of unknown origin may nevertheless be hybrid.

Possible confusion

The closest species is Encephalartos afer, long spelt caffer, another dwarf cycad with an underground stem, now restricted to the Eastern Cape. Verdoorn (1949) gives the differences: in Encephalartos ngoyanus the petiole and rachis are slender, the leaflets are much less crowded, do not spread in different planes, are less narrowed at the base, are not twisted and are often toothed on the lower margin; its roots are fine, whereas those of the Cape species are very thick. The cone scales also have a less prominent face.

PlantZAfrica, which calls the two species closely related and often confused, adds practical characters. In Encephalartos ngoyanus the lower half of the leaf often curves upwards while the upper half is more or less straight; the upper side of the leaflets is soft and glossy, and the underside feels less smooth when stroked gently with the fingers. In Encephalartos afer the leaves are straight or curve gradually downwards, and the leaflets are leathery, dull green, often irregularly arranged on the rachis, very crowded towards the tip, and equally smooth on both sides. Jungle Music finds the two similar in stature and appearance, Encephalartos ngoyanus being harder to grow and less full in most climates. The distance between the two ranges also helps to identify plants of known origin.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos ngoyanus is accepted by The World List of Cycads, IPNI, GBIF and Species+, with no synonym. It was described by Inez Clare Verdoorn in January 1949 in The Flowering Plants of Africa (volume 27, plates 1053 and 1054). The female type is Verdoorn and Christian 716, collected at Ngoye “on grassy slopes near and among boulders”; number 716b serves as the male type. The World List of Cycads gives a probable holotype at the National Herbarium in Pretoria (PRE).

Verdoorn explains the background to her description. In 1945 M.R. Henderson had suggested, in the Journal of South African Botany, that the dwarf cycad of Zululand was distinct from Encephalartos caffer and deserved to be described as a new species. An expedition in 1947 to study Encephalartos in the wild found the plant in the Ngoye mountains and at Ingwavuma. Verdoorn confirmed Henderson's view, relying on the differences in leaves and roots and on the geographical separation. Two specimens in Hutchinson and Rattray's Flora Capensis filed under caffer in fact belong to this species, and Verdoorn henceforth regarded caffer as restricted to the eastern Cape Province.

The name Encephalartos caffer has itself been corrected: The World List of Cycads and IPNI now write Encephalartos afer, under Article 61.6 of the International Code of Nomenclature (the Madrid Code). For the reader, a Zululand plant labelled “caffer” in older books may therefore be Encephalartos ngoyanus.

Etymology and history

The epithet refers to the type locality, the Ngoye Forest in north-eastern Natal (now KwaZulu-Natal), according to Haynes (2022), quoted by The World List of Cycads. PlantZAfrica describes Ngoye as a coastal scarp forest between Mthunzini and Empangeni.

The Ngoye Forest is also famous among cycad enthusiasts for Encephalartos woodii: the only plant of that species ever found, a male, was spotted there in 1895 by John Medley Wood, and its last stem was removed around 1916 (Palmer and Pitman, 1972). PlantZAfrica, which calls Ngoye the original habitat of Encephalartos woodii, adds that another cycad, Stangeria eriopus, grows in the same forest. The link is purely geographical: no published source relates Encephalartos ngoyanus botanically to Encephalartos woodii. In her description Verdoorn thanks the forestry service at Ngoye and a resident of Ingwavuma who had helped the 1947 expedition. A paper devoted to the species appeared in 1990 in the journal of the Cycad Society of South Africa (Giddy, Encephalartos 23).

Common names

PlantZAfrica gives the English name Ngoye dwarf cycad; Species+ gives Ngoye cycad and Ngoye dwarf cycad. No name specific to the species is attested in Zulu: according to PlantZAfrica, most cycads are called isqgiki–somkhovu in that language, a name tied to beliefs about witchcraft. No other English name is attested in a reliable source.

In the wild

Encephalartos ngoyanus occurs in South Africa (KwaZulu-Natal), Eswatini and Mozambique, according to The World List of Cycads; in Mozambique, the study of the country's endemic plants (Darbyshire et al., 2019) records it in Maputo province. Verdoorn (1949) saw it at Ngoye, on grassy slopes near and among boulders, and at Ingwavuma, on a steep slope above a stream; she added that Galpin's collections from the Ubombo mountains and Mkuzi might belong to the species.

According to PlantZAfrica, it ranges from the Ngoye Forest to the surroundings of Mkhuze and Ubombo, the Jozini Dam and both sides of the border between KwaZulu-Natal and Eswatini. It grows on rocky slopes in open grassland and along forest margins, with an annual rainfall of 750 to 1,000 mm. Grass fires are common in this habitat: fire plays an important part in stimulating coning, but too frequent fires have become a threat. Altitude and soil type are not documented.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos ngoyanus is assessed as Vulnerable (VU) on the IUCN Red List under criteria B1ab(iii,v); C2a(i), in the assessment by J.D. Bösenberg published in 2022 and cited by The World List of Cycads; GBIF gives the same category. These criteria reflect a restricted range, a decline in habitat quality and in the number of mature individuals, and a small population. The PlantZAfrica fact sheet, quoting the Red List of South African Plants (Vulnerable), puts the wild population at fewer than 5,000 mature individuals, with several subpopulations declining. The threats are the collection of wild plants for private collections, overgrazing and too frequent veld fires, made worse by slow growth and infrequent reproduction in nature. Past decline is suspected to exceed 20%, and future decline is likely to exceed 10% over the next generation (30 years).

In South Africa, the collection of wild cycads has been banned nationally since 2007, then under Government Notice 371 of 2012. An Italian grower reported in 2013 on the Tropicamente forum that exports of a few species, including Encephalartos ngoyanus, were limited to plants with a caudex under 7 cm; this matches the EU opinions below.

Regulation, according to Species+ (consulted through its public API on 26 October 2026):

  • CITES: Appendix I since 4 February 1977, like the whole genus;
  • European Union: Annex A, current list in Regulation (EU) 2026/1383; for artificially propagated plants from South Africa (source codes A and D), a positive opinion of the EU Scientific Review Group for plants with a caudex of up to 7 cm in diameter (20 June 2011) and a negative opinion above that size (7 February 2013), both current; a new consultation covering the whole genus has been open since 16 September 2026; past suspensions of trade in wild specimens from Mozambique (2006–2017) are no longer in force.

Artificially propagated plants can be traded under these conditions, with the required permits. These notes do not replace the official texts: check Species+ and the national authorities before any purchase or import, and ask for proof that a plant was propagated in cultivation.

Growing Encephalartos ngoyanus

Encephalartos ngoyanus is a slow-growing dwarf cycad for shade or dappled light, and it is rarely grown. PlantZAfrica notes that it is hardly used in cultivation, but considers it a fine addition to most gardens, easy to fit into small spaces, especially as an understorey plant, and lists it as a good pot plant. It grows best in dappled light, in a well-drained soil; it is sensitive to overwatering. Jungle Music, for southern California, recommends filtered light or shade on the coast and inland, and shade in desert areas, a free-draining soil and avoiding wet conditions, especially in winter; it notes that the species takes decades to reach any size and is harder to grow than Encephalartos afer.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

Outdoors, the species suits a shaded or half-shaded rock garden or a woodland edge, in a gritty, free-draining soil where water never lies. Its small size makes it safe along paths, as Jungle Music points out, since the leaflets are soft. In cold areas, PlantZAfrica observes that plants often shed their leaves in autumn and should then be kept dry; this behaviour, which follows from its sensitivity to wet cold, is the main limit to planting out in cool, rainy climates.

Container cultivation

In climates with frost or wet winters, a pot is the safest choice: a deep container, to suit the underground stem and contractile roots, filled with a mineral, free-draining mix, kept in light shade in summer and almost dry and frost-free in winter. In France, a grower in Agen (south-west France) mentioned on the Fous de palmiers forum in November 2008 a few seeds of Encephalartos ngoyanus he was germinating, and a grower in Belley (Ain) and Montpellier reported in September 2012 that a young plant had produced only one poor leaf during the season. The Orto Botanico in Naples grew one plant of South African or Swazi origin in 1993–1994 (Gaudio et al., 1997). No other European growing results are documented.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

The species is rare in collections. BGCI PlantSearch lists eleven institutions holding it, four of them in the United States: the Huntington (San Marino, California), Ganna Walska Lotusland (Santa Barbara, California), the National Tropical Botanical Garden (Kalāheo, Hawaii) and the botanical garden of the University of Hawaii at Hilo. No British or Australian garden declares it, and the Montgomery Botanical Center's online catalogue lists no accession. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not a living plant on display today. Jungle Music usually has only a few plants available and states that seed is not available.

Propagation

Encephalartos ngoyanus is propagated mainly from seed, since it rarely produces suckers. PlantZAfrica advises sowing in river sand on a heated bench at 24 to 28 °C (75 to 82 °F): germination should start about three weeks after sowing, more slowly without bottom heat, and seedlings at the one-leaf stage are susceptible to damping-off. Seed has circulated among European amateurs, according to the Fous de palmiers forum (2008). The species is dioecious, so a male and a female coning at the same time are needed; in cultivation pollination is usually done by hand. Seeds, like plants, are covered by CITES Appendix I.

Pests and diseases

No disease or pest specific to Encephalartos ngoyanus has been described. Like other cycads, it is most at risk from rot of the roots and stem in a waterlogged substrate, a risk that PlantZAfrica's warning about overwatering confirms, and its seedlings are susceptible to damping-off.

Cold hardiness

No cold-hardiness value has been published for Encephalartos ngoyanus. PlantZAfrica states only that it “will tolerate light frost” and that in cold areas plants often shed their leaves in autumn; no temperature, frost duration or plant age is given. The underground stem may benefit from the thermal inertia of the soil, as in other dwarf Encephalartos, but no trial documents this for the species. As with all Encephalartos, survivals in cold areas often concern young plants with an underground caudex, protected by the soil and sometimes by snow; this protection does not make the leaves frost-proof.

Mediterranean and European experience

No outdoor trial has been published in Europe. The French reports concern seedlings and a young plant grown in containers, without temperatures. For growers in Mediterranean climates, or in comparable climates such as coastal California or southern Australia, planting out can only be considered as an experiment in the mildest sites, in light shade, with perfect drainage and a dry winter.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No measured value has been published for these countries, and the American gardens that hold the species (California and Hawaii) give no hardiness data. No USDA zone is documented for the species in a reliable source.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data have been published for Encephalartos ngoyanus, but the risk is documented in the genus: the toxicity of Encephalartos horridus was demonstrated as early as 1944 (Van der Walt), and Encephalartos contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; these substances affect the liver, the digestive tract and the nervous system, and the seeds are the most dangerous part. For another cycad, the sago palm (Cycas revoluta), the ASPCA reports vomiting, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses. Wear gloves to handle seeds and their fleshy coat, pick up fallen seeds and keep plants and seeds out of reach of children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use specific to Encephalartos ngoyanus is documented. PlantZAfrica reports that in Zulu culture cycads in general are planted in front of the homestead gate to protect it from evil spirits.

Frequently asked questions

Where does Encephalartos ngoyanus grow in the wild?

In northern KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa, from the Ngoye Forest to the Jozini area, and in Eswatini and southern Mozambique. It grows on rocky slopes in open grassland and at forest margins.

Where does its name come from?

From the Ngoye Forest in Zululand, where the type plants were collected on grassy slopes near the forest. The same forest is the only place where Encephalartos woodii was ever found.

How can it be told from Encephalartos afer (caffer)?

By the leaves and roots. Encephalartos ngoyanus has a slender petiole, well-spaced, soft, glossy leaflets that are not twisted, and fine roots; the Cape species has crowded, leathery, dull leaflets and very thick roots.

Does it have a trunk?

Not really. Its stem is mostly underground, held there by contractile roots; at most a dome of about 20 cm shows above the soil, except in rock crevices.

Is it endangered?

It is Vulnerable (IUCN, 2022), with fewer than 5,000 mature plants in the wild according to PlantZAfrica. Collecting, overgrazing and too frequent fires are the main threats.

Is it frost-hardy?

Only to light frost, according to PlantZAfrica, and it dislikes wet cold. No outdoor trial in a cold climate has been published, so in frost-prone areas it is best kept in a pot, dry and frost-free in winter.

Is it poisonous?

Very probably. The species has not been studied, but Encephalartos are poisonous, especially their seeds.
