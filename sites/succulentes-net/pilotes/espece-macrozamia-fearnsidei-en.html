Macrozamia fearnsidei is a cycad endemic to the interior of central Queensland, in eastern Australia, where it grows in open eucalypt forest on the sandstone escarpments of the Expedition Range and nearby ranges, north and east of Injune. This species of the genus Macrozamia forms, from an underground trunk, a shrubby crown of erect, spirally twisted leaves with very long, glossy, dark green leaflets. David Jones described it in 1991 and named it after the grazier who looked after the cycads on his property, Wallaroo Station. It was listed as Vulnerable under Australian federal law from 1997 to 2013, and the IUCN now lists it as Least Concern.

Key facts about Macrozamia fearnsidei

  • Accepted name: Macrozamia fearnsidei D.L.Jones (1991), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and the Australian Plant Census
  • Former names and synonyms: none; before 1991, these plants were placed in the Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi group
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Fearnside's cycad (Flora of Australia)
  • Natural range: Australia, central Queensland: Expedition Range and the ranges north and east of Injune, Taroom district
  • IUCN status: Least Concern (LC), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated August 2020 and published in 2022; “special least concern” under Queensland law
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II, like the whole genus; Annex B in the European Union; listed as Vulnerable under federal law from 1997 to 2013 (EPBC Act from 2000)
  • Documented hardiness: no cultivation report (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, open eucalypt forest, gullies and rocky slopes, sandy soil over sandstone; in cultivation, not documented
  • Habit and adult size: underground trunk 15 to 35 cm across; 2 to 20 leaves 70 to 140 cm long, depending on the source
  • In the UK, US and Australia: RHS Garden Wisley declares it to BGCI; the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney holds a wild-collected accession of 2022; no American garden lists it
  • Toxicity: leaves toxic to cattle, especially the regrowth after fire, according to the owner of Wallaroo Station (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Macrozamia fearnsidei

It is recognised by its shrubby crown of erect leaves, by a leaf axis twisted one to five times, and by its very long leaflets, up to 60 cm, dark green and glossy above, deeply channelled and drooping in their last third. David Jones's original description (1991) gives the following characters:

  • Trunk: underground, roughly ovoid, 15 to 35 cm across;
  • Leaves: 5 to 20 in an erect, very bushy crown, rarely almost lying flat, 70 to 140 cm long, dark green and shiny; petiole 15 to 40 cm; axis spirally twisted one to five times, with cream patches between the bases of the upper leaflets;
  • Leaflets: 55 to 120 per leaf, linear, 20 to 60 cm long and 6 to 11 mm wide, glossy dark green above, paler beneath with 9 to 12 raised veins, strongly channelled and themselves twisted one to four times; tip slightly pungent; basal callus cream to pale yellow; the lowest leaflets are barely reduced;
  • Male cones: roughly cylindrical, 15 to 27 cm long and 4.5 to 6.5 cm wide, often curved with age, on a stalk 18 to 30 cm long; most scales carry only a rudimentary spine;
  • Female cones: roughly ovoid, erect, 12 to 18 cm long and 8 to 10 cm wide, on a stalk 18 to 27 cm long; spines up to 2 cm towards the tip;
  • Seeds: 2.3 to 2.7 cm long and 1.9 to 2.3 cm wide, with orange to scarlet flesh.

The Flora of Australia (Hill, 1998) gives somewhat different figures: 2 to 12 leaves, 70 to 140 cm long, mid to dark green; leaflets 20 to 60 cm long and 5 to 9 mm wide, with incurved margins; seeds 23 to 27 mm long with red flesh. Both sources agree on the strongly spirally twisted leaf axis and on the lowest leaflets, which are not reduced to spines.

Hybrids

A natural hybrid with Macrozamia moorei is recorded in WildNet, the Queensland Government's wildlife database, as Macrozamia fearnsidei × Macrozamia moorei, from a single locality. David Jones already noted in 1991 that the species sometimes grows alongside Macrozamia moorei. No description of the hybrid has been published.

Possible confusion

According to David Jones (1991), Macrozamia fearnsidei belongs to the group of species around Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi, a species of the Queensland coast. It differs in its much more robust plants, with up to 20 leaves (2 to 5 in Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi), much longer and broader leaflets that are strongly channelled and glossy, and larger cones. In his key to the Queensland species, Jones separates it from Macrozamia plurinervia by its longer leaflets (up to 60 cm, against 25 cm), which are glossy and thin, and by its green cones, those of Macrozamia plurinervia being glaucous. Macrozamia platyrhachis, the nearest related species geographically, differs in its almost prostrate leaves with a barely twisted axis.

The Flora of Australia, on the other hand, sets it beside Macrozamia crassifolia, whose leaflets are shorter and narrower. The two sources therefore do not name the same species as its closest relative.

Macrozamia moorei, which sometimes shares its habitat, can hardly be confused with it once adult: it is a large species with an above-ground trunk, whose leaflets, reduced to spines, run almost down to the base of the leaf (Jones, 1991).

Taxonomy

Macrozamia fearnsidei D.L.Jones was published on 27 September 1991 in the journal Austrobaileya (volume 3, part 3, page 481), in a paper that also described Macrozamia lomandroides, raised Macrozamia plurinervia to species rank and reinstated Macrozamia mountperriensis. It is accepted by The World List of Cycads and the Australian Plant Census, has no synonym and belongs to the family Zamiaceae.

Jones placed it in section Parazamia, which groups the species with an underground trunk and lowest leaflets not reduced to spines. He explained that he had studied in the field the group of Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi, which L.A.S. Johnson had divided into three subspecies in 1959, and found that its populations are isolated, tied to particular habitats and have characters of their own. This revision led to the recognition of several distinct species, Macrozamia fearnsidei among them. For the reader, this means that plants from the Injune district labelled as a form of Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi before 1991 may belong to this species. The World List of Cycads does not use sections.

Etymology and history

The epithet honours Geoff Fearnside, owner of Wallaroo Station in Queensland. David Jones named the species “in recognition of his successful combination of cattle farming with conservation practices on the property” (Jones, 1991); The World List of Cycads, following Haynes (2022), refers to his efforts to conserve the cycads growing there. Jones also thanked him for allowing the plants to be studied. The genus name joins the Greek makros, “large”, to the genus name Zamia.

The type specimen was collected on 26 August 1990 by David and Barbara Jones at Wallaroo Station, about 64 km north of Injune, in the Leichhardt district; the protologue gives the holotype as held in Canberra, with duplicates in Brisbane, Canberra and Sydney. The plant was known before its description: the Flora of Australia cites collections made in 1978 near Taroom and Injune.

Common names

The Flora of Australia gives the English name “Fearnside's cycad”, formed from the grazier's name; it is a flora name rather than a name in everyday use. No Aboriginal name is documented.

In the wild

Macrozamia fearnsidei lives in the sandstone ranges of inland central Queensland. David Jones (1991) described it as locally common on the escarpments of the Expedition Range, north of Injune, and probably also in the neighbouring ranges. It grows in sandy soil, in open forest of tall eucalypts, either in gullies near seasonal watercourses or on rocky slopes, sometimes with Macrozamia moorei. The Flora of Australia presents it as a widely scattered species in the ranges north and east of Injune, on sandy soil over sandstone. The localities recorded in WildNet span about 55 km from north to south and 135 km from west to east.

Cones mature in September and October, and seeds ripen from February to April (Jones, 1991). No pollination study specific to the species has been published; in Macrozamia, pollination is carried out by specialist insects (see the genus page).

No climate data has been published for the habitat of the species.

Conservation status and regulation

Macrozamia fearnsidei is not threatened at the global level: the IUCN Red List classes it as Least Concern (LC), in the assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated 13 August 2020 and published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, GBIF and Crossref, checked on 26 October 2026).

Its Australian status has changed over time. According to WildNet, it was listed as Vulnerable under the federal Endangered Species Protection Act 1992 (from 1997 to 2000), then under the Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act 1999 (EPBC Act), from 16 July 2000 until 15 May 2013, when it was removed from the list. In Queensland, it is now classed as “special least concern” under the Nature Conservation Act 1992, and WildNet flags it as a sensitive species, which restricts the publication of its localities. In 1991, Jones noted that the species, although locally common, had no protected land.

Like all species of the genus, it is listed in CITES Appendix II and, in the European Union, in Annex B of the wildlife trade regulations (Species+, checked on 26 October 2026). International trade in plants and seeds requires permits. Buy only plants raised in cultivation, with their documents, and check the regulations in force before any purchase or import.

Growing Macrozamia fearnsidei

No published source describes the cultivation of Macrozamia fearnsidei, in Australia or elsewhere, and no grower has published a result. Its natural habitat gives only a few pointers, which remain assumptions.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Soil: sand over sandstone in the wild; a very free-draining, sandy mix appears essential.
  • Light: open eucalypt forest; a bright position with a little shade in the hottest hours seems closest to its habitat.
  • Water: not documented for the species. The habitat includes gullies near seasonal watercourses as well as drier rocky slopes.
  • Space: the leaves reach 1.4 m and the leaflets 60 cm; the plant takes up more room than most Macrozamia with an underground trunk.
  • Growth: not documented for the species.

No observation of the Jardin zoologique tropical in southern France has been published for this species.

Container cultivation

No container-growing report has been published. A deep pot suits the taproot of cycads, and a very free-draining sandy mix matches the soils of its habitat; the long, drooping leaflets need room around the pot.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

  • United Kingdom: RHS Garden Wisley, in Surrey, declares the species to BGCI. No published source describes the plant or says whether it is grown outdoors or under glass.
  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney lists in its Garden Explorer (updated on 1 October 2026) an accession of 2022 (S2022-0083) of wild origin; the Australian National Botanic Gardens in Canberra also declare it to BGCI.
  • United States: no American garden declares it to BGCI, and the Montgomery Botanical Center does not list it in its online catalogue.

Six institutions in all declare it to BGCI; the other three are in Belgium (Meise Botanic Garden), China (Fairy Lake Botanical Garden, Shenzhen) and Singapore (Singapore Botanic Gardens). A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not necessarily a living plant on display today. The species does not appear in the census of Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria. No English-language grower's report has been found.

Propagation

Propagation is by seed. The plants being male or female, both sexes and, in cultivation, hand pollination are needed to obtain fertile seed. In the wild, seeds ripen from February to April (Jones, 1991). No germination data specific to the species has been published, and no source mentions offsets.

Pests and diseases

No problem specific to the species is documented in reliable sources. In Macrozamia grown in Europe, growers' forums mainly mention scale insects and rot in cold, wet soil (see the genus page).

Cold hardiness

No cultivation report exists for Macrozamia fearnsidei, and no frost data has been published for its habitat.

Mediterranean and European experience

No planting in the ground is documented in Europe, and no recorded cold spell concerns the species. In 2014, on the French Fous de palmiers forum, a grower from the Ain and Hérault departments reported the opinion of an Australian nurseryman specialising in cycads, who placed Macrozamia fearnsidei in the group of “less hardy” species of inland Queensland, after the inland and coastal species of New South Wales. This ranking gives no temperature.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No cultivation result has been published from Britain, North America or Australia, and no source gives a USDA zone for the species. Any hardiness figure would be a guess, and this page gives none; until results are published, the cautious course is a pot kept frost-free in winter.

Toxicity and precautions

Macrozamia fearnsidei is toxic to livestock. David Jones (1991) reports, on the authority of Geoff Fearnside, that its leaves, and especially the young growth that appears after a fire, are very toxic to cattle, as are those of most species of the genus. Macrozamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a toxin of the liver; eating the leaves causes irreversible paralysis of the hindquarters in cattle (McKenzie, 2006; see the genus page). No chemical analysis specific to the species has been published. Wear gloves to handle seeds, keep them out of reach of children, dogs and livestock, and contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay in case of ingestion.

Traditional uses

No use specific to this species is documented. Other Macrozamia provided Aboriginal peoples with edible seeds after lengthy treatment (see the genus page).

Frequently asked questions

Who was Fearnside?

Geoff Fearnside, owner of Wallaroo Station in Queensland. David Jones named the species after him in 1991 for combining cattle grazing with the conservation of the cycads on his property.

Is it endangered?

No, not any more. It was listed as Vulnerable under Australian federal law from 1997 to 2013, then removed from the list; the IUCN classes it as Least Concern (2022).

What sets it apart from other small Macrozamia?

Its leaflets. They reach 60 cm, are dark green, glossy and deeply channelled, and droop in their last third, on erect leaves with a spirally twisted axis.

Does it hybridise with Macrozamia moorei?

Yes. A natural hybrid is recorded in Queensland. The two species sometimes grow together, but the hybrid has not been described in detail.

Is it poisonous to cattle?

Yes. According to the owner of Wallaroo Station, quoted by David Jones, its leaves are very toxic to cattle, especially the regrowth after a fire.

Can it be grown outdoors in Britain or the United States?

Nobody knows. No grower has published a result, and an expert ranking places it among the least hardy Macrozamia; a pot kept frost-free in winter is the only prudent option.
