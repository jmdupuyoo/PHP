The genus Macrozamia brings together 41 cycads, all endemic to Australia, that grow mainly in the open forests and eucalypt woodlands of Queensland and New South Wales, with a few isolated species in Western Australia and in the MacDonnell Ranges, in the heart of the continent. Some never get beyond a tuft of leaves rising from an underground stem; others, such as Macrozamia moorei, build trunks several metres tall. Several species were first described in other genera, so old books still use names such as Zamia spiralis, Encephalartos spiralis, Encephalartos miquelii or Cycas riedlei, and Macrozamia tridentata turns up in older literature. In Australia these plants, known as burrawangs or zamia palms, are notorious for the toxicity of their seeds and young leaves, the cause of “zamia staggers” in cattle, and famous too for the seeds that Aboriginal people made edible through long processing.

Key facts about Macrozamia

  • Accepted name: Macrozamia Miq., published in January 1842 in Miquel's Monographia Cycadearum (IPNI); the type species is disputed: Macrozamia spiralis according to Hutchinson (1924), Macrozamia riedlei according to Johnson (1959) (see Taxonomy)
  • Number of species: 41, with no accepted subspecies or varieties (The World List of Cycads, version 2026.08.15; Species+ also counts 41). POWO could not be consulted directly
  • Former names: species first published in Zamia (Zamia spiralis Salisb., 1796), Cycas (Cycas riedlei Fisch. ex Gaudich., 1829) or Encephalartos (Encephalartos spiralis, Encephalartos miquelii, Encephalartos dyeri, Encephalartos macdonnellii, Encephalartos moorei); Macrozamia tridentata (Willd.) Regel, a name of uncertain application; conversely, Macrozamia peroffskyana, Macrozamia denisonii and Macrozamia hopei now refer to species of Lepidozamia
  • Family: Zamiaceae; closest genus: Lepidozamia
  • Common names: “burrawang” and “zamia palm” in Australian English; “by-yu” among the Noongar of Western Australia, for the seed of Macrozamia riedlei with its flesh
  • Natural range: endemic to Australia. 19 species in Queensland, 16 in New South Wales, 2 shared by these two states, 3 in Western Australia and 1 in the Northern Territory. None in South Australia
  • IUCN status: all 41 species were assessed in 2022: 1 Critically Endangered (CR), 9 Endangered (EN), 7 Vulnerable (VU), 6 Near Threatened (NT) and 18 Least Concern (LC), according to the data shown by The World List of Cycads
  • CITES: Appendix II since 4 February 1977, with annotation #4 (seeds, pollen and in vitro cultures exempt); Annex B in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: amateur observations only, mostly from France. Macrozamia communis loses its leaves at about −7 to −9 °C (19.4 to 15.8 °F) and then regrows (Roussillon, Montpellier); the foliage of Macrozamia moorei was damaged from −3 or −4 °C (26.6 or 24.8 °F) for one grower in the Gironde, but stayed intact at −4 °C in Roussillon; Macrozamia johnsonii turned yellow under fleece at −8 to −10 °C (17.6 to 14 °F) near Nantes in 2012, then produced new leaves. Indicative values (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: full sun to partial shade depending on the species; very free-draining soil, preferably poor and sandy
  • Habit and size: from an underground stem barely visible above the soil to trunks of 6.5 m (about 21 ft) in Macrozamia dyeri and 8 m (about 26 ft) in Macrozamia moorei, the two largest species (Jones, 1993)
  • Toxicity: seeds and young leaves highly toxic to people and livestock (see Toxicity and precautions)

Characteristics of the genus Macrozamia

A Macrozamia is recognised by its pinnate leaves arranged in a crown, whose leaflets carry at their base a small gland, often paler or coloured, and by its cones, whose scales end in an upturned spine. The description below follows The World List of Cycads.

  • Habit: dioecious, palm-like shrubs with a thick, cylindrical stem, above or below ground, covered with the bases of old leaves. Many species have only an underground trunk; a few, including Macrozamia moorei, form true trunks.
  • Leaves: pinnate, spirally arranged and interspersed with cataphylls, small leaves reduced to scales. The lowest leaflets are often reduced to spines. Young leaves are covered with transparent hairs, simple or branched. In some species the leaf axis is twisted, at least in young plants, as the name Macrozamia spiralis suggests.
  • Leaflets: simple or forked (dichotomous), with many parallel veins and no midrib, inserted near the edges of the upper side of the axis. Forked leaflets gave their names to Macrozamia diplomera, Macrozamia heteromera and Macrozamia polymorpha.
  • Cones: each plant is male or female. The cones are borne on a stalk; their scales often end in an upturned spine. Each female scale carries two ovules, rarely three.
  • Seeds: globose to ellipsoid, surrounded by a fleshy layer (sarcotesta), usually red, more rarely yellow, orange or brown.
  • Roots: as in all cycads, coralloid roots house cyanobacteria that fix nitrogen from the air.

Macrozamia, Lepidozamia or Cycas?

Young Macrozamia are often mistaken for a Cycas revoluta or a Lepidozamia. In Cycas, each leaflet has a clearly visible midrib, whereas Macrozamia leaflets have only parallel veins; Cycas seeds hang in a ring below the leaves, while those of Macrozamia develop in cones. In Lepidozamia, the leaflets arise from the top of the leaf axis and have no coloured gland at their base. Telling one Macrozamia from another is much harder: growers on the French forum Fous de palmiers point out that many plants sold as Macrozamia communis are in fact Macrozamia moorei or Macrozamia mountperriensis, and vice versa. One often-quoted pointer: in Macrozamia communis the leaflets spread almost flat on either side of the axis; in Macrozamia moorei they form a V and the leaf is stiffer.

Taxonomy and classification

Macrozamia is an accepted genus of the family Zamiaceae, with 41 species in version 2026.08.15 of The World List of Cycads; Species+, the official CITES database, gives the same number. The Dutch botanist Friedrich Anton Wilhelm Miquel created the genus in 1842. His Macrozamia spiralis was based, for its name, on Zamia spiralis, described by Salisbury in 1796 from a collection made near Port Jackson (Sydney); but, according to Johnson (1959), Miquel drew his description almost entirely from the plates of Ferdinand Bauer, which actually show Macrozamia riedlei, from Western Australia. The type species of the genus is therefore disputed: Hutchinson (1924, cited by Johnson) had designated “Macrozamia spiralis”, but Johnson considered this choice not binding and adopted Macrozamia riedlei, the species of Bauer's plates. Later sources cite one species or the other.

In the 19th century, several species moved between Zamia, Encephalartos and Macrozamia. Lehmann published Encephalartos spiralis in 1834; Ferdinand von Mueller described several species in Encephalartos that other authors, including Alphonse de Candolle in 1868, later transferred to Macrozamia (Macrozamia miquelii, Macrozamia macdonnellii). In 1932, Julius Schuster went the other way and lumped many plants under a very broad Macrozamia tridentata, divided into subspecies, varieties and forms; these names are now synonyms or doubtful names, and The World List of Cycads lists Macrozamia tridentata among the names whose application remains uncertain.

The current classification starts with the revision by L.A.S. Johnson (1959). He recognised 14 species in two sections: section Macrozamia, larger plants whose leaflets contain mucilage canals and whose lowest leaflets are reduced to spines, and section Parazamia, smaller plants without mucilage canals, with veins raised on the underside of the leaflet and no spiny leaflets (criteria taken from a snippet of the Gymnosperm Database). In the same work Johnson described Macrozamia communis, Macrozamia lucida and Macrozamia stenomera. The number of species then nearly tripled through the work of David L. Jones, Paul I. Forster and Ken D. Hill: four species in 1991 and 1992 (including Macrozamia plurinervia, a former subspecies raised to species rank), seven species of section Parazamia from south-east Queensland in 1994 (Macrozamia conferta, Macrozamia cranei, Macrozamia crassifolia, Macrozamia machinii, Macrozamia occidua, Macrozamia parcifolia and Macrozamia viridis, all related to Macrozamia plurinervia or Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi), nine species in the Flora of Australia in 1998, then Macrozamia serpentina in 2001.

The World List of Cycads does not arrange the species by section, and no complete, verified list of the 41 species by section is documented, which is why the index on this page follows the Australian states. In practice, the names in Encephalartos or Zamia that you may come across for these Australian plants are synonyms. As for Macrozamia littoralis and Macrozamia pectinata, sometimes seen in old texts, they do not even concern Australia: they are invalid names applied to the Mexican Dioon edule.

Etymology and history of the genus

The name Macrozamia comes from the Greek makros, “large”, and the genus name Zamia, according to The World List of Cycads: Miquel saw these plants as “large Zamia”, even though many species described since are small. The species epithets tell part of the story of the genus, according to Haynes's etymological compendium (2022), as reproduced by The World List of Cycads:

  • Macrozamia riedlei honours Anselme Riedlé (1775–1801), head gardener at the Jardin des Plantes in Paris, who sailed with Nicolas Baudin's expedition to Australia and collected the type of the species there; it was described in 1829 by Charles Gaudichaud-Beaupré, as Cycas riedlei, in the account of the voyage of the Uranie.
  • Macrozamia moorei honours Charles Moore (1820–1905), first director of the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney, who studied the genus and himself described several species in 1884.
  • Macrozamia johnsonii honours L.A.S. Johnson (1925–1997), director of the same garden and author of the 1959 revision.
  • Macrozamia miquelii honours Miquel, the author of the genus; Macrozamia macdonnellii, Sir Richard MacDonnell, governor of South Australia; Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi, Prince Paul Wilhelm of Württemberg (1785–1852), patron of science and plant collector.
  • Macrozamia communis means “common”: the species forms dense stands on the coast of New South Wales. Macrozamia cardiacensis refers to Cardiac Hill, a hill so steep that botanists struggled to carry the heavy female cones down it; Macrozamia lomandroides recalls its likeness to Lomandra; Macrozamia serpentina, the serpentine soils of its type locality.

The European history of Macrozamia begins with the colony at Port Jackson: from 1788 the first settlers' accounts note that the seeds are poisonous, and in 1793 John Hunter described this “nut” that Aboriginal people knew how to prepare (Asmussen, 2011). The genus is old: according to Jones (1993), cited by the Gymnosperm Database, the oldest Macrozamia fossils date from the Oligocene of Tasmania, an island where the genus no longer grows.

Common names

In English, the Flora of New South Wales (Harden, 1990), cited by the Gymnosperm Database, gives “burrawangs” and “zamia-palms”. “Burrawang” refers above all to Macrozamia communis; cattle farmers on the New South Wales coast speak of “Burrawang staggers” for the poisoning of their stock. In Queensland, according to the state's veterinary services, “zamia” or “zamia palm” is commonly used for the native cycads, which invites confusion with the American genus Zamia. Among the Noongar of south-western Australia, “by-yu” (or “bayio”, “boyoo”) refers to the seed of Macrozamia riedlei with its flesh, according to the sources gathered by Asmussen (2011). Outside Australia, gardeners and nurseries mostly use the scientific name.

Biogeography and ecology

Macrozamia live in the subtropical and warm-temperate regions of Australia, usually on poor soils, in open forest and eucalypt woodland. Queensland (19 species) and New South Wales (16 species, plus 2 shared with Queensland) hold almost all the diversity, and many species occupy only a few ranges of hills.

  • Coast and coastal ranges of New South Wales: Macrozamia communis forms dense stands in sclerophyll forest, on sandy to loamy soils, from the Macleay valley to Bega (Harden, 1990).
  • Inland Queensland and its plateaus: Macrozamia moorei grows in colonies on hills and in rocky gorges around Carnarvon, Springsure and Emerald, in a climate where hard frosts are frequent in winter and rainfall barely exceeds 500 mm (about 20 in) a year (Jones, 1993).
  • South-west of Western Australia: Macrozamia riedlei, Macrozamia fraseri and Macrozamia dyeri, very far from the eastern species.
  • Central Australia: Macrozamia macdonnellii is the only cycad of the arid centre; it grows on sheltered slopes of the MacDonnell Ranges.

Fire is part of their environment, but its role in reproduction is debated. The archaeologist John Beaton (1982) thought that Aboriginal people used fire to trigger mass fruiting; Asmussen (2011) points out that this link is not confirmed for all species, and that an 11-year study of Macrozamia communis (Kennedy et al., 2001) found cone production to be irregular and not synchronised from one plant to another.

Pollination by thrips and weevils

Macrozamia are pollinated by specialist insects, not by wind. In 2001 Irene Terry showed that Macrozamia communis depends on two pollinators from different orders, the thrips Cycadothrips chadwicki and the weevil Tranes lyterioides: the first demonstration that a cycad is pollinated by specialists from two different insect orders. Their larvae develop in the male cones, feeding on the pollen or the cone tissue.

In 2005 Terry, Walter, Donaldson, Snow, Forster and Machin measured how effective these insects are in Macrozamia machinii (Tranes weevils) and Macrozamia lucida (thrips). When both wind and insects were kept away from the female cones, no seed formed; when only wind was excluded, seeds formed all the same. A weevil pollinates on average 26.2 ovules per cone and a thrips 2.4: it takes about 25 thrips to match one weevil.

In 2007 Terry, Walter, Moore, Roemer and Hull described in Science a “push-pull” pollination system in Macrozamia lucida. Every day the male cones heat up and give off scents (mainly β-myrcene and ocimene) so strong that they drive away the pollen-laden thrips; as the temperature falls, the emissions decrease and the thrips are drawn to the female cones. According to University of Utah press releases, known from search snippets, the male cone heats up every day between 11 a.m. and 3 p.m., to about 14 °C (25.2 °F) above the surrounding air and up to about 38 °C (100.4 °F). The authors see in this a possible intermediate stage in the evolution of animal pollination.

Regulation and conservation

More than 40 % of the species are threatened. According to the 2022 assessments of the IUCN Red List, as shown by The World List of Cycads, Macrozamia humilis is Critically Endangered (CR); nine species are Endangered (EN): Macrozamia cranei, Macrozamia elegans, Macrozamia lomandroides, Macrozamia parcifolia, Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi, Macrozamia platyrhachis, Macrozamia plurinervia, Macrozamia spiralis and Macrozamia viridis; seven are Vulnerable (VU): Macrozamia cardiacensis, Macrozamia conferta, Macrozamia crassifolia, Macrozamia johnsonii, Macrozamia machinii, Macrozamia occidua and Macrozamia secunda. Six are Near Threatened (NT), including Macrozamia moorei, and eighteen Least Concern (LC), including Macrozamia communis and Macrozamia miquelii. The Red List itself could not be consulted directly.

All species have been listed in CITES Appendix II since 4 February 1977: international trade in plants requires an export permit. The listing carries annotation #4, in the version adopted at CoP20 and in force since 5 March 2026: seeds, spores and pollen, seedlings and tissue cultures obtained in vitro and transported in sterile containers, and cut flowers of artificially propagated plants are not covered by CITES. Macrozamia seeds can therefore move without a CITES permit, although plant health and import rules still apply. The United Kingdom, the United States and Australia apply CITES through their own national legislation and permit systems. For readers in the European Union, the genus falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, the list in force being set by Commission Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026. These statuses were checked on Species+ on 1 October 2026; check them, and the customs and plant health rules, before buying from abroad. This general information does not replace the official texts.

For the amateur, the rule is simple: buy seed-grown plants of known origin. On the Fous de palmiers forum, growers report that large specimens imported from Australia came from authorised removals during construction works, and that seeds of small, rare species sold in some years came from illegal collecting. These accounts cannot be checked here, but they are a good reason to ask where a plant comes from.

Growing Macrozamia

Macrozamia are grown like the other cycads of mild climates: very free-draining soil, regular watering in summer, and no combination of cold and wet soil in winter. Their great drawback is slowness: a decorative crown takes years, and a plant defoliated by frost needs one or two seasons to rebuild itself.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Light: Macrozamia moorei, a plant of open country, takes full sun; at the Jardin des Plantes in Montpellier, in the south of France, a specimen in the ground grows in full sun in front of a south-facing wall, according to a grower on the Fous de palmiers forum. Macrozamia communis grows rather under eucalypts in the wild: it tolerates sun once acclimatised, but a young plant moved into the sun too quickly will scorch.
  • Soil: free-draining and rather mineral (coarse sand, volcanic scoria, pumice). A grower in Cagnes-sur-Mer (Alpes-Maritimes, France) reported in 2012 that his Macrozamia communis thrives in chalky soil, although the species is often said to need acid soil.
  • Watering: regular in summer; a specialist grower on the Fous de palmiers forum stresses that cycads are not true xerophytes. In winter, cold combined with wet is the main cause of rot.
  • Feeding: the same grower finds cycads fairly hungry for nitrogen; an Italian grower considers Macrozamia sensitive to fertilisers rich in phosphorus, a remark not confirmed by any published source.
  • Pests: scale insects are the problem most often mentioned; an Italian grower notes that his Macrozamia turn yellow in the sun and get covered in scale in the shade. In 2026 a grower in Six-Fours-les-Plages (Var, France) reported that a young Macrozamia moorei lost its growing point after a heavy attack of mealybugs.
  • Propagation: from seed, in a deep pot, because the taproot that emerges first is very fragile. You need a male plant, a female plant and, outside Australia, hand pollination.

At the Jardin zoologique tropical in La Londe-les-Maures (Var, south-eastern France), which grows five species, Macrozamia communis, Macrozamia moorei, Macrozamia riedlei, Macrozamia spiralis and Macrozamia mountperriensis, experience gives the following pointers. This Mediterranean experience may help readers in comparable climates, such as coastal California or southern Australia, without giving an exact equivalent for any particular place.

  • Watering: Macrozamia stand drought well, but they need watering in summer when it does not rain: once a week is the minimum for the plants to do well, twice a week is the optimum. Regular summer watering clearly improves their appearance and growth.
  • Exposure: in the south of France, most species take full sun once mature and acclimatised; partial shade also suits them, particularly Macrozamia communis, and gives better protection from frost.
  • Deficiencies: some species show magnesium, manganese or iron deficiencies, with yellowish-green foliage or yellow speckling. A complete, balanced fertiliser usually corrects them.
  • Winter: a dry frost is less dangerous than the same frost after a spell of rain, when soil and plant are saturated. Perfectly drained soil is essential, and light protection (fleece, dry mulch, a cover against rain) is useful when frost is forecast.

Container cultivation

In cool climates, a pot is the safe way to grow most Macrozamia, and the only sensible one for the rare species, for which no outdoor cold data have been published. Choose a deep container for the taproot and a mineral, free-draining mix; do not let the rootball dry out in summer, and keep the plant drier and frost-free in winter, in a cold glasshouse, conservatory or bright room. Growers' reports on plants in pots or under glass show what they can take:

  • A grower in Roussillon (Pyrénées-Orientales, France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that a fairly large Macrozamia moorei in a pot, left outdoors, took −4 °C (24.8 °F) in early December 2012 without any damage to the foliage.
  • A grower in the Var (France) reports on the same forum that his potted Macrozamia communis, sheltered under a Phoenix, had its foliage scorched in February 2012 (−6.7 °C, 19.9 °F, under the carport, probably about −8 °C, 17.6 °F, at ground level in his estimate); new leaves emerged from April, and the plant looked respectable again by the end of August.
  • A grower in Roussillon reports that a Macrozamia diplomera about 18 years old, with a trunk 3.5 to 4 cm across, withstood −7 °C (19.4 °F) without any damage in an unheated glasshouse (post of March 2014; date of the frost not stated).
  • A grower in the Vendée (western France) reports that his young potted Macrozamia communis, moved in spring 2014 onto a terrace facing south-east, had its leaves burnt by the sun; moved back to partial shade, it resumed growth during the summer. Acclimatise any plant raised under glass gradually.

Cold hardiness

No garden or institution has published measured cold-hardiness results: the data come from amateurs, mostly on the French forum Fous de palmiers, and concern almost only three species, Macrozamia communis, Macrozamia moorei and Macrozamia johnsonii, the commonest in the trade. These reports show that the underground stem survives better than the foliage: complete defoliation is frequent in hard winters, followed by slow recovery. They are amateur observations of indicative value, not general rules, and the identity of the plants is not always certain. The forum posts were re-read in their threads in October 2026.

Reports from Europe

In broad terms, Macrozamia communis grows without protection in Montpellier, on the French Riviera and in Roussillon, but loses all or part of its leaves at about −7 to −9 °C (19.4 to 15.8 °F). A grower from Saint-Raphaël reported in 2008 having seen it in the most sheltered gardens of the Riviera (Saint-Jean-Cap-Ferrat, Menton) and at La Mortola, just over the border in Italy. On the Atlantic coast of France, around Nantes, in the Vendée and the Gironde, the three species get through winter only with protection, and the foliage is often damaged; a grower in the Gironde considers Macrozamia moorei not worth trying without shelter in USDA zone 8 (his own classification). These Atlantic reports are the closest guide for mild, damp climates such as south-west England. No dated report is documented for mountain areas, nor for the cold spell of January 1985.

  • A grower in Montpellier (Hérault, France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his Macrozamia communis of various sizes, in the ground and unprotected for years, were all completely defoliated in winter 2011–2012 by lows of −8 to −9 °C (17.6 to 15.8 °F), then all produced new leaves in summer 2012, without a single loss.
  • A grower in Roussillon (Pyrénées-Orientales, France) reports on the same forum that his Macrozamia communis planted outdoors, intact down to about −4 °C (24.8 °F), was three-quarters defoliated in winter 2009–2010 at −7 °C (19.4 °F), with 27 days of frost. He added in 2011 that, without protection or tree cover, the leaflet tips burn at −5 °C (23 °F) and 30 to 50 % of the foliage at −7 °C, although another specimen stayed almost intact at −7 °C.
  • A grower in the hinterland of the Riviera, between Cannes and Grasse (Alpes-Maritimes, France), reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that in December 2009 his Macrozamia communis and Macrozamia moorei in the ground, without any protection, had their leaves browned but not burnt by a low of −4 °C (24.8 °F). He adds that his Macrozamia moorei in the ground has already taken −6 °C (21.2 °F) with only a few browned leaves (date not stated).
  • A grower in the southern suburbs of Nantes (Loire-Atlantique, France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his two Macrozamia communis in the ground, under the light canopy of a eucalyptus, showed slight burns at −6 °C (21.2 °F) under protection in winter 2007–2008; in January 2009, at −8 °C (17.6 °F) and despite protection, one was badly damaged and the other spotted with burns. He moved them back into pots, where they regrew well.
  • A grower in Héric (Loire-Atlantique, France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his Macrozamia johnsonii, in the garden under fleece or an upturned pot, had yellow and brown leaves after the cold spell of February 2012 (one night at about −10 °C, 14 °F, the others at about −8 °C, three days without thaw); the plant produced its first new leaf at the end of May 2012.
  • A grower in Saint-Médard-de-Guizières (Gironde, France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his Macrozamia moorei (underground trunk of 40 cm), tried outdoors in winters 2013–2014 and 2014–2015 after about ten years in a frost-free glasshouse, was defoliated at about −3 to −4 °C (26.6 to 24.8 °F); it went back under glass.
  • A grower formerly in the Paris suburbs, now on the Côte Bleue (Bouches-du-Rhône, France), reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his Macrozamia communis withstood −12 °C (10.4 °F), simply kept dry under fleece and bubble wrap at the foot of a Trachycarpus; in some fifteen years it was defoliated only twice, including in winter 2011–2012 under basic protection.
  • A grower in Concarneau (Finistère, Brittany, France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his Macrozamia communis in the ground was “recovering” in October 2012, with a small tuft of new leaves, after winter 2011–2012; in his garden he had recorded −5 °C (23 °F) for 2 to 3 hours in February 2012.
  • A grower in Focene (Fiumicino, Lazio, Italy), on the coast, reports on the Italian forum Tropicamente that his plant, bought as Macrozamia moorei and identified from a photograph as Macrozamia communis by another member, in the ground for about four years and never protected, suffered no damage in winter 2011–2012 despite −2.5 to −3 °C (27.5 to 26.6 °F) and 2 to 3 cm of snow.

In Italy, the Negombo thermal park on Ischia displays in the ground several Macrozamia communis imported from Australia in 1990, at the entrance, near the seawater pool and on the rocks; no figures are documented on their tolerance of salt spray.

Reports from North America

PalmTalk, the main English-language forum, is protected by Cloudflare and could be read only through search snippets. A grower in Gainesville (Florida, United States) reports there that four Macrozamia species in the ground came through −7 °C (19.4 °F), year not stated. A grower in Modesto (Central Valley, California, United States) reports that his Macrozamia johnsonii takes both −7 °C (19.4 °F) in winter and +43 °C (109.4 °F) in summer, years not stated. These snippets give neither the state of the plants after the frost nor the protection used. Further north, a small cycad nursery on the Sunshine Coast of British Columbia (Canada) reports on the UBC Botanical Garden forums, in May 2006, that a three-year-old Macrozamia communis planted out the previous winter was “green and thriving”, while a Dioon edule planted at the same time lost its leaves; no temperature is given.

In short, Macrozamia communis can be tried in the ground in a Mediterranean climate and in sheltered gardens with mild winters, in very free-draining soil, accepting the loss of its leaves in a hard winter. Macrozamia moorei, whose foliage seems, depending on the report, more or less hardy than that of Macrozamia communis, is best kept for the mildest gardens, and large specimens, which are costly, deserve protection. For Macrozamia johnsonii, the only dated French report is encouraging, under protection. For the other species, rare in cultivation and without published cold data, a large pot overwintered frost-free remains the prudent choice.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In short: in Britain Macrozamia are essentially glasshouse plants; in the United States they are grown outdoors in southern Florida and coastal California; in Australia the big botanic gardens grow most of the genus in the open. A species listed by a garden in the BGCI PlantSearch database (consulted in October 2026) shows that it has been declared there at some point, not that a living plant is there today.

  • United Kingdom: according to search snippets of its collection page (the site itself could not be read), Macrozamia is one of the strengths of Kew's cycad collection, grown under glass in the Palm House, the Temperate House and the Jodrell glasshouses; Kew declares Macrozamia communis, Macrozamia diplomera, Macrozamia douglasii, Macrozamia macdonnellii, Macrozamia miquelii and Macrozamia moorei in BGCI PlantSearch. The Royal Botanic Garden Edinburgh's Garden Explorer (updated 18 October 2026) lists Macrozamia communis and Macrozamia moorei; its glasshouses are closed for restoration. The Cambridge University Botanic Garden catalogue lists Macrozamia communis (accession of 1964) and Macrozamia moorei (2004). RHS Wisley declares eight species in BGCI, including Macrozamia johnsonii, Macrozamia lucida and Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi, and Glasgow six. The only outdoor trace is Tresco Abbey Garden, on the Isles of Scilly, an outdoor garden that describes its climate as practically frost-free: it declares Macrozamia communis in BGCI, its only cycad listed there, but no source says where the plant grows or whether it is still alive.
  • United States: Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida), whose cycads grow outdoors, holds 17 Macrozamia species in its online catalogue, 11 of them with accessions from 2020 or later. Toxicity and precautions

Yes, Macrozamia are highly toxic, especially their seeds and young leaves. A research veterinarian in New South Wales (Healy, 1967) distinguished two diseases of livestock.

  • “Zamia staggers”: cattle that eat the leaves, above all the young shoots, gradually develop a gait disorder of the hind legs. According to Queensland's veterinary services (known from a search snippet), the signs usually appear after more than 14 days of grazing: the animal loses coordination of its hindquarters, staggers and finally goes down. The damage to the spinal cord is irreversible and there is no treatment. The toxin responsible for these nervous signs has not been identified. In New South Wales, farmers speak of “Burrawang staggers” or “rickets”. Australian veterinary documents, known from snippets, note that the seeds and young leaves are palatable and that regrowth after a fire is often to blame.
  • Liver and gut damage: the seeds contain azoxyglycosides, including macrozamin. In the gut these compounds release methylazoxymethanol (MAM), a potent liver toxin and a carcinogen in laboratory animals. Among the poisonings that occur sporadically in New South Wales, Healy (1967) cites as a typical case the one published by Seddon, Belschner and King (1931): 30 % mortality in a flock of 6,000 Merino ewes, attributed to eating ripe seeds of “Macrozamia spiralis”. Neither the date nor the exact place of the episode is given, and at the time this name was mostly applied to Macrozamia communis or Macrozamia lucida, before Johnson (1959) restored it to Salisbury's small species: the species actually involved is therefore unknown. In 2006, steers on the mid-north coast of New South Wales died of acute liver necrosis after grazing “burrawang” leaves, without nervous signs (Glassop & Gill, 2007).

Concentrations vary with the species and the part of the seed: according to Ladd et al. (1993), cited by Asmussen (2011), macrozamin makes up about 3.9 % of the fresh weight of the flesh of Macrozamia riedlei and of the kernel of Macrozamia miquelii, 3.7 % of the kernel of Macrozamia moorei, but only 0.4 % of the kernel of Macrozamia riedlei. No data specific to dogs and cats are documented for the genus, but poisoning of pets by cycads is well known: pick up fallen seeds, especially if children or dogs use the garden. If a seed or leaf has been eaten, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Aboriginal use of the seeds

Despite their toxicity, Macrozamia seeds were an important food for several Aboriginal peoples, who knew how to detoxify them. Asmussen's review (2011) brings together 43 written accounts from 1788 to 1979; five species are recorded as eaten: Macrozamia communis, Macrozamia miquelii, Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi, Macrozamia riedlei and Macrozamia spiralis.

  • Eastern Australia: the kernel was eaten. The commonest method was long leaching: the kernels, often crushed, were left for two days to two weeks in the water of a stream or pool, sometimes in a fibre bag, then roasted in the ashes or ground into flour for cakes. For Macrozamia miquelii, the seeds were first roasted in their shells, then the kernels leached for six to eight days.
  • South-west of Western Australia: the Noongar, by contrast, ate the red flesh of Macrozamia riedlei, the most toxic part of the seed, and discarded the kernel. After a few days' soaking, the seeds were buried in rush-lined pits, called mordak, for two weeks to a month; the flesh was then eaten raw or roasted, and traded. Europeans who tasted it compared it to dates or olives.
  • Central Australia: no account mentions Macrozamia macdonnellii being eaten, perhaps because of its scarcity and the lack of water for leaching.

This knowledge, handed down over generations, is not a recipe: incomplete preparation causes severe poisoning. Never eat cycad seeds.

Frequently asked questions

Can a Macrozamia stay outdoors in winter in the UK or a cool climate?

Only in the mildest, most sheltered gardens, and even then as an experiment. No Macrozamia growing outdoors in Britain is documented apart from a Macrozamia communis declared by Tresco, on the frost-free Isles of Scilly. In France, Macrozamia communis survives unprotected on the Mediterranean coast but loses its leaves at about −7 to −9 °C (19.4 to 15.8 °F), and on the mild, damp Atlantic coast it gets through winter only when kept dry under protection. Elsewhere, grow it in a pot and overwinter it frost-free.

What is the difference between a Macrozamia and a Cycas revoluta?

The leaflets. Those of Cycas revoluta have a strong midrib; those of Macrozamia have only parallel veins. Macrozamia also bear their seeds in cones and, according to growers, produce their leaves one at a time rather than in a single flush.

Are Macrozamia poisonous to animals?

Yes. In Australia they cause “zamia staggers”, an irreversible paralysis of the hindquarters in cattle, and fatal liver damage in cattle and sheep. The seeds are the most dangerous part. Pick them up, and call a vet at the slightest suspicion that a dog or another animal has eaten some.

How many species of Macrozamia are there?

41, all Australian, according to The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and Species+. The number has nearly tripled since 1959, when L.A.S. Johnson recognised only 14, thanks to the species described between 1991 and 2001.

Why does my Macrozamia not set seed?

Because it takes a male plant, a female plant and a pollinator. Macrozamia are pollinated by specialist Australian thrips and weevils; without them, outside Australia, you have to pollinate by hand. An isolated female may produce a cone, but its seeds will be infertile.

Does a Macrozamia take full sun?

Yes for most mature, acclimatised plants, especially Macrozamia moorei, a plant of open country. A young plant raised in shade or under glass will scorch if moved abruptly into the sun: acclimatise it gradually, starting in partial shade.

Why do Macrozamia grow so slowly?

It is their nature, and a lack of warmth makes it worse. According to Jones (1993), a Macrozamia moorei takes less than a century to reach 2 m (about 6.5 ft) of trunk and forms its first cones at around 50 years old. In France, growers find that a plant defoliated by frost needs one or two seasons to rebuild its crown.
