Zamia vazquezii is a small cycad with an underground stem from the north of the state of Veracruz, in eastern Mexico. This species of the genus Zamia is widespread in collections, yet Critically Endangered in the wild, where only two localities are known. The reason is that it was grown for decades under the mistaken name of Zamia fischeri, and was only recognised as a distinct species in 1998, by botanists from New York and Naples. Its upright leaves, with many oval, toothed leaflets, give it the look of a fern.

Key facts about Zamia vazquezii

  • Accepted name: Zamia vazquezii D.W.Stev., Sabato & De Luca (1998), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and by GBIF; POWO could not be consulted directly
  • Former names and synonyms: no synonym; long grown under the name Zamia fischeri, which belongs to another species
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: no name attested, in English or in Spanish
  • Natural range: Mexico, north of the state of Veracruz (endemic); 40 to 350 m according to herbarium specimens
  • IUCN status: Critically Endangered (CR), criteria A2cd; B1ab(i,ii,iii,v)+2ab(i,ii,iii,v); C2a(i); D, assessment published in 2022 (corrected version 2023)
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (whole genus, since 1977; seeds exempt under annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union; in Mexico, “in danger of extinction” under standard NOM-059-SEMARNAT-2010
  • Documented hardiness: no published measurement; in Roussillon (southern France), foliage undamaged after several days at −2 °C (28.4 °F) in February 2012 and two brief frosts of −3 to −4 °C (26.6 to 24.8 °F), in a sheltered, dry spot; rated for USDA zone 9b by a French specialist nursery (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: evergreen tropical forest according to herbarium data; in cultivation, part shade and a free-draining compost, rot being the main risk reported
  • Habit and adult size: underground, roughly globular stem up to 10 cm in diameter; 3 to 30 upright leaves 30 cm to 1 m long, with 12 to 25 (30) pairs of leaflets
  • In the UK, US and Australia: declared to BGCI by Kew and by some twenty American and three Australian gardens; three accessions at Montgomery Botanical Center (Florida); grown in the ground in Palm Beach County (Florida) without cold damage, according to a local cycad society
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; treat it as poisonous, like all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Zamia vazquezii

It is recognised by its tuft of upright, numerous and fairly long leaves, carrying many oval leaflets, thin as paper and finely toothed in their upper part: the whole plant looks more like a fern than a cycad. The original description (Stevenson, Sabato, Moretti and De Luca, Delpinoa, 1998) gives the following characters:

  • Stem: underground, roughly globular, up to 10 cm in diameter.
  • Cataphylls (small leaves reduced to scales that protect the growing point): oval, 1 to 1.5 cm long and 1.5 to 2 cm wide.
  • Leaves: 3 to 30, 30 cm to 1 m long, upright; petiole 15 to 40 cm, smooth or with a few scattered prickles; rachis with 12 to 25 pairs of leaflets, sometimes up to 30.
  • Leaflets: ovate to obpyriform (shaped like an upside-down pear), wedge-shaped at the base, drawn out to a point at the tip, with many teeth along the upper two-thirds of the margin, papery to membranous, hairless; the middle ones, the largest, measure 6 to 7 (8) cm by 3 to 4 cm.
  • Male cones: tawny, ovoid to ovoid-cylindrical, 5 to 7 (8) cm by 2 to 2.5 cm, on a stalk 1.5 to 2.5 cm long; pollen sacs only on the underside of the scales.
  • Female cones: tawny to brown, cylindrical to ovoid-cylindrical, 10 to 15 cm by 5 to 7 cm.
  • Seeds: orange-red to red, 1.3 to 1.8 cm by 0.5 to 0.8 cm.
  • Chromosomes: 2n = 18.

The authors compare the leaflets to those of ferns of the genus Cyrtomium or of some Adiantum. In cultivation, new leaves emerge bronze according to a grower on Ischia, in Italy (Tropicamente forum, 2012), and the nursery Cycadales.eu shows the species among the Mexican Zamia with brightly coloured new leaves. A member of the Palm Beach Palm and Cycad Society, in Florida, distinguishes two forms in cultivation, one with green and one with bronze new leaves, which can be told apart only when new leaves are present.

Hybrids

No hybrid, natural or raised in cultivation, has been documented in a publication. The long confusion with Zamia fischeri has mainly produced labelling errors, not published crosses. In 2015, on the French forum Fous de palmiers, a specialist collector raised the possibility of a hybrid for a seedling obtained with no known male plant nearby, without being able to check it.

Possible confusion

The main confusion is between Zamia vazquezii and Zamia fischeri, the species of the Sierra Madre Oriental under whose name it was grown for decades. The 1998 description gives simple characters, visible to the naked eye on adult plants:

  • Zamia vazquezii: more than six leaves, longer than 30 cm, upright, with more than 12 pairs of ovate or obpyriform leaflets with a tapering or drawn-out tip; 2n = 18; Veracruz.
  • Zamia fischeri: fewer than six leaves, shorter than 30 cm, bent back, with fewer than 12 pairs of lanceolate leaflets with a sharp tip; 2n = 16; San Luis Potosí and Querétaro, populations separate from those of Veracruz.

Nicolalde-Morejón and colleagues (2011) group the two species in a “Zamia fischeri complex” of north-eastern Mexico (Tamaulipas, San Luis Potosí, Querétaro, Hidalgo and northern Veracruz): generally small plants with papery, toothed leaflets and an underground stem, which can also be told apart by their female cones. On young plants the distinction is trickier: on the Fous de palmiers forum, a specialist collector pointed out in 2010 that the juvenile leaves of cycads often represent the adult leaves poorly and that the two species are almost impossible to separate at that stage. Several plants and seed lots sold as Zamia fischeri were recognised there as Zamia vazquezii (2010, 2014, 2015). The author of an article on zamias published in 2012 on the Australian gardening site GardenDrum makes the same point: Zamia fischeri being rare in cultivation, plants sold under that name are likely to be Zamia vazquezii.

The other Zamia of Veracruz are easier to recognise. Zamia furfuracea, very common in gardens, has thick, leathery leaflets, downy beneath. Zamia inermis, endemic to the centre of the state, has a petiole and rachis without prickles, as its name says. Zamia loddigesii, found in Veracruz and other states, has lanceolate leaflets, much longer and leathery, while Zamia purpurea, from Veracruz and Oaxaca, carries only three or four pairs of broad, glossy leaflets.

Taxonomy

Zamia vazquezii is a name accepted by The World List of Cycads and by GBIF; POWO, the site’s nomenclatural authority, could not be consulted directly. The species was described in an article with an explicit title, “What is Zamia fischeri Miquel?”, published in volume 37–38 of the new series of Delpinoa, the journal of the Naples botanic garden (pages 9 to 17, description on page 14). The volume is dated 1995–1996, but it was not printed until November 1998: this is the date given for the name by The World List of Cycads and IPNI (record 319963-2; the identifier 1016948-1 is a duplicate citation).

The article is signed by Dennis Wm. Stevenson, of the New York Botanical Garden, and by Sergio Sabato, Aldo Moretti and Paolo De Luca, of the University of Naples Federico II; the name of the species is attributed to Stevenson, Sabato and De Luca only, although some authors, such as Nicolalde-Morejón and colleagues in 2011, add Moretti. The type was collected in Veracruz on 22 January 1989 by Mario Vázquez Torres and colleagues (number 3990); the holotype is kept in New York (NY), with isotypes at FTG (Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden), MO (Missouri Botanical Garden), NY, U (Utrecht) and XALU (Universidad Veracruzana, Xalapa).

The authors’ reasoning runs as follows. Zamia fischeri had been described by Miquel in a letter to the Belgian nurseryman Louis Van Houtte, published in 1845; the half-dozen or so collections known up to 1932, kept in Utrecht and St Petersburg, came from San Luis Potosí and matched Miquel’s description and drawing. After 1932, plants from Veracruz entered cultivation under the same name, and the flora of the cycads of Veracruz by Vovides and colleagues (1983, 1992) also treated them as Zamia fischeri. By comparing plants from the two states, grown for years under the same conditions at Fairchild Tropical Garden in Miami, the New York Botanical Garden and the Naples botanic garden, the authors found that each kept its own characters. Cytology confirmed the difference: plants from San Luis Potosí always have 2n = 16, those from Veracruz always 2n = 18 (Moretti and colleagues, 1991; Caputo and colleagues, 1996).

In practice, most “Zamia fischeri” in collections, on old labels and in catalogues are really Zamia vazquezii. The name has no synonym, since the species had never been described before under another name. The World List of Cycads also cites the revision of the Zamia of “Mega-Mexico” by Nicolalde-Morejón, Vovides and Stevenson (Brittonia, 2009), which has not been seen.

On the molecular side, the transcriptome-based study by Lindstrom and colleagues (2024), which sampled a plant from Veracruz, strongly supports a distinct “Fischeri clade”, first recognised by Calonje and colleagues (2019), made up of Zamia vazquezii, Zamia fischeri and Zamia inermis. Whereas the 2019 study placed this clade as sister to the other mainland American Zamia, most of the 2024 analyses place it as sister to the Caribbean clade, a link also reflected in shared characters such as the prickle-free petiole; the authors date the split between the two clades to about 26 million years ago and speciation within the Fischeri clade to the early Miocene, about 19 million years ago, while noting that the position of the clade differs between analyses.

Etymology and history

The epithet honours Mario Vázquez Torres, described by the original description as a keen student of the cycads of Central America, who discovered the species and was the first to collect it. The World List of Cycads, after Haynes’s etymological compendium (2022), gives his name as Santiago Mario Vázquez Torres, of the Instituto de Investigaciones Biológicas in Xalapa, Veracruz. He is also a co-author of the description of Zamia inermis and of the flora of the cycads of Veracruz cited by the authors.

The history of the species is linked to Naples. According to an article on the glasshouses of the botanic garden published in 2019 in Delpinoa (Sibilio), expeditions organised between 1974 and 1994 by Paolo De Luca, Sergio Sabato and Aldo Moretti, with Mario Vázquez Torres and Dennis Stevenson, brought Ceratozamia, Dioon and Zamia back to Naples; the species is still grown there under glass.

Common names

No common name is attested for Zamia vazquezii, in English or in Spanish. In the trade the plant often circulates under the mistaken scientific name Zamia fischeri, which is not a common name but an error to be corrected.

In the wild

Zamia vazquezii is endemic to the state of Veracruz (The World List of Cycads, original description), and more precisely to the north of the state according to the study by Gómez Díaz (2025), which draws on the IUCN assessment. Only two localities are known, in three municipalities, with an extent of occurrence of about 88 km²; none lies in a protected area. The exact localities are not published, as for many threatened cycads. The herbarium specimens gathered by Nicolalde-Morejón and colleagues place the species between 40 and 350 m, in the Gulf of Mexico biogeographic region (2014); their 2011 table assigns it to evergreen tropical forest, in the sense of Rzedowski (1978).

The original description stresses a single geographical fact: the Veracruz populations are separate from those of Zamia fischeri, which live in San Luis Potosí and neighbouring Querétaro; the 2014 synthesis gives Zamia fischeri for Hidalgo, Querétaro, San Luis Potosí and Tamaulipas, between 140 and 900 m. Like other Zamia, the species has male and female plants; its pollinators and phenology in the wild are not documented in the sources consulted.

Conservation status and regulation

Zamia vazquezii is rated Critically Endangered (CR) on the IUCN Red List, in the assessment by J.D. Bösenberg published in 2022 (e.T42120A69835685 according to The World List of Cycads), under criteria A2cd; B1ab(i,ii,iii,v)+2ab(i,ii,iii,v); C2a(i); D. A corrected version was published in 2023 (e.T42120A243413573); GBIF gives the same category. The Red List itself could not be consulted; Gómez Díaz (2025), who cites it, summarises its content: two localities and no more than 50 wild plants, between 2 and 49 mature individuals observed, a declining population, a loss of about 91 % of the habitat through deforestation and severe fragmentation, with habitat loss and over-collection for ornamental use as the threats.

In Mexico, the species is listed as “in danger of extinction” under standard NOM-059-SEMARNAT-2010 (Nicolalde-Morejón and colleagues, 2014; Gómez Díaz, 2025). In the 2025 study, which ranks the cycads of Veracruz with a conservation priority index, Zamia vazquezii has the highest score, ahead of Zamia inermis: it is the most threatened species in the state. The author recommends education, awareness-raising and long-term monitoring.

The contrast with its spread in cultivation is striking: it is frequently mentioned on growers’ forums, including under the name Zamia fischeri, and a Florida cycad society notes that it is not rare in cultivation. Cultivated plants are no substitute for the wild populations, however, and their origin is seldom documented.

Like all Zamia except Zamia restrepoi, Zamia vazquezii falls under CITES Appendix II, where the genus has been listed since 4 February 1977. International trade in the plants is therefore allowed, but requires an export permit issued by the country of origin. The listing carries annotation #4: seeds, pollen, seedlings and tissue cultures obtained in vitro and transported in sterile containers are not covered by CITES. In the European Union the species falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, whose current list is set by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026.

Species+ has no separate entry for this species: it is covered by the listing “Zamia spp.”. This general information does not replace the official texts: check the regulations in force before buying or exchanging plants, ask the seller for proof of origin and prefer plants raised from seed in cultivation.

Growing Zamia vazquezii

There is no published, verified growing guide for Zamia vazquezii; the available information comes from botanic gardens, nurseries and growers. It describes a plant that does well in a pot, produces several flushes a year when happy and fears rot above all. A specialist collector on the Fous de palmiers forum considered it easy to grow in 2015 “as long as it is warm”, in a compost rich in sand and coarse material to help drainage and limit fungal attack. The nursery Cycadales.eu also describes it as easy to grow given heat and humidity, adds that young plants are very sensitive to drying out, and notes that adults are more tolerant if the compost drains well.

The species has been grown for decades in botanic gardens: at Fairchild Tropical Garden in Miami (accession 69-521), the New York Botanical Garden and the Naples botanic garden, where the Veracruz plants kept their characters in cultivation (original description).

Cycadales.eu places Zamia vazquezii in the group of Zamia from Mexico, Guatemala, El Salvador and Honduras, of “medium” difficulty: plants mostly from climates with a marked dry season, which tolerate dry air and a fairly dry compost in winter. For this group it recommends (Lavaud, 2023):

  • a free-draining, slightly alkaline or neutral compost; any kind of water;
  • in winter, 5 to 10 °C (41 to 50 °F), with daytime rises to 20–25 °C (68–77 °F); prolonged cool spells without such rises are only moderately tolerated, with a fairly dry but not parched compost;
  • in summer, 15 to 20 °C (59 to 68 °F) at night and 25 to 30 °C (77 to 86 °F) by day, with up to 40 °C (104 °F) tolerated if watered generously;
  • at least four to six months of summer-type temperatures a year: grown at winter temperatures all year round, these tropical plants decline and die in the medium term.

These are tips from experience for a group of species, not measurements made on Zamia vazquezii.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

The most detailed English-language report comes from Palm Beach County, in southern Florida. In an undated article for the Palm Beach Palm and Cycad Society, a member describes the species as very easy to grow, in shade or full sun, though it looks best with some shade; well-grown plants carry many leaves 60 to 90 cm (two to three feet) tall. In the ground the plants usually keep their leaves in winter and have never been affected by cold in his garden, whereas plants in containers usually lose their leaves in winter. He finds it under-used in landscaping, for example as a border plant.

In Australia, the author of the 2012 GardenDrum article grows it in the semi-shade of a brush box tree (Lophostemon confertus) and regards it as ideal for a semi-shaded, well-drained position; in reply to a reader, he suggested it for a sheltered spot in a Sydney garden and noted that his plants had stayed fresh through extremely hot, dry weather.

In southern France, a grower in Roussillon has kept a plant outdoors under a camphor tree for several winters (see Cold hardiness). In wetter climates the risk is cold, waterlogged soil: on the Italian forum Tropicamente, a grower in Ravenna reported in May 2011 losing several cycads to rot, including a Zamia vazquezii, in a very wet winter; another participant, whose location is not stated, said he had lost his the same way that year.

Container cultivation

The specialist collector quoted above, on the Fous de palmiers forum (2013), describes how his female plant, rotted at the growing point, took three years to form a new crown before taking off again: a stem with a damaged apex should not be thrown away unless it has rotted to the core. Light also needs care: a grower in Roussillon noted in January 2014 that low winter sun passing under the canopy scorched the leaves. On Ischia, a grower whose garden rarely falls below 4 °C (39.2 °F) at night saw it produce two flushes in 2012; a grower in Ravenna keeps it in a pot, like almost all his cycads.

From these reports, a free-draining compost, reduced watering in the cool season and a bright spot sheltered from direct winter sun are the precautions that stand out; they have not been validated by published trials.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In short: one of the most widely held Zamia in botanic gardens, with a British declaration (Kew), many American ones and three Australian ones.

  • United States: the online catalogue of Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida) lists three accessions, numbers 94330, 941486 and 2000137, the first two dating from 1994 according to their numbering (consulted on 24 October 2026). Fairchild Tropical Garden (Miami) held plants under accession 69-521 when the species was described. Fairchild, the Huntington (San Marino, California), San Diego Botanic Garden (Encinitas), San Diego Zoo, Descanso Gardens (La Cañada Flintridge, California), the Missouri Botanical Garden (St Louis), the New York Botanical Garden, Naples Botanical Garden, Marie Selby Botanical Gardens (Sarasota), Miami Beach Botanical Garden, the Botanic Gardens at Kona Kai (Key Largo), Como Park Zoo and Conservatory and the University of Minnesota conservatory (St Paul), the National Tropical Botanical Garden (Kalāheo), Honolulu Botanical Gardens, the University of Hawaii at Hilo, the Four Seasons Resort on Lāna‘i and the John Fairey Garden declare it to BGCI.
  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney, Brisbane Botanic Gardens Mount Coot-tha and the Cairns Botanic Gardens (Queensland) declare it to BGCI.
  • United Kingdom: the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, declare it to BGCI.

A BGCI declaration shows that a garden held the plant at some point, not that it is alive today, and the sources do not say whether these plants grow outdoors or under glass. Plants acquired as Zamia fischeri may also turn out to be Zamia vazquezii.

Propagation

The species is propagated from seed, and seeds are fertile only if a female plant has been pollinated by a male. A specialist collector on the Fous de palmiers forum (2015) points out that Zamia produce seeds that look complete even without pollination: with no male nearby, or without hand pollination, they are sterile. He advises cleaning off the flesh around the seed, possibly treating it with a fungicide, and germinating it in a bag of barely moist peat or sphagnum moss; another participant obtained a seedling after several months of waiting, when he had given up hope. The same collector reports that his male plant produces two cones every 8 to 10 months. In Palm Beach County, the Florida grower quoted above finds the species easy to raise from seed and notes that local pollinators sometimes pollinate the plants.

Seeds are exempt from CITES under annotation #4, but they must come from cultivated plants of legal origin and, given the confusion with Zamia fischeri, from correctly identified parents.

Pests and diseases

The problem most often reported is rot of the stem or growing point, mentioned several times in France and Italy (see Growing). A French collector reported in 2011 an infestation of scale insects on an adult plant, which resisted an aerosol insecticide used more often than recommended. In Palm Beach County, the Florida grower notes that the plants tend to get scale insects and mealybugs during the dry months; he cuts off the leaves in early spring, which also makes the plant produce a full new crown and stops new leaves struggling through too many old ones. No disease specific to the species is documented.

Cold hardiness

There is no published measurement of the cold tolerance of Zamia vazquezii. The available data come from growers and concern brief, light frosts in sheltered positions: they show that the foliage withstands a few degrees below freezing when dry, without setting a limit. The French nursery Cycadales.eu rates it for USDA zone 9b, “and even colder under canopy”, and finds it surprisingly hardy, with plants growing outdoors under trees in southern France; this is a commercial rating.

Mediterranean and European experience

A grower in Roussillon (Pyrénées-Orientales, southern France), in USDA zone 9b according to his own forum signature, reports on the Fous de palmiers forum:

  • in February 2012, after a dozen days of daily frost at the start of the month (absolute minimum −5 °C, or 23 °F; once −4 °C, or 24.8 °F; several times −2 to −3 °C, or 28.4 to 26.6 °F; with a violent north-westerly wind), that the foliage of his Zamia vazquezii (which he notes is wrongly called Zamia fischeri) was undamaged despite several days at −2 °C (28.4 °F) under the eaves;
  • in December 2012, that the plant had come through −3 to −4 °C (26.6 to 24.8 °F) twice, completely undamaged, a brief cold measured at plant level, in a sheltered, dry spot, and that the cumulative frost of two successive winters had not harmed its leaves; he considered it hardier than Zamia furfuracea;
  • in January 2014, that it was spending its second winter outdoors, under a camphor tree, where the main problem was low January sun scorching the leaves.

He does not say whether the plant was in a pot or in the ground in 2012. This is a single plant, sheltered from rain and wind, and brief frosts: nothing shows how the species behaves in cold, sodden soil, which Italian growers identify as the main risk. In Italy, no frost episode has been documented for the species. The Jardin zoologique tropical at La Londe-les-Maures, in the Var, has published no observation of this species; for comparison only, a Zamia integrifolia, a Florida species of another group, survived −7 °C (19.4 °F) there in February 2012 with damage to its foliage, a result that cannot be transferred to Zamia vazquezii. For readers in comparable climates (coastal California, southern Australia), these Mediterranean results suggest at most a trial in a sheltered, dry position, with the safer option of a pot brought under cover in winter.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

In Palm Beach County, Florida, plants in the ground have never been affected by cold in the garden of the society member quoted above; the article gives no temperatures. No dated cold record has been found in the UK or Australia. The World List of Cycads lists a 2005 article on growing the species in central Texas (Nazor, The Cycad Newsletter), which has not been seen.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity study specific to Zamia vazquezii has been found. The risk is, however, documented in the genus and in other cycads: Zamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, and for Zamia furfuracea the ASPCA cites cycasin and BMAA, a neurotoxic amino acid, with vomiting, bloody diarrhoea and liver damage in dogs, cats and horses; the seeds are the most dangerous part. In the absence of evidence to the contrary, treat the plant as poisonous: keep seeds and leaves out of reach of children and animals, and wear gloves to clean seeds. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet straight away.

Traditional uses

No traditional use is documented for this species; its only known use is ornamental, in cycad collections.

Frequently asked questions

How can I tell Zamia vazquezii from Zamia fischeri?

By the adult leaves. Zamia vazquezii has more than six, upright and longer than 30 cm, with more than 12 pairs of oval leaflets that recall a fern; Zamia fischeri has fewer than six, bent back and short, with lanceolate leaflets. On young plants the distinction is almost impossible.

Is my “Zamia fischeri” really a Zamia vazquezii?

Probably. According to the 1998 description, most plants grown as Zamia fischeri belong to Zamia vazquezii, and collectors have reported several recent cases of plants and seeds sold under the wrong name. Count the leaves and look at the shape of the leaflets.

Why is it so widespread if it is Critically Endangered?

Because it entered cultivation after 1932 under the name Zamia fischeri, long before it was recognised as a species. It spread through collections, whereas in the wild only two localities and at most about fifty plants are known.

Where does it grow in the wild?

Only in the north of the state of Veracruz, Mexico, between about 40 and 350 m, in evergreen tropical forest according to herbarium data. The localities are not published.

Is it frost-hardy?

To light, brief frosts, yes, at least in shelter. In Roussillon, a sheltered plant kept dry stayed undamaged at −2 °C (28.4 °F) for several days and at −3 to −4 °C (26.6 to 24.8 °F) during brief cold spells; no harsher data exist.

Can I grow it outdoors in the UK?

Not reliably. Outdoor results come from southern Florida, Australia and the south of France; in Britain it is a plant for a pot kept in a frost-free glasshouse or conservatory in winter, at least four to six months of warmth a year being needed for healthy growth.

Why is my Zamia vazquezii rotting?

Most often because of a cold, over-wet compost in winter, the problem growers report most. Water less in the cool season; a stem rotted at the apex can recover if the core is sound.

Is it poisonous?

It must be assumed so. No analysis concerns the species, but the cycads studied contain serious toxins, especially in their seeds.
