Encephalartos ferox is a cycad with a mostly underground trunk that grows along the Indian Ocean coast, from north-eastern KwaZulu-Natal in South Africa to southern Mozambique, in dune forest, coastal thicket and woodland on sand. This species of the genus Encephalartos, described in 1851 by the Italian botanist Giuseppe Bertoloni as Encephalartus ferox, is easy to recognise by its glossy dark green, holly-like leaflets ending in sharp lobes and by its orange to scarlet cones, unique among southern African cycads. The KwaZulu-Natal plants were first described as Encephalartos kosiensis, now a synonym, and a Mozambican subspecies, Encephalartos ferox subsp. emersus, was described in 2015. Listed as Near Threatened by the IUCN, it is a plant of frost-free climates, widely grown under glass and in the mildest gardens.

Key facts about Encephalartos ferox

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos ferox Bertol.f. (1851), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15), GBIF and Species+; two accepted subspecies, subsp. ferox and subsp. emersus P.Rousseau, Vorster & A.E.van Wyk (2015)
  • Former names and synonyms: Encephalartos kosiensis Hutch. (1932); original spelling Encephalartus ferox
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Maputaland cycad, Zululand cycad, Tongaland cycad (English, PlantZAfrica); Kozi cycad (Species+); umthobane, uthobani (Zulu); chihanga (Tsonga)
  • Natural range: north-eastern KwaZulu-Natal (Sodwana Bay to Kosi Bay) and the coast of Mozambique (Maputo, Gaza and Inhambane provinces), south of the Save River
  • IUCN status: Near Threatened (NT, criterion A4cd), assessment by J. S. Donaldson and colleagues dated September 2020, published in 2022; subsp. emersus Critically Endangered (CR)
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I (whole genus, since 1977); Annex A in the European Union; positive EU opinion for artificially propagated plants from South Africa up to a caudex diameter of 15 cm
  • Documented hardiness: no frost in the wild; young plants in the ground in France survived the cold spell of February 2012; no damage at −3.9 °C (25 °F) in a San Diego nursery (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: partial shade, well-drained sandy soil and plenty of water in summer (PlantZAfrica); filtered light in a rich but free-draining soil (Jungle Music)
  • Habit and adult size: trunk underground, rarely emerging to about 1 m (1 to 1.5 m in subsp. emersus), 20 to 40 cm in diameter; leaves 1 to 2.6 m long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: declared by Kew, Edinburgh, Oxford, Chelsea Physic Garden, Wisley and other British gardens; Montgomery, Fairchild, Lotusland and more than thirty other US gardens; Sydney, Melbourne (outdoors), Cairns, Adelaide and Townsville in Australia
  • Toxicity: seeds poisonous, as in other Encephalartos (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos ferox

Look for broad, glossy dark green leaflets that widen towards the tip and end in three to five sharp lobes, and for red or orange cones. Dyer (1965) found no close relative among the South African species. His description, completed by the revision of Rousseau and colleagues (2015) and by PlantZAfrica, gives:

  • Trunk: underground, rarely emerging (to about 1 m), 20 to 40 cm in diameter, single or with one to four suckers at the base; plants in full sun sucker more, according to Donaldson (1998, cited by Rousseau and colleagues)
  • Leaves: 1 to 2.6 m long, arching, hairy and often coppery when young (PlantZAfrica), then hairless; petiole with two to six spines in the typical subspecies
  • Leaflets: on average 30 to 37 pairs, broad (3 to 5 cm, sometimes more), 14 to 19 cm long, wavy, with two to six teeth on the upper margin and none to four on the lower, and a tip with two or three close-set teeth
  • Male cones: spindle-shaped, orange to red, rarely yellow, 32 to 44 cm long (up to 96 cm for one measured cone), up to five produced in succession
  • Female cones: ovoid to cylindrical, usually red, less often orange or yellow, 30 to 57 cm long, one or two (very rarely three) at a time
  • Seeds: with a fleshy coat that is white and then red when ripe, 4 to 5 cm long, 81 to 577 per cone (Phelan and colleagues, cited by Rousseau and colleagues)

PlantZAfrica (S. Turner, Walter Sisulu National Botanical Garden, February 2002) gives slightly different figures: stems up to 35 cm across, leaves 1 to 2 m long, leaflets about 15 cm by 3 to 5 cm, lobed on both margins, crowded and overlapping along the leaf; one to five stalkless female cones, 25 to 50 cm tall and 20 to 40 cm across; one to six narrow, cylindrical male cones per crown, 40 to 50 cm long and 8 to 10 cm across, on stalks up to 3 cm; and glossy, bright orange to red seeds up to 5 cm long. Cone colour varies: Rousseau and colleagues mostly saw orange male cones and red female cones, with a gradient towards yellow, and the form with very broad, five-toothed leaflets seems confined to the southernmost populations, the KwaZulu-Natal plants once called Encephalartos kosiensis. The San Diego nursery Jungle Music sells a flat-leaflet form and a form with leaflets curled almost into a tube; these are nursery names, not recognised taxa.

Hybrids

No natural hybrid of Encephalartos ferox is known, since no other species of the genus grows within its range (Rousseau and colleagues, 2015). In cultivation, crosses with other Encephalartos, which usually hybridise readily, have mostly failed; the few hybrids obtained, with very low yields, are Encephalartos ferox × Encephalartos woodii, × Encephalartos trispinosus and × Encephalartos caffer, now Encephalartos afer (Vorster, unpublished data, cited by Rousseau and colleagues).

PlantZAfrica also reports that some authors regard Encephalartos woodii, known from a single male plant found in 1895, as a natural hybrid between Encephalartos natalensis and Encephalartos ferox. This is only one hypothesis among several: the same source also mentions a relic of an extinct species, a mutation or a robust form of Encephalartos natalensis.

Possible confusion

An adult plant in cone is hard to mistake: its leaflets widening towards the tip with three to five sharp lobes, and its salmon to scarlet cones, are unique in southern Africa (Dyer, 1965). The only other Encephalartos with red cones is Encephalartos gratus, from Malawi and Mozambique, whose foliage is very different; a morphological analysis (Treutlein and colleagues, 2005) placed the two species together, with weak support.

Subspecies emersus resembles Encephalartos hildebrandtii of Tanzania and Kenya in its foliage; the latter has an aerial trunk 2.5 to 6 m tall, longer leaves and narrower, more toothed leaflets, a point Rousseau and colleagues make to help identify cultivated plants of unknown origin. Young plants without a known origin are harder: on the Italian Tropicamente forum, a grower admitted in 2012 that at that stage he could easily confuse seedlings of Encephalartos ferox, Encephalartos horridus, Encephalartos trispinosus and Encephalartos arenarius.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos ferox is accepted by The World List of Cycads, GBIF and Species+. Giuseppe Bertoloni (“Bertol. f.”, Bertoloni the son) published it in 1851 in the Memorie della Reale Accademia delle Scienze dell'Istituto di Bologna (volume 3, pp. 264–265), as Encephalartus ferox, in a paper on the plants of Mozambique. He had only two leaves sent from Mozambique by Fornasini, which he considered close to Encephalartos horridus and to “Encephalartos lanuginosus” but distinct. According to Rousseau and colleagues (2015), the type is a painting of the holotype plant kept at Kew, made by Fornasini, collector and artist, probably from the Inhambane area.

The KwaZulu-Natal plants, discovered at Kosi Bay by Aitken and Gale, were described in 1932 by John Hutchinson as Encephalartos kosiensis, from a plant grown in Durban since 1930; a few years later they were recognised as Bertoloni's species (Dyer, 1965). Rousseau and colleagues (2015) studied the populations along the whole Mozambican coast: the southernmost plants have a few characters of their own (very broad leaflets ending in five teeth) but share most others, with no ecological separation, so that reinstating Encephalartos kosiensis is not justified.

The same study described Encephalartos ferox subsp. emersus (Phytotaxa 204(2): 99–115, 2015; holotype Rousseau 1175, Pretoria) for the single population north of Vilanculos, south of the Save River. It differs in its always emergent trunk 1 to 1.5 m tall, a shorter and spinier petiole (8 to 12 spines), shorter leaves, more numerous and narrower leaflets (2.5 to 3.4 cm), already at the seedling stage, and smaller cones, usually yellow, whose female scales are green inside. The typical populations form the autonym Encephalartos ferox subsp. ferox. Phylogenetically the species remains isolated: molecular analyses place it in a well-supported group, but its closest relative is not established, perhaps an east-coast South African species with an underground trunk.

Etymology and history

The Latin epithet ferox means “fierce” and refers to the stiff leaflets ending in large, sharp teeth (Haynes, 2022, via The World List of Cycads; Rousseau and colleagues, 2015). The genus name comes from the Greek en (within), kephale (head) and artos (bread), after the starchy pith (PlantZAfrica).

Fornasini's letter, published by Bertoloni (1851), already describes the living plant: a “bulb” as big as a human body, from which one or more “fruits” like pineapples, but red, emerge; a plant that is not common but very useful to the inhabitants, who eat the trunk and make a flour from it. Fornasini had one transplanted into his garden, where it bore three cones. In South Africa, Dyer (1965) reports that seed sown in Durban in 1937 produced a plant whose trunk was still almost entirely underground when it coned for the first time, twelve years later.

Common names

PlantZAfrica (SANBI) gives the English names Maputaland cycad, Zululand cycad and Tongaland cycad, the Afrikaans names Maputalandbroodboom and Tongalandbroodboom, the Zulu names umthobane and uthobani, and the Tsonga name chihanga. Species+ lists Tongaland cycad, Zululand cycad and Kozi cycad, the last recalling the old name Encephalartos kosiensis. Jungle Music calls it an “understory cycad with red cones”, a description rather than a name.

In the wild

Encephalartos ferox lives on the coastal plain of Maputaland and southern Mozambique. PlantZAfrica places it in KwaZulu-Natal from Sodwana Bay to Kosi Bay, then along the Mozambican coast to Vilanculos; Rousseau and colleagues (2015) found it at no locality north of the Save River. The typical subspecies grows in coastal dune forest and thicket and in miombo woodland on sand, usually in partial or full shade, between 20 and 100 m above sea level, with 1,000 to 1,250 mm of rain a year; it can grow less than 50 m from the beach and locally forms large populations (PlantZAfrica). There is no frost. Fire is part of its habitat: it damages stems and leaves, but the plant resprouts from its underground trunk.

Subspecies emersus occupies a quite different habitat: a palm-dotted floodplain (Phoenix reclinata, Hyphaene), where the plants grow in full sun on old termite mounds forming hummocks above the flooded areas, up to twenty plants per mound, with about 800 mm of rain a year (Rousseau and colleagues, 2015).

In Mozambique the cones open from late March to early May, and the seeds fall around September. Weevils of the genus Porthetes are the probable pollinators. PlantZAfrica describes seeds sought after by baboons, vervet monkeys, dassies, fruit bats and hornbills, which eat the fleshy coat and spit out the kernel; Rousseau and colleagues, by contrast, note very poor dispersal in the Mozambican populations, with many seeds staying at the foot of the plant.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos ferox is listed as Near Threatened (NT) on the IUCN Red List under criterion A4cd, which concerns an ongoing population reduction. The assessment by J. S. Donaldson, H. A. Matimele, D. Raimondo and colleagues is dated 16 September 2020 and was published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, GBIF, Crossref). The subspecies were assessed separately: subsp. ferox Near Threatened and subsp. emersus Critically Endangered (CR), according to the IUCN list relayed by GBIF; Rousseau and colleagues had proposed that category for emersus in 2015 because of illegal collecting. The Red List itself could not be consulted directly.

The status has changed over time: “rare” in 1997, Least Concern in the IUCN action plan (Donaldson, 2003), which then estimated the population at 100,000 plants, and now Near Threatened. PlantZAfrica, citing the Red List of South African Plants (checked in 2016), states that the species, still widespread and common, is declining through collecting and habitat destruction, with a projected reduction approaching 30 % over three generations, and that KwaZulu-Natal's conservation service considers it dependent on protection in provincial nature reserves. Rousseau and colleagues cite collecting for local and foreign horticulture, overly frequent fires that kill seedlings, coastal development and farming; about 10 % of the Encephalartos sold at the Warwick medicinal plant market in Durban were attributed to this species (Cousins and colleagues, 2011). The 2017 South African Biodiversity Management Plan for Encephalartos (Government Gazette No. 40793) also names it among the species traded in traditional medicine markets.

Like all species of the genus, it has been listed in CITES Appendix I since 4 February 1977 and, in the European Union, in Annex A of the wildlife trade regulations (current list: Regulation (EU) 2026/1383), according to Species+. For artificially propagated plants from South Africa, the EU Scientific Review Group has held a positive opinion since 20 June 2011, limited to plants with a caudex no more than 15 cm in diameter; a new consultation covering the whole genus opened on 16 September 2026. Species+ also records past trade suspensions for Mozambique, no longer in force: CITES suspended all exports from 2012 to 2019 (lifted by Notification 2019/027), and the EU suspended imports of wild plants under decisions taken between 2008 and 2017. Check the current rules and ask for proof of cultivated origin before any purchase.

Growing Encephalartos ferox

Encephalartos ferox is common in the horticultural trade; it grows best in partial shade and needs plenty of water, but does not tolerate severe frost (PlantZAfrica). Jungle Music, in San Diego, recommends filtered light, as an understorey plant, in a rich but free-draining soil that never becomes waterlogged; it notes that plants right on the coast have done well with half a day of sun or more, and that the armed leaflets should be kept away from paths. Growth is slow: the plant sown in Durban in 1937 took twelve years to produce its first cone (Dyer, 1965).

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

Outdoors, the species needs a frost-free or almost frost-free climate. In southern California, Jungle Music grows it in the open and calls it a good garden plant and an attractive patio plant, given room for its leaves, which rise at first and then settle more horizontally. In Australia, the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria census lists it in outdoor beds at Melbourne Gardens. In France, plants grow in the ground on the harbour front at Le Lavandou (Var), where a member of the Fous de palmiers forum photographed them in January 2011 (the photograph above shows a plant grown at Le Lavandou); no temperature has been published for them. In the Gironde, a grower has kept one against a south-facing stone wall since 2010 (see Cold hardiness). For readers in comparable Mediterranean climates, these cases show that the species can be grown outdoors only in the mildest, best-sheltered sites.

Container cultivation

In cooler climates the species is a glasshouse or conservatory plant, as at the Palmengarten in Frankfurt, where the plant photographed above grows under glass. In Italy, a grower in Rome kept a young potted plant under a veranda closed on three sides (see Cold hardiness). A pot allows the plant to be moved under cover in winter, and the PlantZAfrica advice on shade and abundant summer water applies equally to containers; in winter the compost should be kept on the dry side, since root and trunk rot are the main risks in cold, wet soil.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In Britain, Kew, the Royal Botanic Garden Edinburgh, Oxford Botanic Garden, the Chelsea Physic Garden, Glasgow Botanic Gardens, RHS Garden Wisley, Paignton Zoo, the National Botanic Garden of Wales, Birmingham Botanical Gardens, Sheffield Botanical Gardens and the Millennium Seed Bank declare the species to BGCI PlantSearch, which shows that a garden has declared it at some point, not that a plant is alive today; in Britain cycads are grown almost exclusively under glass. In the United States, Montgomery Botanical Center lists twelve accessions in its online catalogue, two of them from 2018 according to their numbers (20180331 and 20180332), and Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden in Miami records it in its living collection; Lotusland in Montecito names it among the notable species of its cycad garden, and more than thirty other gardens, including the Huntington, the San Diego Botanic Garden, the San Diego Zoo, the Missouri Botanical Garden, Naples Botanical Garden, the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley and the United States Botanic Garden, declare it to BGCI. In Australia, the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney records it in its living collection, the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria grows it in outdoor beds at Melbourne, and Cairns, Adelaide, James Cook University in Townsville, Kings Park in Perth and Noosa declare it to BGCI. In Italy, the Naples Botanic Garden held five plants of South African and Mozambican origin in 1993–1994 and now shows female cones in its Califano glasshouses, and its seed list has offered pollen of the species.

Propagation

Encephalartos ferox is propagated from seed or by removing suckers (PlantZAfrica). Plants of both sexes must be in cone at the same time; in gardens, pollination has to be done by hand. PlantZAfrica describes the method:

  • collect pollen from the male cone; it keeps for many months in a freezer
  • when the scales of the female cone open very slightly, wash the pollen, mixed with distilled water, into the cone
  • to sort the seeds, soak them for two to three days, rub off the fleshy coat and put them in a bucket of water: the heavier fertile seeds sink, the infertile ones float
  • the embryo takes six to seven months to develop before germination, during which cleaned seed can be stored in a dry cupboard; a tuberous taproot appears first, and the first leaf about six weeks later

Seeds and plants fall under CITES Appendix I.

Pests and diseases

No disease specific to Encephalartos ferox is described. In the wild, caterpillars of geometrid moths (Zerenopsis, Diptychis, Paraptychodes) feed on its leaves (Rousseau and colleagues, 2015). In cultivation, as with other Encephalartos, the main risk is root and trunk rot in cold, waterlogged soil.

Cold hardiness

Encephalartos ferox is not a cold-climate plant: there is no frost in its range, and PlantZAfrica states that it does not tolerate severe frost. A few reports nevertheless show that young plants, with the caudex still underground, have survived hard frosts. As with all Encephalartos, such survivals often concern young plants protected by the thermal inertia of the soil, sometimes by snow; an adult with an exposed trunk has no such protection.

Mediterranean and European experience

  • A grower at Saint-Médard-de-Guizières (Gironde, south-western France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum, in July 2015, that he has grown a young Encephalartos ferox in the ground for five years, with a caudex barely bigger than a walnut, raised in a planter backed by dressed stone facing due south, the caudex surrounded by stones. It came through the cold spell of February 2012; it loses its leaves about one winter in three when he forgets to cover it with fleece and a small roof, and regrows each summer.
  • In April 2012, another forum member, whose locality is not given, reported that three seedlings planted in the ground the previous autumn had come through the February 2012 cold spell well, despite thirteen days without thaw and lows of −12 °C (10.4 °F). Without a precise location, this report is indicative only; it illustrates the better resistance of very young plants at ground level.
  • A grower in Rome reports on the Tropicamente forum that a young potted plant, left under a veranda closed on three sides, showed only tiny spots on its leaves after the cold of 17 December 2010, which he put at −8 °C (17.6 °F); another member doubted that the plant, sheltered by the wall, had really experienced that temperature.
  • Another Italian grower, without giving his locality, reported damage to Encephalartos ferox in full growth in December 2009 after a single night at −1 °C (30.2 °F) between two days close to 20 °C (68 °F).
  • On the Var coast, the Le Lavandou plants show that the species holds in the ground in the mildest sites, but no temperature has been published for them.

For readers in comparable climates (coastal California, southern Australia), these reports suggest that young plants can survive brief frosts in a sheltered spot, while adults should be kept frost-free.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

In southern California, Jungle Music states that a freeze of 25 °F (−3.9 °C) at its nursery caused no damage to the species, without giving the year or the age of the plants, and puts its cold tolerance in the mid to low 20s °F (roughly −4 to −7 °C), a nursery assessment. No USDA zone is published for the species by a botanical source. In Melbourne, the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria grows it in outdoor beds, but publishes no cold data. In Britain, where botanic gardens keep their cycads under glass, it is a plant for a frost-free glasshouse or conservatory.

Toxicity and precautions

The seeds of Encephalartos ferox are poisonous: PlantZAfrica speaks of “poisonous kernels”, spat out by animals that eat only the fleshy coat, and Fornasini already described the seeds as not good to eat in 1851. The risk is documented in the genus: the toxicity of Encephalartos horridus was demonstrated as early as 1944 (Van der Walt), and Encephalartos, like other cycads, contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a toxin affecting the liver and the nervous system; the seeds are the most dangerous part. The ASPCA reports vomiting, diarrhoea, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses that have eaten cycads.

Wear gloves to handle seeds, all the more since the leaflets are very prickly, and keep plants and seeds away from children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

According to Fornasini's letter published by Bertoloni (1851), the people of Mozambique ate the trunk and made a flour from it. PlantZAfrica describes, for South African cycads in general, the removal of the starchy pith, which was tied up in animal skins, fermented and then ground into meal; the Afrikaans name broodboom means “bread tree”. More recently the species was found on sale in a Durban medicinal plant market (Cousins and colleagues, 2011), where whole plants are collected from the wild.

Frequently asked questions

Is Encephalartos ferox frost-hardy?

No, apart from exceptions. The species never meets frost in the wild. Young plants at ground level survived the February 2012 cold spell in France, but adults have no such protection; outside the mildest coastal sites, grow it in a container kept frost-free in winter.

Why are its cones red?

It is a character of the species among southern African cycads. Cones range from salmon to scarlet, more rarely yellow; in subsp. emersus, from Mozambique, they are mostly yellow.

What is the difference between subsp. ferox and subsp. emersus?

The second has a trunk always above ground, 1 to 1.5 m tall, narrower leaflets and mostly yellow cones. It is known from a single population, on a floodplain north of Vilanculos, and is Critically Endangered.

Is Encephalartos kosiensis a different species?

No. It is the name given in 1932 to the Kosi Bay plants, later recognised as Encephalartos ferox. Rousseau and colleagues (2015) concluded that their peculiarities did not justify reviving the name.

Does it need a lot of water?

Yes, in the warm season. PlantZAfrica describes a species that needs plenty of water and prefers partial shade, in a free-draining soil. In winter the soil must stay sound to avoid rot.

Is it endangered?

It is Near Threatened: still common, it is declining through collecting and coastal development. Its subspecies emersus is Critically Endangered.

Can I buy one legally?

Yes, if the plant is cultivated and comes with the required CITES and, in Europe, EU documents. The species is in CITES Appendix I and in Annex A in the European Union.
