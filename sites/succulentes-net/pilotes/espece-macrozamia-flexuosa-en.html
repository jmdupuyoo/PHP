Macrozamia flexuosa is a small cycad endemic to the Hunter Region of New South Wales, in eastern Australia, where it grows in eucalypt forest from Bulahdelah south to Lake Macquarie and Wyong, and inland to Belford. This species of the genus Macrozamia has only an underground trunk, from which rise one to six erect leaves whose axis is twisted several times around itself, hence its name. Described by Charles Moore in 1884, it was long treated as a subspecies, Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi subsp. flexuosa, a name still found in older books. A study published in 2019 put its population at several million plants, and the IUCN now lists it as Near Threatened.

Key facts about Macrozamia flexuosa

  • Accepted name: Macrozamia flexuosa C.Moore (1884), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and the Australian Plant Census
  • Former names and synonyms: Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi subsp. flexuosa (C.Moore) L.A.S.Johnson (1959)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: curly cycad (Flora of Australia)
  • Natural range: Australia, New South Wales, Hunter Region: from Bulahdelah to Lake Macquarie and Wyong, inland to Belford
  • IUCN status: Near Threatened (NT), criteria B1ab(iii,v)+2ab(iii,v), assessment by S. Bell dated May 2020 and published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II, like the whole genus; Annex B in the European Union; not listed under New South Wales or federal threatened-species law
  • Documented hardiness: no dated cultivation report; about 28 frost days a year at Cessnock, in the natural range (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, eucalypt forest and woodland on siliceous soils, mostly on Permian sediments; in cultivation, not documented
  • Habit and adult size: underground trunk 8 to 20 cm across; 1 to 6 leaves 45 to 100 cm long (up to 12 to 14 leaves according to the PACSOA)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: three accessions of 2023 at the Montgomery Botanical Center (Florida) and eight other American gardens declared to BGCI; a wild-collected plant of 1980 in the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney; no British garden lists it
  • Toxicity: not studied for the species; seeds and young leaves are toxic throughout the genus (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Macrozamia flexuosa

It is recognised by its erect leaves, whose axis twists several times in a spiral, borne on a long, almost cylindrical petiole, and by its soft, mid-green, glossy leaflets with upturned margins. These are the distinguishing features given by the Flora of Australia (Hill, 1998), whose measurements are as follows:

  • Trunk: underground, 8 to 20 cm across;
  • Leaves: 1 to 6 in the crown, 45 to 100 cm long, moderately keeled, mid-green, semi-glossy to highly glossy; petiole 20 to 35 cm; axis strongly spirally twisted;
  • Leaflets: 80 to 150 per leaf, undivided, 17 to 30 cm long and 3 to 7 mm wide, clearly paler beneath, with more or less incurved margins and a tip that is not spiny; the lowest leaflets are not reduced to spines;
  • Male cones: spindle-shaped, about 15 cm long and 4.5 cm wide;
  • Female cones: ovoid, 10 to 16 cm long and 7 to 11 cm wide, with spines 5 to 35 mm long;
  • Seeds: ovoid, 24 to 27 mm long and 19 to 23 mm wide, with red flesh.

Craig Thompson and Paul Kennedy, two growers who studied the species in the field and wrote the account published by the Palm and Cycad Societies of Australia (PACSOA), add the following:

  • the leaves are 60 to 80 cm long on average, at most about 1 m; a plant usually carries 4 to 6, up to 12 to 14, and plants with only one to three leaves are not uncommon;
  • the axis usually makes 3 to 4 complete turns, from 2 to 6 depending on the leaf, while staying more or less straight and erect;
  • the leaflets point slightly forwards, at 45 to 50° to the axis, and seem to radiate in all directions because of the twisting;
  • young leaves are light green and turn mid-green; on some plants the petiole and axis of new leaves are reddish brown, and the callus at the base of the leaflets is pinkish red, before turning green and creamy white.

Charles Moore's original description (1884) already stressed the trunk “never raised above the ground”, leaves “usually spirally twisted, of a flexuous habit, about 2 feet long” and flexible leaflets about a quarter of an inch wide. The chromosome number is 2n = 18 (Johnson, 1963, cited by the Flora of Australia).

Hybrids

Macrozamia flexuosa hybridises naturally with two neighbouring species. PlantNET, the online flora of New South Wales, records hybrids with Macrozamia communis on the North Coast of the state. The PACSOA describes hybrids with Macrozamia reducta in the coastal suburbs of Newcastle, where the two species grow together, and near Cessnock, where a large stand of Macrozamia reducta separates two stands of Macrozamia flexuosa, with some obvious hybrids. These crosses are striking, because the other two species have a straight axis and lowest leaflets reduced to spines.

Possible confusion

The PACSOA groups it with three other New South Wales species whose leaf axis makes several turns: Macrozamia fawcettii and Macrozamia plurinervia, larger and more robust, with broader petioles, axes and leaflets, and Macrozamia concinna, smaller, to which it is the closest despite their taxonomic history. According to the Flora of Australia, Macrozamia concinna is told apart by its stiff, dark green leaflets and its shorter petiole (9 to 24 cm). The ranges of these species do not overlap: Macrozamia flexuosa is the only one that lives near the coast of the Hunter Region.

Stephen Bell (2019) points out another trap: young Macrozamia reducta and Macrozamia communis sometimes have a twisted axis and have been mistaken for Macrozamia flexuosa, which explains a few doubtful records of the species around Sydney. Adults of these two species have their lowest leaflets reduced to spines. Macrozamia spiralis, from the Sydney region, also has a slightly twisted axis; Johnson (1959) already noted that half-grown plants of it had often been taken for Macrozamia flexuosa, whose leaves turn through at least two complete revolutions and whose leaflets are concave.

Taxonomy

Macrozamia flexuosa C.Moore was published in the Journal and Proceedings of the Royal Society of New South Wales (volume 17, page 121), in a paper on the genus Macrozamia; The World List of Cycads and the Australian Plant Census date it to 1884, Bell (2019) to 1883. It belongs to the family Zamiaceae and has a single synonym.

In 1959, in his revision of the Australian Zamiaceae, L.A.S. Johnson reduced it to a subspecies of Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi, a species of the Queensland coast, under the name Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi subsp. flexuosa, one of three subspecies he recognised; he noted that the southern populations of his subspecies plurinervia showed a gradual approach to it. In 1991, after visiting the type localities of the three taxa, David Jones found that each forms isolated populations, tied to a particular habitat and with characters of their own, without the intermediate forms reported by Johnson, and concluded that the name Macrozamia flexuosa could be used at species rank (Jones, 1991). The Flora of Australia (Hill, 1998) adopted this rank, and Bell (2019) credits the change to that treatment. Bell also notes that the plant had first been regarded as a variety of Macrozamia spiralis.

For the reader, this means that Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi subsp. flexuosa and Macrozamia flexuosa are the same plant. The PACSOA and Bell place it in section Parazamia, characterised by a small habit, 1 to 15 leaves, thick veins raised on the underside of the leaflets, no mucilage canals and lowest leaflets not reduced to spines. The World List of Cycads does not use sections.

Etymology and history

The epithet comes from the Latin flexuosus, “zigzag” or “bent alternately in opposite directions”, referring to the spirally twisted leaves (Haynes, 2022, as given by The World List of Cycads). The PACSOA compares this sinuous axis to a coiled snake. The genus name joins the Greek makros, “large”, to the genus name Zamia.

Moore gave no specimen, only a locality: the plant “grows plentifully between Raymond Terrace and Stroud” and is, in his words, very different in character and habit of growth from any other species he knew. In 1959 Johnson took as holotype the only collection of that period and district in the Sydney herbarium: a sterile specimen gathered by Ernst Betche in January 1883 at Limeburners Creek, near Karuah, north of Newcastle (NSW 40951). The oldest record of the species found by Bell (2019) dates from 1881, near Limeburners Creek.

Common names

The Flora of Australia gives the English name “curly cycad”, which describes the twisted leaves; it is a flora name rather than a name in everyday use. No Aboriginal name is documented.

In the wild

Macrozamia flexuosa is locally common in the eucalypt forests of the lower Hunter Valley. The Flora of Australia describes it as scattered in sclerophyll forest on siliceous soils, in coastal districts from Bulahdelah to Lake Macquarie. The study by Stephen Bell (2019), based on 588 records, refines its range: from Bulahdelah to Wyong and inland to Belford, in nine local government areas, above all Cessnock (300 records). The species grows mostly on Permian sediments, but also on older (Carboniferous) and younger ground (Triassic Narrabeen sandstone, Quaternary deposits), under annual rainfall ranging from less than 700 to 1,400 mm.

According to Bell, it is found most often in Lower Hunter Spotted Gum–Ironbark Forest (41% of records), then in Kurri Sand Swamp Woodland (16%), Coastal Foothills Spotted Gum–Ironbark Forest (15%) and Coastal Plains Smooth-barked Apple Woodland (13%). In Lower Hunter Spotted Gum–Ironbark Forest, a community listed as endangered in New South Wales, it grows under Corymbia maculata and Eucalyptus fibrosa among prickly shrubs, sometimes with Macrozamia reducta. The PACSOA describes it near Cessnock on flat, poor clay soils, under a eucalypt canopy with a dense understorey of Bursaria.

The PACSOA gives the climate of Cessnock, at 62 m: 769 mm of rain a year over 102 days, more than 60% of it in summer and autumn; the temperature falls at least once a week to −0.4 °C (31.3 °F) in July and rises to 36.1 °C (97.0 °F) in January; frost occurs on 28 days a year on average.

The species mostly forms scattered single plants or small groups rather than dense stands. Bell notes that cones and seedlings appear scarce in many populations, and suggests limits in pollination or seed dispersal, masked by the longevity of the adults; no pollination study has been carried out on this species. As in other Macrozamia, the heavy seeds fall close to the parent plant; at Columbey, partly eaten seeds were found less than a metre from the adults. The PACSOA, by contrast, considers that the species cones irregularly, but probably more often than most inland Macrozamia: female cones are usually solitary, sometimes two, and male plants carry up to three. According to estimates cited by Bell, the plants may live 60 years or more.

Conservation status and regulation

Macrozamia flexuosa is Near Threatened (NT) on the IUCN Red List, in the assessment by Stephen Bell dated 1 May 2020 and published in 2022 (criteria B1ab(iii,v)+2ab(iii,v)), as given by The World List of Cycads, the Flora of Australia, GBIF and Crossref (checked on 26 October 2026).

This classification is recent. The previous IUCN assessment (Hill, 2010) classed it as Endangered (EN), on the basis of a supposed population of 2,500 to 10,000 adults threatened by collecting and clearing; six years earlier it had been judged Least Concern. In his 2019 review, Bell calculated an extent of occurrence of 6,319 km², an area of occupancy of 696 km² and a population of 1.74 to 43.5 million plants, present in at least 14 reserves, notably the Werakata, Columbey and Belford national parks. He proposed the NT category, stressing that the species may be in slow decline. Clearing, irreversible and still continuing outside reserves, remains the main threat. Again according to Bell, the species is listed neither under New South Wales law nor on the federal list; the Atlas of Living Australia shows no listing either (checked on 26 October 2026).

Like all species of the genus, it is listed in CITES Appendix II and, in the European Union, in Annex B of the wildlife trade regulations (Species+, checked on 26 October 2026). International trade in plants and seeds requires permits. Buy only plants raised in cultivation, with their documents, and check the regulations in force before any purchase or import.

Growing Macrozamia flexuosa

No published source describes the cultivation of Macrozamia flexuosa in detail. Its natural habitat and Australian field observations give a few pointers, with no guarantee of results.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Soil: siliceous soils, sandy to poor clay in the wild; a free-draining mix, low in lime, seems the safest choice.
  • Light: the plant lives under a eucalypt canopy; filtered light, or a bright position with a little shade in the hottest hours, seems closest to its habitat.
  • Water: the species grows under 700 to 1,400 mm of rain a year, falling mostly in summer and autumn; regular watering in summer and soil that is never waterlogged in winter are sound rules for Macrozamia. The PACSOA mentions a large stand growing in and around a town rubbish dump, which shows some tolerance of disturbed ground.
  • Space: with leaves up to 1 m long, the plant stays small.
  • Growth: not documented for the species; the small Macrozamia with an underground trunk are known to be slow.

No observation of the Jardin zoologique tropical in southern France has been published for this species.

Container cultivation

A trunk less than 20 cm across and a few leaves make container growing possible; a deep pot suits the taproot of cycads. No container-growing report has been published for the species.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

  • United States: the Montgomery Botanical Center, at Coral Gables in Florida, lists three accessions of 2023 (20230265 to 20230267) in its online catalogue. Eight other American gardens declare the species to BGCI: the Huntington, Lotusland, the Los Angeles County Arboretum, Descanso Gardens, the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley, Marie Selby Botanical Gardens in Sarasota, Phipps Conservatory in Pittsburgh and the Four Seasons Resort on Lanai, Hawaii.
  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney lists in its Garden Explorer (updated on 1 October 2026) a wild-collected plant accessioned in 1980 (S1980-0357), planted in bed 102i, and a second accession of 2024. Mount Annan, the Australian National Botanic Gardens in Canberra and the Adelaide Botanic Garden also declare it to BGCI.
  • United Kingdom: no garden declares it to BGCI, and no other source mentions it there.

Fifteen institutions in all declare it to BGCI, the other two being in China and France. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not necessarily a living plant on display today, and does not say whether it grows outdoors or under glass. The species does not appear in the census of Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria. No English-language grower's report has been found.

Propagation

Propagation is by seed. The plants being male or female, both sexes and, in cultivation, hand pollination are needed to obtain fertile seed. No germination data specific to the species has been published, and no source mentions offsets. According to estimates cited by Bell (2019), related species take 10 to 20 years to cone for the first time.

Pests and diseases

No problem specific to the species is documented in reliable sources. In Macrozamia grown in Europe, growers' forums mainly mention scale insects and rot in cold, wet soil (see the genus page).

Cold hardiness

No dated cultivation report exists for Macrozamia flexuosa, and no source gives a USDA zone for it.

Mediterranean and European experience

No planting in the ground is documented in Europe, and no recorded cold spell concerns the species. In 2014, on the French Fous de palmiers forum, a grower from the Ain and Hérault departments reported the opinion of an Australian nurseryman specialising in cycads, who ranked Macrozamia flexuosa among the “relatively hardy” species of the coast and ranges of New South Wales, with Macrozamia communis, after a first group of inland species judged hardier. This ranking gives no temperature.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No cultivation result has been published from Britain, North America or Australia. In the wild, at Cessnock, the species experiences about 28 frosts a year, with weekly winter lows close to 0 °C (32 °F) in July: a climate much milder in winter than that of the inland species. Any hardiness figure would be a guess, and this page gives none.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data specific to Macrozamia flexuosa has been published, but the risk is documented throughout the genus. Macrozamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a toxin of the liver; the young leaves cause irreversible paralysis of the hindquarters in cattle (McKenzie, 2006; see the genus page). Wear gloves to handle seeds, keep them out of reach of children, dogs and livestock, and contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay in case of ingestion.

Traditional uses

No use specific to this species is documented. Other Macrozamia of New South Wales provided Aboriginal peoples with edible seeds after lengthy treatment (see the genus page).

Frequently asked questions

Why are its leaves twisted?

It is a fixed character of the species, which gave it its name. The axis of each leaf usually makes three or four complete turns, so that the leaflets seem to point in every direction.

Is it a subspecies of Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi?

No, not any more. Johnson classed it that way in 1959, but Jones (1991) and then the Flora of Australia (1998) restored it as a species, under the name given by Charles Moore.

Is it endangered?

It has been Near Threatened (NT) on the IUCN Red List since 2022. A 2019 study estimated its population at several million plants, part of them in reserves, but clearing in the lower Hunter continues to reduce its habitat.

Does it hybridise with other Macrozamia?

Yes, with Macrozamia reducta around Newcastle and Cessnock, according to the PACSOA, and with Macrozamia communis on the North Coast, according to PlantNET. No published study describes these hybrids in detail.

How can it be told from Macrozamia concinna?

By the leaflets and the petiole. Macrozamia flexuosa has soft, mid-green, glossy leaflets and a petiole 20 to 35 cm long; Macrozamia concinna has stiff, dark green leaflets and a shorter petiole.

Is it cold-hardy?

Nobody knows. No grower has published a result, and its coastal habitat has only light frosts. A pot kept under cover in winter is the prudent choice.

Is it poisonous?

Very probably. No analysis concerns this species, but the seeds and young leaves of Macrozamia are toxic to people and animals.
