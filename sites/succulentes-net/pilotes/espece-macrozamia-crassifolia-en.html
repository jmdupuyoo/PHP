Macrozamia crassifolia is a small cycad from south-eastern Queensland, in eastern Australia, confined to two small areas near Mundubbera and Eidsvold, in the Burnett district, where it grows among granite rocks and boulders on rugged slopes under open forest. A species of the genus Macrozamia described in 1994, it forms a few erect, spirally twisted leaves on an underground stem that sometimes branches, with very narrow, long and thick dark green leaflets that droop in their outer third. It was first taken for an aberrant form of Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi. It is listed as Vulnerable.

Key facts about Macrozamia crassifolia

  • Accepted name: Macrozamia crassifolia P.I.Forst. & D.L.Jones (1994), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and the Australian Plant Census
  • Former names and synonyms: none; before its description, the informal name Macrozamia sp. (Mundubbera P.I.Forster 4674)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales; section Parazamia, Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi complex
  • Common names: no common name attested in English or any other language
  • Natural range: endemic to south-eastern Queensland, Australia: around Mundubbera and Eidsvold (Burnett district), at 340 to 420 m
  • IUCN status: Vulnerable (VU B1ab(iii,v)), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated 10 September 2020 and published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union; Vulnerable under Queensland law; listed as Vulnerable under the federal EPBC Act from 2000 to 2013
  • Documented hardiness: no cultivation data (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, rugged slopes among granite rocks, in sandy or stony soils, under open forest; in cultivation, not documented
  • Habit and adult size: underground stem 10 to 20 cm across, up to four stems per clump; 1 to 5 leaves 50 to 110 cm long per crown
  • In the UK, US and Australia: declared to BGCI by RHS Garden Wisley; not listed in the Sydney gardens or by Montgomery Botanical Center
  • Toxicity: not documented for the species; seeds and leaves toxic throughout the genus (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Macrozamia crassifolia

It is recognised by its very narrow (2 to 3.5 mm) but long leaflets, up to 55 cm, thick, deeply channelled, dark green above and bright green beneath, drooping in their outer third, and by the thick, felty brown wool that covers the leaf bases. The original description by Paul Forster and David Jones (1994) gives the following characters.

  • Stem: underground, more or less ovoid, 10 to 20 cm across, branching, with up to four stems in a clump.
  • Leaves: 1 to 5 per crown, erect, 50 to 110 cm long, dark green; leaf base covered in thick, brown, felty wool; petiole 5 to 26 cm long; axis twisted one to four times, dark green with white marks between the bases of the leaflets.
  • Leaflets: 104 to 172 per leaf, narrowly linear, 15 to 55 cm long and 2 to 3.5 mm wide, set at about 45° to the axis, dark green and dull above, bright green beneath, deeply channelled, thick, crowded (3 to 24 mm apart), drooping in the outer third; white to greenish-white base; the lowest leaflets are not reduced to spines.
  • Male cones: more or less cylindrical, 10 to 16 cm long and 3 to 4.5 cm across; the scales of the upper third carry stiff, pointed spines up to 1.3 cm long.
  • Female cones: more or less ovoid, green, 11 to 15 cm long and 6 to 8 cm across, on a stalk 10 to 28 cm long; the spines of the scales lengthen towards the top of the cone, the longest about 4 cm.
  • Seeds: oblong to ovoid, 1.9 to 2.6 cm long and 1.8 to 2.2 cm wide, with orange to red flesh.

The Flora of Australia gives leaflets 2 to 5 mm wide and describes the leaves as “highly glossy”, whereas the original description calls the leaflets dull above. It also gives an apical spine of 0 to 4 mm on the female cone scales, whereas the original description mentions spines of about 4 cm at the top of the female cone; one of the two figures is probably an error. WildNet, the Queensland Government's wildlife database, mentions up to five growing points in a clump.

Hybrids

No hybrid has been reported.

Possible confusion

The species belongs to the complex of Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi, a species of the coastal lowlands of the Wide Bay district, with very narrow, deeply channelled leaflets. Forster and Jones (1994) distinguish it by its much thicker, darker green leaflets, by the less conspicuous base of the leaflets, by the prominent spines at the top of the scales of the upper part of the male cone (very short or vestigial in Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi) and by its larger female cones. In their key, Macrozamia parcifolia, from the Biggenden area, differs by its finer, thin-textured leaflets, 1 to 3 mm wide, set at about 30° to the axis.

The Flora of Australia also sets Macrozamia fearnsidei, from central Queensland, beside Macrozamia crassifolia; its leaflets are longer and much wider (6 to 11 mm according to its original description). The PACSOA sums up the difference with Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi as “larger leaflets”.

Taxonomy

Macrozamia crassifolia P.I.Forst. & D.L.Jones is accepted by The World List of Cycads and the Australian Plant Census, with no synonym. It was published in 1994 in the journal Austrobaileya (vol. 4, no. 2, pp. 275–276), among seven new species of section Parazamia from south-eastern Queensland: small plants with an underground stem, usually twisted leaves, no lower leaflets reduced to spines and no mucilage canals in the leaflets.

The authors recognised two complexes in this section in Queensland. Macrozamia crassifolia belongs to that of Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi, whose species have very narrow, deeply channelled leaflets, together with Macrozamia parcifolia; the other complex gathers the species related to Macrozamia plurinervia, such as Macrozamia conferta.

The type, a male plant, was collected at Mundubbera on 10 January 1992 by Paul Forster and Peter Machin (Forster 9384B); the holotype is held at the Queensland Herbarium in Brisbane, in three sheets with dried and spirit-preserved cones, and an isotype at the Australian National Herbarium in Canberra.

Etymology and history

The epithet combines the Latin crassus, “thick”, and folium, “leaf”, in reference to the thick leaflets of the species (Forster and Jones, 1994; Haynes, 2022, as given by The World List of Cycads). The genus name Macrozamia combines the Greek makros, “large”, with the genus name Zamia.

Paul Forster first collected it in November 1984. It was then taken for an aberrant form of Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi, and later collections, from 1988 to 1993, showed that it was a distinct species.

Common names

No common name is attested for Macrozamia crassifolia, in English or in any other language; the Flora of Australia, WildNet and the Atlas of Living Australia give none. The scientific name is used.

In the wild

Macrozamia crassifolia lives in two small areas near Mundubbera and Eidsvold, in south-eastern Queensland, in small, scattered stands (Forster and Jones, 1994; Flora of Australia). It grows at 340 to 420 m among granite rocks and boulders on rugged slopes, in sandy or stony soils, under open forest dominated by Allocasuarina inophloia, several eucalypts (Eucalyptus dura, Eucalyptus petalophylla, Eucalyptus trachyphloia, Eucalyptus watsoniana) and Lysicarpus angustifolius. The localities recorded in WildNet span about 80 km from north to south.

The cones mature from October to January and the seeds ripen from February to April. Cross-pollination is carried out by a weevil of the genus Tranes, in an obligate mutualism (Forster et al., 1994, as cited by the Flora of Australia). According to WildNet, quoting the Queensland Herbarium (2007), ripe seeds are probably not ready to germinate for about twelve months, because fertilisation is delayed in cycads, and the plants may not cone every year when conditions are unfavourable.

Conservation status and regulation

Macrozamia crassifolia is listed as Vulnerable (VU) on the IUCN Red List under criterion B1ab(iii,v), which refers to a small range with a continuing decline in the quality of the habitat and in the number of mature plants. The assessment, by J.D. Bösenberg, is dated 10 September 2020 and was published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, GBIF and Crossref, checked on 26 October 2026). It is also listed as Vulnerable under Queensland's Nature Conservation Act, and WildNet flags it as a sensitive species.

According to WildNet, it was listed as Vulnerable under the federal Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act 1999 (EPBC) from 16 July 2000 to 15 May 2013, when it was removed from the list; the Atlas of Living Australia now shows only its Queensland listing. In 1994 the authors knew six populations, all on private land within two restricted areas, but locally common.

Like other Macrozamia, it has been listed in CITES Appendix II since 4 February 1977, with annotation #4: seeds and pollen are exempt, but plants are not. In the European Union it falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026 (Species+, checked on 26 October 2026). Check the regulations in force before any purchase or import, and buy only seed-raised plants with their papers.

Growing Macrozamia crassifolia

No published source describes the cultivation of Macrozamia crassifolia, in Australia or elsewhere. The Palm and Cycad Societies of Australia (PACSOA) advise, in a few words, a sunny, well-drained position. Its habitat suggests a sandy, very free-draining granitic soil and the bright light of open forest; these are only pointers. It is a small plant, suited to a pot, although its leaves can reach 1.1 m.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Light: sunny, according to the PACSOA; in the wild, open forest among rocks.
  • Soil: sandy or stony and very free-draining; in the wild, sandy soils over granite (Flora of Australia).
  • Water: no published data for the species.
  • Space: a clump of a few crowns, with leaves 50 to 110 cm long.

Container cultivation

No container culture has been described. A deep pot of free-draining, sandy mineral compost matches its habitat. A plant in a pot loses the protection that the soil gives to its underground stem in winter (see Cold hardiness).

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

  • United Kingdom: RHS Garden Wisley (Surrey) declares it to BGCI.
  • United States: no garden declares it to BGCI, and it is not in the online catalogue of Montgomery Botanical Center.
  • Australia: it is listed neither in the Garden Explorer of the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney nor in that of the Australian Botanic Garden Mount Annan, and no Australian garden declares it to BGCI.

In all, four institutions declare the species to BGCI (checked on 26 October 2026): RHS Garden Wisley, the Prague Botanical Garden, the Fairy Lake Botanical Garden in Shenzhen and Château Pérouse, in France. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not necessarily a living plant today. No English-language grower's report has been found.

Propagation

Macrozamia crassifolia is propagated by seed, as the plants are male or female; both sexes are needed and, in cultivation, hand pollination. As the stem can branch into a clump of up to four stems, division of a clump is possible in theory, but no source describes it for this species. According to WildNet, freshly ripe seeds probably need about a year before they can germinate. Wear gloves to clean the seeds, whose flesh is toxic.

Pests and diseases

No pest or disease specific to Macrozamia crassifolia is documented. For Macrozamia grown in Europe, growers' forums mainly mention scale insects and rot in cold, wet soil (see the genus page).

Cold hardiness

No cultivation result is documented for Macrozamia crassifolia, anywhere in the world.

Mediterranean and European experience

No result has been published in Europe, in the ground or in a pot. In 2014, a member of the French Fous de palmiers forum relayed the ranking of an Australian cycad specialist, who placed Macrozamia crassifolia among the “less hardy” species of inland Queensland, without giving a temperature.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No frost data have been published for its habitat, and no hardiness rating, even commercial, has been found. Any figure would be a guess, so none is given here. The declaration of RHS Garden Wisley to BGCI says nothing about the conditions in which the plant is kept. In Britain and in the colder parts of the United States, a pot kept frost-free in winter is the prudent choice.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data specific to Macrozamia crassifolia have been published, but the risk is well documented for the genus. Macrozamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a poison of the liver; cattle that eat the leaves can develop a locomotor disorder of the hind legs (Healy, 1967), which McKenzie (2006) describes as irreversible, ending in paralysis of the hindquarters, with no treatment.

Treat the plant as toxic: wear gloves to handle the seeds, keep them out of reach of children, dogs and livestock, and in case of ingestion contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use specific to Macrozamia crassifolia is documented. Other Macrozamia provided Aboriginal peoples with seeds that could be eaten after long processing (see the genus page).

Frequently asked questions

Why is it called crassifolia?

Because of its thick leaflets. The name combines the Latin words crassus, “thick”, and folium, “leaf”.

How can it be told from Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi?

By the thickness and colour of the leaflets, and by the cones. Its leaflets are thicker and darker, its male cones carry conspicuous spines at the top, and its female cones are larger.

Where does it grow?

Near Mundubbera and Eidsvold, in south-eastern Queensland. It lives at 340 to 420 m among granite rocks, under open forest.

Is it endangered?

It is Vulnerable. The IUCN (2022) and Queensland list it as Vulnerable; it was on the federal list of threatened species from 2000 to 2013.

Does it form clumps?

Yes, sometimes. Its underground stem can branch and carry up to four crowns of leaves.

Can it be grown in Britain or the United States?

Nobody knows yet. RHS Garden Wisley declares it, but no grower has published any experience, and an Australian specialist ranks it among the less hardy Macrozamia; a pot kept frost-free in winter is the prudent option.
