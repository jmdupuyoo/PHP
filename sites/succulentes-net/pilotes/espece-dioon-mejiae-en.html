Dioon mejiae is the only species of the genus Dioon that lives outside Mexico: it is a cycad of north-eastern Honduras, where it forms populations of tens of thousands of plants in the departments of Olancho, Yoro and Colón. Known there as tiusinte, it is one of the most widely eaten cycads in the Americas: its seeds, prepared by long traditional methods, still feed thousands of people. Long described as a small, rare plant from a single canyon, it turned out to be a tree-like Dioon whose trunks exceed 10 m. Listed as Endangered on the IUCN Red List since 2022, it is uncommon in cultivation and dislikes frost. Kew’s checklist treats it under the older name Dioon pectinatum, a choice not followed by The World List of Cycads (see Taxonomy).

Key facts about Dioon mejiae

  • Accepted name: Dioon mejiae Standl. & L.O.Williams (1950), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15), GBIF, Species+ and the 2020 revision of the genus; Kew’s World Checklist of Vascular Plants, the database behind POWO, accepts Dioon pectinatum instead (see Taxonomy)
  • Former names and synonyms: Dioon edule var. latipinnium Dyer (1884); Dioon pectinatum Mast. (1893), a doubtful name (nomen dubium) for The World List of Cycads, a synonym for the 2020 revision, the accepted name for Kew’s checklist
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: no English name is attested; tiusinte, teocinte or palma teosinte in Honduran Spanish, tiñik in the Tol language
  • Natural range: Honduras (Colón, Olancho, Yoro), at 100 to 500 m according to the 2020 revision, 120 to 1,000 m according to Haynes (2007); wild occurrence in Nicaragua not demonstrated
  • IUCN status: Endangered (EN A2acd+3cd+4acd; B1ab(i,ii,iii,iv,v)+2ab(i,ii,iii,iv,v)), assessment by J. Haynes and M. Bonta dated July 2020 and published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: no measured value; nurseries claim about −7 to −5 °C (“low 20s °F”, California) or −5 °C (23 °F) (Northern Territory, Australia); a frost-sensitive plant from frost-free lowlands (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: morning sun or strong filtered light according to a Californian nursery, sun or shade according to an Australian one; well-drained soil
  • Habit and adult size: trunks often multiple, more than 10 m tall on old plants; leaves 1 to 2 m long; very slow growth in the wild
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Royal Botanic Garden Edinburgh (catalogue), Kew and Glasgow (BGCI); Montgomery Botanical Center (23 accessions, 13 of them from 2003), Fairchild and some 25 other American gardens; Royal Botanic Garden Sydney (catalogue) and six other Australian gardens (BGCI)
  • Toxicity: to be treated as toxic, especially the raw seeds (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Dioon mejiae

Dioon mejiae is recognised by its long, flat leaves, its broad and numerous leaflets with smooth margins in adult plants, and its young leaves covered in soft wool. The taxonomic revision of the genus published in 2020 by Hernández-Tapia, Jiménez-Ramírez and Vovides describes it as follows:

  • Habit: plants 1 to 2 m tall or more; trunk about 30 cm (12 in) across.
  • Leaves: 11 to 14 per crown, 1 to 2 m long; the lowest leaflets are reduced to spines along the petiole.
  • Leaflets: 100 to 140 pairs, sickle-shaped, 1.3 to 1.6 cm wide, the middle ones 12.7 to 19.3 cm long; margins entire, rarely with one to three spines.
  • Male cones: cylindrical, 30 to 80 cm long.
  • Female cones: ovoid, 30 to 50 cm long and 20 to 30 cm across, on a stalk.
  • Seeds: globose, 4 to 5 cm across, with a cream to yellowish-white fleshy coat.

These dimensions understate the size of old plants. Jody Haynes, of the Montgomery Botanical Center, visited more than twenty populations with Mark Bonta in 2003: he describes a tree-like Dioon with multiple trunks exceeding 10 m, and many plants at least 36 ft (about 11 m) tall. The San Diego nursery Jungle Music describes rather slender trunks, under 12 in (30 cm), leaves about 6 ft (1.8 m) long and super-soft, woolly new leaves.

Hybrids

No natural or garden hybrid of Dioon mejiae has been documented.

Possible confusion

With Dioon spinulosum

This is the most similar species, with the same large, flat leaves and broad leaflets. According to the 2020 revision, Dioon spinulosum keeps 8 to 12 spines on the margin of each leaflet throughout its life, whereas the adult leaflets of Dioon mejiae have smooth margins; its seeds are ovoid to ellipsoid with a yellow coat, against globose and cream in Dioon mejiae. Both species carry basal spines on the petiole, so this character does not separate them. On the French Fous de palmiers forum, growers also point to the young leaves, hairless in Dioon spinulosum, woolly in Dioon mejiae (2009). Seedlings of Dioon mejiae have spiny leaflets, which makes young plants harder to name.

With Dioon rzedowskii

Dioon rzedowskii, from northern Oaxaca, also loses the spines of its leaflets with age. According to the 2020 revision, it has no basal spines on the petiole, whereas Dioon mejiae has them, and its seeds are ovoid to cylindrical. Its Mexican origin is enough to rule it out in the wild.

With Dioon edule

Dioon edule has much narrower leaflets (0.4 to 1.5 cm) and a trunk that rarely exceeds 1.5 m in the wild. Yet the oldest name attached to Dioon mejiae is Dioon edule var. latipinnium, “with broad leaflets” (see Taxonomy).

Taxonomy

Paul C. Standley and Louis O. Williams described Dioon mejiae in 1950 in the journal Ceiba (1: 36–38), from a plant cultivated in the garden of Dr Isidoro Mejía at Danlí (El Paraíso), at 750 m, collected on 17 February 1949 (Standley 16756). The original description places the type in the herbarium of the Chicago Natural History Museum (F), with a duplicate at the Escuela Agrícola Panamericana (EAP) in Honduras, as does the 2020 revision; The World List of Cycads gives a holotype in Washington (US). According to Haynes (2007), the description was incomplete and partly wrong: it knew only one wild population, gave the wrong village name and underestimated the size of the plant (a trunk “up to 1 m or more”). Haynes adds that it did not describe the reproductive organs; the 1950 text does in fact describe a female cone about 30 cm by 20 cm, but neither the male cone nor the seeds. Haynes and Bonta published an amended description in 2007.

Two older names are attached to the species. Dioon edule var. latipinnium, published by Thiselton-Dyer in 1884 in the Biologia Centrali-Americana, is based on a plant grown at Kew, of vague Mexican origin; The World List of Cycads and the 2020 revision treat it as a synonym. Dioon pectinatum, published by Maxwell Masters in 1893 in the Gardeners’ Chronicle, is known only from an illustration, chosen as lectotype by Stevenson and Sabato in 1986. The World List of Cycads regards it as a doubtful name (nomen dubium), impossible to attach with certainty to a species, which leaves Dioon mejiae as the correct name.

The 2020 revision, however, lists Dioon pectinatum as a synonym of Dioon mejiae; published in 1893, it would then have priority. Kew’s World Checklist of Vascular Plants, the database on which Plants of the World Online is based (June 2026 version, published on GBIF), draws that conclusion: it accepts Dioon pectinatum, native to Honduras, with Dioon mejiae and Dioon edule var. latipinnium as synonyms. This page follows The World List of Cycads, the reference list of the IUCN Cycad Specialist Group, as do GBIF’s backbone, Species+ and the IUCN Red List; a reader searching POWO may therefore find the plant under Dioon pectinatum.

According to the 2020 revision, which follows the phylogeny of Dorsey and co-authors (2018), Dioon mejiae belongs to the Spinulosum clade, with Dioon spinulosum and Dioon rzedowskii: the clade of the large Dioon with tall trunks and broad leaflets.

Etymology and history

The epithet honours Dr Isidoro Mejía H., a physician at Danlí who died in 1950, in whose garden the type was collected (Haynes, 2022, as given by The World List of Cycads). According to the 1950 description, this physician and surgeon had brought back, some forty years earlier, seeds from a dry, rocky canyon in Olancho, on the road from Pueblo Nuevo to Olanchito; the plants raised from these seeds, multiplied by their offsets (“hijos”), had spread to gardens in El Paraíso and Olancho.

The plant was known to Hondurans long before it was described. According to Bonta and co-authors (2019), it appears as teocinte and variants of that name in the earliest surviving land titles of the region, in the eighteenth century, then in inventories, agricultural censuses and travel accounts of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries; a carving on the eighteenth-century façade of the church at Gualaco is thought to represent it. The authors suggest that Nahua trading colonies, reported in the region by Hernán Cortés in the early sixteenth century, may have brought the name and some of the uses from Mexico.

According to Haynes (2007), the 1950 description kept alive for more than half a century the image of a rare species. Mark Bonta’s explorations from 2003, followed by the Montgomery Botanical Center expedition the same year, recorded at least 30 populations over about 10,000 ha and more than 650,000 adult plants: in Haynes’s words, the most abundant cycad in the New World.

Common names

No English common name is attested. In Honduras the species is called tiusinte (Haynes, 2007; Bonta and co-authors, 2019), teocinte in old documents and at the teocinte festival of Gualaco, and palma teosinte according to Species+ and the 2020 revision. Haynes (2007) gives tiñik in Tol, the language of the Tolupan people. Elsewhere, teosinte is the name of a wild grass related to maize (then placed in the genus Euchlaena); Standley and Williams already judged in 1950 that it was pointless to ask why the name had been given to the Dioon.

In the wild

Dioon mejiae is the only Dioon of Central America, more than 700 km from the nearest other species according to Haynes (2007). It grows in the departments of Olancho, Yoro and Colón. In Nicaragua, Standley had seen large cultivated plants at Managua as early as 1949 and young plants from Managua in a garden at Condega; Haynes (2007) likewise cites only garden plants, and the 2020 revision considers wild populations near the border probable but unconfirmed: wild occurrence there remains to be demonstrated.

The two main sources do not describe the same range of habitats. The 2020 revision places it in tropical deciduous forest, under a warm, humid to semi-dry climate, between 100 and 500 m. Haynes (2007), who visited the populations, describes habitats ranging from lowland wet forest to mixed deciduous forest and dry thorn scrub, between 120 and 1,000 m, with 800 to 2,000 mm (31 to 79 in) of rain a year, on soils of pH 6.3 to 8. The populations number from 6 to 150,000 plants, at densities of 10 to 240 plants per hectare; two of them exceed 100,000 plants.

The species appears to be the only host plant of the butterfly Eumaeus childrenae, and a weevil of the genus Rhopalotria, then undescribed, is thought to be its pollinator (Haynes, 2007). Adult plants survive fires, which are often lit for farming and grazing.

Conservation status and regulation

Dioon mejiae is listed as Endangered (EN) on the IUCN Red List, in the assessment by J. Haynes and M. Bonta dated 9 July 2020 and published in 2022, under criteria A2acd+3cd+4acd and B1ab(i,ii,iii,iv,v)+2ab(i,ii,iii,iv,v): an observed and expected population decline, and a restricted range in which habitat and plant numbers are decreasing (The World List of Cycads, GBIF and Crossref). This status follows other categories: “rare” or “vulnerable” in 1995, “rare” in 1997, data deficient in 2003; in 2007 Haynes even recommended Least Concern, given the numbers then discovered. The 2020 revision still quotes this last category, now superseded. Bonta and co-authors (2019) describe the main threat: ranchers destroy tiusinte in pastures by burning, cutting and poisoning, which sets them against the seed gatherers.

The species is listed in CITES Appendix II, like the whole genus since 4 February 1977, with annotation #4, which exempts seeds and pollen in particular. In the European Union it is listed in Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, whose current annexes are set by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, in force since 29 June 2026, according to Species+. Databases checked on 26 October 2026: check the regulations in force before buying, and prefer seed-grown plants from cultivation.

Growing Dioon mejiae

Dioon mejiae is uncommon in cultivation, and the published advice is scarce and sometimes contradictory:

  • Light: the Californian nursery Jungle Music recommends morning sun or strong filtered light; the PACSOA wiki (Australia) recommends a sunny position, and the Australian nursery Cycad International (Katherine, Northern Territory) stresses its adaptability to both sun and shade.
  • Soil: well-drained; PACSOA describes it as drought-tolerant and suited to a warm, fairly humid garden.
  • Growth: very slow in the wild and in Florida. At the Montgomery Botanical Center, a plant raised from seed collected in 1976 had about 60 cm of trunk when Haynes wrote his article, roughly 2 m (7 ft) per century; in the gardens of Juticalpa, Honduras, growth drops to about 70 cm per century (Haynes). Cycad International, in the tropical north of Australia, finds on the contrary that plants in cultivation produce more and larger leaves than wild ones and grow reasonably fast.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

In tropical and subtropical climates, Dioon mejiae can be planted out: it grows outdoors at the Montgomery Botanical Center in Florida and, according to Cycad International, makes a sizeable plant fairly quickly in tropical northern Australia. In Mediterranean Europe it is mainly seen in botanic gardens: at the La Concepción historical-botanical garden in Málaga (photograph of March 2024 on Wikimedia Commons), in the botanic garden of Valencia (photograph of May 2023 posted on the Fous de palmiers forum), in Palermo in the gardens of the Royal Palace (2018) and in the glasshouses of the Naples botanic garden. These sources do not say how the plants are wintered. No observation of the species has been published for the Jardin zoologique tropical at La Londe-les-Maures, in the Var.

Container cultivation

Outside frost-free climates, grow it in a large pot with a free-draining mix, moved in winter into a bright, frost-free place. Jungle Music points out that the woolly new leaves are fragile when they emerge, so they are best not handled.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

  • United Kingdom: the Royal Botanic Garden Edinburgh lists the species in its online Garden Explorer (October 2026), and the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, and Glasgow Botanic Gardens declare it to BGCI PlantSearch; all are glasshouse collections, and no outdoor cultivation is documented in Britain.
  • United States: the Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida) lists 23 accessions in its online catalogue, 13 of them from 2003, the year of its Honduran expedition, and eight from 2012; the Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden (Miami) lists it in its online catalogue. BGCI PlantSearch also lists some 25 other American gardens, including the Huntington, Lotusland, San Diego Botanic Garden, the San Diego Zoo, Naples Botanical Garden, Marie Selby Botanical Gardens, the National Tropical Botanical Garden (Hawaii) and conservatories such as Brooklyn, Denver and the New York Botanical Garden. A BGCI listing is a declaration by the garden, not proof that the plant is alive today.
  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney lists it in its online Garden Explorer; Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria, the System Garden of the University of Melbourne, Adelaide Botanic Garden, Cairns Botanic Gardens, Mackay Regional Botanic Gardens and James Cook University (Townsville) declare it to BGCI. Cycad International, at Katherine (Northern Territory), offers plants.

Propagation

Dioon mejiae is propagated from seed; a male and a female plant are needed to obtain fertile seeds. Since seeds are exempt from CITES, they are the simplest way to obtain plants of known origin. The plant also produces offsets: according to the 1950 description, the plants raised from Mejía’s seeds multiplied easily by their “hijos”, which spread the species through the gardens of the region. Haynes (2007) reports multiple trunks on old plants.

Pests and diseases

No disease specific to the species is documented. In the wild, the caterpillars of the butterfly Eumaeus childrenae feed on its leaves (Haynes, 2007). In cultivation the main risk is rot of the trunk and roots in a cold, wet mix.

Cold hardiness

No measurement of the cold tolerance of Dioon mejiae has been published. Its natural range, in the lowlands of Honduras, is frost-free, and the available opinions place it among the most cold-sensitive Dioon.

Mediterranean and European experience

No in-ground cultivation is documented in France. On the French Fous de palmiers forum, a grower considers Dioon spinulosum and Dioon mejiae the least hardy species of the genus, able in his view to take −2 °C (28 °F) if the cold does not last (2015): this is an opinion, not a dated observation. On the Italian Tropicamente forum, a grower shows a plant from a 1992 sowing that he protects every winter because of its tropical origin (2009); in the same thread, others find unconvincing the −4 to −5 °C (25 to 23 °F) tolerances claimed by some websites. Plants are visible in the gardens of Málaga and Palermo, but the sources say nothing of the minimum temperatures they have met. For readers in comparable Mediterranean-type climates, the European experience suggests treating the species as frost-sensitive, at least as much as Dioon spinulosum.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

  • California: Jungle Music claims cold hardiness to about −7 to −5 °C (“into the low 20’s F”); this is a commercial claim, without conditions or dates.
  • Australia: Cycad International (Katherine, Northern Territory) writes that the species “has been reported as surviving temperatures down to −5 °C” (23 °F), without saying where or on what size of plant; it recommends tropical and subtropical climates, perhaps temperate ones.
  • United Kingdom: no report exists; the species needs a heated glasshouse in Britain.

No dated report from a British, American or Australian grower has been found. Given the frost-free habitat, these nursery figures should be treated with caution.

Toxicity and precautions

Dioon mejiae should be treated as toxic, especially the raw seeds. Cycads contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a toxin affecting the liver and the nervous system. In Honduras the seeds are eaten only after long traditional preparation; Bonta and co-authors (2019) collected no account of lasting neurological effects among consumers, but stress that the health effects of cycads remain poorly known. These preparations should not be copied. Wear gloves to handle the seeds, and keep plants and seeds out of reach of children and animals. If any part has been eaten, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

Tiusinte is one of the most widely eaten cycads in the Americas. According to Haynes (2007), its seeds are an important food resource for 30,000 to 40,000 people. Bonta and co-authors (2019), who interviewed people at Gualaco (Olancho), describe these uses:

  • Harvest: tall plants are climbed using notches cut long ago into the trunk, and the female cones are detached on the spot; the gatherers, the tiusinteros, sometimes sell the seeds, which are also found in the market at Olanchito.
  • Food: after detoxification, the seeds give a dough for long, cylindrical tamales and for tortillas; at Río Grande (Gualaco), a sun-bleached flour is used for rosquetes and breads sold at the teocinte festival; dried, the seeds keep and can be prepared all year round.
  • Value: the Honduran mestizos who eat them rate these foods above even those made from maize, and tiusinte is grown together with maize.
  • Religion: during Holy Week, tiusinte dishes are served and the leaves decorate the Stations of the Cross; at Punuare (Olancho), plants in the cemetery supply the leaves for the wreaths of the Day of the Dead.
  • Other uses: toys carved from the seed shells (whistles, cup-and-ball games); at Esquipulas del Norte, tiusinte is part of local heritage.

The 1950 description already recorded these uses: people boiled and ground the large brown seeds, “like chestnuts”, to make a kind of tortilla, and the leaves, sought after for decorating altars and funeral wreaths, sold at a fairly high price; the authors were told that the wild plants of the only canyon then known were protected by the government. The 2020 revision confirms ornamental use, leaves for religious festivals and funeral wreaths, and seeds for tortilla flour.

Frequently asked questions

Can Dioon mejiae be grown outdoors?

Only in frost-free climates. It grows outdoors in southern Florida and tropical northern Australia, but no in-ground success is documented where frost occurs. Elsewhere, grow it in a pot and move it to a bright, frost-free place in winter.

Does it grow in Nicaragua?

This has not been demonstrated. Standley saw cultivated plants there as early as 1949, Haynes (2007) cites only garden plants, and the 2020 revision considers wild populations near the Honduran border possible but unconfirmed.

Why is it sometimes called Dioon pectinatum?

Because Kew’s checklist, on which POWO is based, treats the 1893 name Dioon pectinatum as the correct name for this plant. The World List of Cycads, followed here, considers that name doubtful, as it rests only on an old illustration, and keeps Dioon mejiae.

How tall can it grow?

More than 10 m in the wild, often on multiple trunks, but over several centuries. At the Montgomery Botanical Center in Florida, it grows about 2 m of trunk per century.

How can it be told from Dioon spinulosum?

By the margins of the adult leaflets, smooth in Dioon mejiae, edged with 8 to 12 spines in Dioon spinulosum. The woolly young leaves and the globose seeds with a cream coat also help.

Are its seeds edible?

In Honduras, yes, but only after a long traditional preparation meant to remove the toxins. Raw, they are toxic: do not eat them.

Is it endangered?

Yes. Despite its large populations, it has been listed as Endangered by the IUCN since 2022, because of population decline and the destruction of plants in pastures. It is protected by CITES (Appendix II).
