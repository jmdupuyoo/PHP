Encephalartos chimanimaniensis is a tree-like cycad of south-eastern Africa, restricted to the Chimanimani Mountains, a quartzite massif straddling the border between Zimbabwe and Mozambique. This species of the genus Encephalartos, with glossy green leaves and very long, loose male cones, was described in 1969 from plants grown in a Zimbabwean garden. Often said to be extinct in the wild, it is in fact listed as Endangered (EN) by the IUCN, and fragmented wild populations were mapped in 2016 and 2017.

Key facts about Encephalartos chimanimaniensis

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos chimanimaniensis R.A.Dyer & I.Verd. (1969), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15)
  • Former names and synonyms: no synonym; the plants were previously included in Encephalartos manikensis in the broad sense
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Chimanimani cycad (English, Species+ and Flora of Zimbabwe)
  • Natural range: Chimanimani Mountains, Mozambique (Manica province) and Zimbabwe (Manicaland)
  • IUCN status: Endangered (EN), B1ab(i,ii,iii,iv,v)+2ab(i,ii,iii,iv,v), assessment published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I (whole genus); Annex A in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: a single, incomplete report of damage after one night at −1 °C (30.2 °F) in southern Italy in December 2009 (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, grassland on quartzite; in cultivation, not documented
  • Habit and adult size: trunks branching from the base, up to 1.75 m tall and 45 cm across; leaves about 1.5 m long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: declared to BGCI by the Huntington, San Diego Botanic Garden, San Diego Zoo and two other American gardens; no British or Australian record
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; treat it as poisonous like all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos chimanimaniensis

Look first at the male cones: long (up to 70 cm), with loose scales that leave the axis visible, they look nothing like the female cones, unlike those of Encephalartos manikensis. The original description by Robert Allen Dyer and Inez Verdoorn (1969) gives these characters:

  • Trunks: stout, branching from the base (rarely single), up to 1.75 m tall and 45 cm in diameter, covered in leaf bases and woolly bracts
  • Leaves: about 1.5 m long, oblong-elliptic in outline, tapering gradually to the base; rachis almost straight to arching, woolly when young
  • Leaflets: bright glossy green, inserted 2 to 3.5 cm apart, twisted and arranged like an open Venetian blind; middle leaflets ovate-lanceolate, slightly sickle-shaped, 15 to 20 cm long and 2.5 to 3 cm wide, often with two to four small teeth towards the base of both margins, the tip sometimes hooked; the lowest leaflets reduced to prickles
  • Male cones: up to three together, subcylindrical, up to 70 cm long and 8 to 11 cm wide, on a stalk about 15 cm long; scales loose, their fertile part channelled, the swollen tip recalling, according to the authors, a tortoise's head
  • Female cones: about 45 cm long and 18 to 20 cm wide, on a stalk 7 to 8 cm long; scale tips 5.5 to 6.5 cm wide, strongly wrinkled when dry
  • Seeds: red, apparently with little flesh, 3.7 to 4 cm long and 2 to 2.5 cm across

In their key based on leaves, the authors relied on the long leaflets (15 to 22 cm), well spaced and few-toothed, and on the rather elliptic outline of the leaf. They warned that this vegetative key “is by no means infallible” and that male cones remain the best guide.

Hybrids

No hybrid of Encephalartos chimanimaniensis has been described. The origin of seed offered in the trade is rarely documented, so the purity of seed-raised plants cannot be taken for granted.

Possible confusion

The species belongs to the “Encephalartos manikensis complex”, a group of cycads from the borderlands of Zimbabwe and Mozambique whose foliage is very similar. In 1969 Dyer and Verdoorn separated four species from it: Encephalartos pterogonus, Encephalartos concinnus, Encephalartos chimanimaniensis and Encephalartos munchii. The differences lie mainly in the male cones:

  • Encephalartos manikensis has compact male cones, with scales almost as broad as long, that look like the female cones
  • Encephalartos pterogonus has very compact male cones whose scales carry wing-like lateral angles
  • Encephalartos concinnus has compact male cones with narrow, neat scales overlapping “like tiles”
  • Encephalartos munchii has loose male cones like Encephalartos chimanimaniensis, but its scales are thinner and spread almost at right angles, and its leaflets are glaucous, crowded and held up in a V

Without cones, the authors admitted, telling these plants apart remains uncertain.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos chimanimaniensis is an accepted name with no synonyms. Robert Allen Dyer and Inez Verdoorn, of the Botanical Research Institute in Pretoria, published it in 1969 in the journal Kirkia (7: 147–158; description on pp. 154–155, figs 13–15), in a paper on “Encephalartos manikensis and its near allies”. The type comes from the Mozambican side of Mount Chimanimani (Munch 443, 444, 445); the description names the herbarium in Harare (SRGH) as holding the holotype, whereas The World List of Cycads places it in Pretoria (PRE) and cites the numbers 443, 444 and 554. The discrepancy could not be resolved.

The authors had seen no plants in the wild. They worked from plants in the large rock garden of R. C. Munch at Mona farm near Rusape, from herbarium specimens and from the field notes of several naturalists. They also recorded two unnamed forms: two plants seen in 1965 in the south of the massif, on the Zimbabwean side, at about 3,900 ft (about 1,190 m), which they thought might deserve subspecies rank, and a population in the Chipinge district with smaller scales. Neither form has been described since, according to The World List of Cycads, the checklist of the IUCN Cycad Specialist Group, which accepts the species in its version of 15 August 2026.

Etymology and history

The epithet chimanimaniensis means “of the Chimanimani”, recalling that the species is endemic to this massif (Haynes, 2022, cited by The World List of Cycads).

The history of the species is tied to R. C. Munch, a farmer of the Rusape area who gathered cycads from the nearby mountains in his garden and who prompted the Pretoria botanists to reopen the study of the Encephalartos manikensis complex in 1966. The type plant in his garden came from the eastern slope of Mount Chimanimani, on the Mozambican side. In their conclusion, Dyer and Verdoorn already named people as the greatest threat to wild Encephalartos, because of the worldwide demand for large plants for gardens.

Common names

The English name Chimanimani cycad is used by Species+ and by the online Floras of Zimbabwe and Mozambique. No name in a local language is documented.

In the wild

Encephalartos chimanimaniensis is confined to the Chimanimani Mountains, in Zimbabwe (Manicaland) and Mozambique (Manica province). The Floras of Zimbabwe and Mozambique give its habitat as grassland on quartzite. The exact altitudes and climate of its sites are not published, apart from the form recorded at about 1,190 m in 1965.

Its status in the wild was long unclear. The Flora of Zimbabwe, citing in particular the Zimbabwean plant red list of 2002, says it was known from a single site in one river catchment, could not be found during recent searches and is “considered extinct”; the 2010 management plan for the Chimanimani transfrontier conservation area lists it as extinct in Zimbabwe. Surveys carried out in 2016 and 2017 in the Chimanimani and Chipinge districts, with support from the Rufford Foundation, nevertheless mapped the remaining wild populations. Their report describes subpopulations badly fragmented by the removal of adult plants, female plants isolated from males, cones that no longer ripen at the same time and therefore almost no natural pollination, and low genetic diversity. The pollinators of the species have not been studied.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos chimanimaniensis is listed as Endangered (EN) on the IUCN Red List (assessment by J. D. Bösenberg, published in 2022), under criteria B1ab(i,ii,iii,iv,v)+2ab(i,ii,iii,iv,v): a very small range and area of occupancy, fragmented populations and a continuing decline in range, habitat, number of sites and number of adults. It is therefore not classed as Extinct in the Wild, contrary to what some websites still state. The Red List itself could not be consulted directly; the category and criteria are those recorded by The World List of Cycads.

Like the whole genus, it has been listed in CITES Appendix I since 4 February 1977, so plants, seeds and pollen are all covered, and in the European Union it is in Annex A of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, according to Species+, the official CITES and EU database, consulted in October 2026. Plants sold must come from cultivation and carry the required documents; check the current rules before any purchase or import.

Conservation outside the wild remains modest. According to the presentation of the project supported by the Rufford Foundation, the main herbaria of Zimbabwe held fewer than eight mature plants between them; a nursery was set up at the National Herbarium and Botanic Garden in Harare from seed, suckers and stem cuttings, but too few plants had been produced by 2018 to consider reintroduction.

Growing Encephalartos chimanimaniensis

No published source describes the cultivation of Encephalartos chimanimaniensis in detail, and the advice below is deduced from its habitat and from the few observations available. The species comes from mountain grassland on quartzite, a rock that gives sandy, poor, free-draining soils, so a mineral compost without standing water is the logical choice.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

Dyer and Verdoorn studied plants that grew and coned in a rock garden at Rusape, in Zimbabwe, which shows that the species can be grown in the ground in a tropical upland climate. No outdoor planting is documented in Europe, North America or Australia with growing details. Given the single report of damage after a light frost (see Cold hardiness), planting out is only realistic in frost-free gardens.

Container cultivation

In cooler climates, the species is a container plant to be kept frost-free in winter. A grower in Salento (Apulia, Italy) showed the summer's new leaves of his plants on the Tropicamente forum in October 2009, without details of how they were grown. On the French Fous de palmiers forum, a collector showed a young plant in growth in 2010 and 2011, and a German nursery offered seed in 2013, with no reliable information on its origin.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

The species is rare in public collections. The Huntington and San Diego Botanic Garden and San Diego Zoo in California, together with two other American gardens, report it to BGCI PlantSearch, which shows that a garden has declared the species at some point, not that the plant is alive today. It is not in the online catalogue of Montgomery Botanical Center in Florida, and no British or Australian botanic garden has been found listing it.

Propagation

The species is propagated from seed and, as the Harare project did, from suckers and stem cuttings. The project compared how well these three types of material established, but its figures are not published in its reports. Seed must come from cultivated, hand-pollinated plants.

Pests and diseases

The only documented problem concerns the Harare nursery: many young plants there were attacked by a weevil, which caused some of them to wilt, and others rotted. The team adjusted watering and checked collected material before bringing it into the nursery, since infested plants passed the weevils on to the others. The weevil species is not named.

Cold hardiness

No precise measurement of the cold tolerance of Encephalartos chimanimaniensis exists. The only report found describes damage after a light frost.

Mediterranean and European experience

A grower in Salento (Apulia, Italy) reports on the Tropicamente forum that in December 2009 a single night at −1 °C (30.2 °F), between two days with highs close to 20 °C (68 °F), caused “quite a lot of damage” to several of his Encephalartos, including Encephalartos chimanimaniensis, Encephalartos ferox and Encephalartos concinnus; he does not say whether they were in pots or in the ground, how old they were or whether they were protected. In December 2008 the same grower had written, from his experience with “fairly large and thriving” plants, that the group formed by Encephalartos concinnus, Encephalartos chimanimaniensis, Encephalartos pterogonus, Encephalartos munchii, Encephalartos tegulaneus, Encephalartos villosus, Encephalartos ferox and Encephalartos whitelockii has no chance of surviving a frost. No in-ground trial is documented in France. For gardeners in comparable Mediterranean climates, these observations argue for frost-free winter protection.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No published source gives the lowest temperatures reached at the species' sites in the Chimanimani Mountains, and no English-language growing report with a measured value has been found. Survival in cold conditions among Encephalartos often concerns young plants whose caudex is still underground, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil; an adult with a trunk above ground has no such protection. No USDA zone is published for the species by a botanical source. In Britain it can only be grown under glass; in the United States it is held by gardens in coastal southern California, which publish no cold data for it.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data are published for Encephalartos chimanimaniensis itself, but the risk is documented in the genus: the toxicity of Encephalartos horridus was demonstrated as early as 1944 by Van der Walt, and Encephalartos, like other cycads, contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; the seeds are the most dangerous part. The ASPCA reports vomiting, diarrhoea, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses that have eaten cycads.

Treat the plant as poisonous: wear gloves to clean seeds, pick up any that fall, and keep plants and seeds away from children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use of Encephalartos chimanimaniensis is documented. The threats described are the removal of plants for trade and the degradation of habitats.

Frequently asked questions

Is Encephalartos chimanimaniensis extinct in the wild?

No, according to the most recent data. The IUCN has listed it as Endangered since the assessment published in 2022, and very fragmented wild populations were mapped in 2016 and 2017. Some sources, including the Flora of Zimbabwe, still describe it as extinct on the basis of older information.

How do I tell it from Encephalartos manikensis?

Mainly by the male cones. In Encephalartos chimanimaniensis they are long and loose, with the axis visible between the scales; in Encephalartos manikensis they are compact and look like the female cones. The leaflets of Encephalartos chimanimaniensis are also longer and more widely spaced, but this character alone is not reliable.

How big does it get?

It forms trunks branching from the base, up to 1.75 m tall and 45 cm across, crowned with leaves about 1.5 m long. Its male cones can reach 70 cm.

Is it frost-hardy?

No, according to the only report available: an Italian grower saw damage after a single night at −1 °C (30.2 °F). Grow it outdoors only in frost-free gardens; elsewhere keep it in a pot under glass in winter.

Where does its name come from?

From its only home, the Chimanimani Mountains on the border between Zimbabwe and Mozambique. The botanists Dyer and Verdoorn named it in 1969.

Can I buy one legally?

Yes, if the plant was propagated in cultivation and carries the CITES documents required for a species in CITES Appendix I and, in the European Union, Annex A. Seed in the trade is rare and its origin is seldom documented.

Is it poisonous?

It must be assumed so. No data exist for the species, but Encephalartos seeds contain toxins dangerous to people and animals.
