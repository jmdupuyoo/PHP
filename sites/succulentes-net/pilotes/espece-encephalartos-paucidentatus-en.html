Encephalartos paucidentatus, the Barberton cycad, is a large tree-like cycad of the mountains of eastern Mpumalanga, South Africa, around Barberton, and of the neighbouring mountains of the Piggs Peak district in north-western Eswatini. This species of the genus Encephalartos, described in 1926, can form a trunk about 6 m tall crowned with long, glossy dark green leaves whose narrow leaflets have few or no teeth, hence its name. South African botanists and gardeners count it among the finest garden cycads. It grows in low forest and mountain bush on steep, rocky slopes, and the IUCN assesses it as Vulnerable (assessment published in 2022): illegal collecting has greatly reduced its populations. Plants have survived unprotected, though defoliated, winter lows of −13 to −16 °C (9 to 4 °F) at a botanic garden in North Carolina.

Key facts about Encephalartos paucidentatus

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos paucidentatus Stapf & Burtt Davy (1926), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15), Species+ and GBIF
  • Synonym: Encephalartos altensteinii var. paucidentatus Regel (1874), according to The World List of Cycads
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Barberton cycad (PlantZAfrica, Species+)
  • Natural range: South Africa (eastern Mpumalanga, Barberton mountains) and Eswatini (Piggs Peak district)
  • IUCN status: Vulnerable (VU), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated 12 May 2020 and published in 2022; already Vulnerable in the IUCN action plan of 2003
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I since 1977, like the whole genus; Annex A in the European Union, with a positive EU opinion on artificially propagated plants from South Africa whose caudex does not exceed 15 cm
  • Documented hardiness: plants defoliated but alive after a winter with lows of −13 to −16 °C (9 to 4 °F), in the ground and unprotected, in North Carolina (2013–2014); PlantZAfrica recommends a frost-free position (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, low forest and mountain bush on steep, rocky slopes, up to 1,800 m, with 1,250 to 1,500 mm of rain a year; in cultivation, light shade or sun and a moist, well-drained soil
  • Habit and adult size: trunk up to about 6 m tall and 40 to 70 cm in diameter; leaves 1.5 to 2.5 m, crown about 5 m across; male cones 40 to 60 cm, female cones 35 to 50 cm
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Kew (BGCI); Fairchild, the Huntington, Lotusland, the San Diego Botanic Garden, the San Diego Zoo, Descanso Gardens, the Fullerton Arboretum and three Hawaiian gardens in the United States; Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria and the University of Melbourne's System Garden in Australia (BGCI); Juniper Level Botanic Garden (outdoor trial, 2013–2014)
  • Toxicity: seeds toxic, as in all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos paucidentatus

Look for a large trunk, a broad, graceful crown of glossy dark green leaves and, above all, long, narrow leaflets, often slightly recurved, with up to about thirty prominent veins on the underside, a character Dyer (1965) considers distinctive among related species. Dyer's revision and the PlantZAfrica fact sheet give the following characters:

  • Trunk: single or sometimes branching at the base, upright or reclining, up to about 6 m tall and 40 to 70 cm in diameter, with a brown woolly crown
  • Leaves: 1 to 2 m long according to Dyer, 1.5 to 2.5 m according to PlantZAfrica, fairly straight or slightly arching; young leaves covered in thick brownish hairs that are gradually lost; petiole straight and yellow
  • Leaflets: becoming gradually smaller towards the base of the leaf, where they are lobed and then reduced to prickles; the median ones straight or slightly curved, 15 to 25 cm long and 2 to 3.2 cm wide, with some twenty to thirty prominent veins beneath
  • Male cones: one to five per trunk, subcylindrical, yellowish, 40 to 50 cm (up to 60 cm) long and 12 to 15 cm wide, on a stalk of about 10 cm; the scales end in a curved beak (Dyer, 1972)
  • Female cones: one to three per trunk (up to five according to PlantZAfrica), oblong, 35 to 50 cm long and 20 to 25 cm wide, on a short, thick stalk; scales warty
  • Seeds: red, about 4 cm long and 2.5 cm wide

The cones are at first covered with brown hairs, which are partly shed with age. The male cones are much slimmer than the female cones (Dyer, 1972), and pollen is shed in early winter in the southern hemisphere (PlantZAfrica). Jungle Music, a nursery in southern California, draws attention to the “frosted” look of the leaves, caused by small hairs on the leaflets, and notes that new leaves tend to stand upright and that stems branch only occasionally, usually after injury.

Hybrids

No natural hybrid of Encephalartos paucidentatus is documented. Dyer (1972) considered whether Encephalartos heenanii, discovered in 1969 about 1.5 km from a population of Encephalartos paucidentatus, might be a hybrid between this species and Encephalartos laevifolius, which grows a little further away; he found no evidence for this and described it as a species in its own right. In collections, where several large green species are grown together and pollinated by hand, seed of unknown origin may nevertheless be hybrid.

Possible confusion

The closest species is Encephalartos heenanii, from the same Piggs Peak area. According to Dyer (1972), Encephalartos heenanii branches more at the base, has smaller trunks (up to 2.25 m), shorter leaves, leaflets that are not recurved and cones densely covered in shaggy brown wool; its male and female cones are so alike that they can hardly be told apart, whereas in Encephalartos paucidentatus the male cones are much slimmer than the female ones.

Dyer (1965) also compares it with Encephalartos transvenosus, the Modjadji cycad of Limpopo, with which medium-sized plants can be confused: the leaflets of Encephalartos transvenosus are broader in relation to their length, curve back from the rachis and lack the prominent veins on the underside. The plants of Kaapsehoop, near Barberton, belong to Encephalartos laevifolius, a very different species from that of the mountains above Barberton where Encephalartos paucidentatus grows (Dyer, 1965). Jungle Music also notes the resemblance to Encephalartos heenanii and Encephalartos transvenosus; its remark that their localities “intersect” is not supported by Dyer, who places Encephalartos transvenosus in Limpopo.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos paucidentatus was described by Otto Stapf and Joseph Burtt Davy in 1926 in Burtt Davy's Manual of the flowering plants and ferns of the Transvaal with Swaziland (1: 40 and 99, fig. 4a). The World List of Cycads gives as type a collection by I.B. Pole Evans (PRE 5185) attributed to the Zoutpansberg district (holotype at Pretoria). Dyer (1965) explains that this provenance caused confusion: the species had first been reported from the farm Breslau, north-west of the Soutpansberg, but every attempt to confirm this failed, and he had no doubt that it was restricted to the Barberton area and neighbouring Eswatini.

The World List of Cycads cites as a synonym Encephalartos altensteinii var. paucidentatus Regel, published in 1874 in St Petersburg. The published sources do not say on which plants this old name was based. The accepted name has not changed since 1926.

Etymology and history

The epithet comes from the Latin paucus, “few”, and dentatus, “toothed”: it refers to the leaflets with few teeth and almost smooth margins of the plants studied for the description (Haynes, 2022, quoted by The World List of Cycads; PlantZAfrica).

According to PlantZAfrica, summarising Dyer (1965), the discovery is probably due to George Thorncroft of Barberton and the Reverend Rogers, who sent a plant to the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, and alerted botanists; Thorncroft was then in regular correspondence with Burtt Davy and Pole Evans. Dyer (1965) records that, in cooperation with the Transvaal nature conservation service, residents of Barberton created the Ida Doyer Nature Reserve, which protects fine specimens. In 1972 Dyer published the first description of fresh male and female cones, from plants in the Piggs Peak area observed by Denis and David Heenan, who found there female trunks with up to three large cones and male trunks with up to five.

Common names

PlantZAfrica and Species+ give the English name Barberton cycad, after the town around which the species grows. No other name has been published.

In the wild

Encephalartos paucidentatus lives in the mountains of eastern Mpumalanga, in a small area around Barberton, and in the neighbouring mountains of the Piggs Peak district, in north-western Eswatini (PlantZAfrica). Dyer (1965) describes it as fairly scattered in low forest and mountain bush; in mountain ravines near Piggs Peak it is sometimes common (Dyer, 1972).

According to PlantZAfrica, the species grows up to 1,800 m, with 1,250 to 1,500 mm of rain a year, in cool, moist conditions, in low forest on steep, rocky slopes, in semi-shade. Since cycads are pollinated by wind and insects, the activity of weevils, which increases sharply while the cones are forming, suggests that they take part in pollination; PlantZAfrica presents this as a hypothesis.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos paucidentatus is assessed as Vulnerable (VU) on the IUCN Red List. The current assessment, by J.D. Bösenberg, is dated 12 May 2020 and was published in 2022 (GBIF, Crossref); The World List of Cycads gives the criteria A2ac; B1ab(i,ii,iii,v)+2ab(i,ii,iii,v), that is, a past decline of the population and a small, fragmented and shrinking range. In 2003 the IUCN action plan (Donaldson, 2003) already classed it as Vulnerable, with 8,000 to 12,000 plants, a slight decline, populations in protected areas, and collecting and habitat destruction as threats. PlantZAfrica (2006) stresses that the population has declined considerably and that the species is often collected illegally. The action plan also records that nurseries linked to rural communities were set up in South Africa for this species, so that local people would benefit from the production of plants.

Regulation, according to Species+ (consulted through its public API on 26 October 2026):

  • CITES: Appendix I since 4 February 1977, like the whole genus;
  • European Union: Annex A, current list in Regulation (EU) 2026/1383; for artificially propagated plants from South Africa, a negative genus-wide opinion of 3 December 2010 was lifted on 11 March 2011, and a positive opinion specific to the species, limited to plants whose caudex does not exceed 15 cm in diameter, has been in force since 20 June 2011; a new consultation covering the whole genus has been open since 16 September 2026.

Seeds are covered in the same way as plants. Any plant offered for sale must come from cultivation and carry the required CITES documents (and, in the European Union, the EU documents). These notes do not replace the official texts: check Species+ and the national authorities before any purchase or import.

Growing Encephalartos paucidentatus

In South Africa Encephalartos paucidentatus is regarded as one of the finest garden cycads (Dyer, 1965; PlantZAfrica), provided it is given room to spread a crown about 5 m across. PlantZAfrica (J. Winter, Kirstenbosch, 2006) sets out the rules: the species likes shade but tolerates full sun, and does best in light shade, in a moist, well-drained soil, in a frost-free position. It is one of the faster-growing cycads: once the trunk has reached its full diameter, it grows by 30 to 50 mm a year in good conditions. In sun the plant becomes very handsome, but its leaves stay somewhat shorter than in shade.

Transplanting is the delicate point: once established, Encephalartos paucidentatus dislikes being moved, and Cynthia Giddy (1978), quoted by PlantZAfrica, advises keeping as many roots as possible, as they re-establish poorly. PlantZAfrica recommends regular watering and feeding, a 5 cm mulch of well-rotted compost in autumn and, in early spring, two good handfuls per plant of a mixture of bone meal, organic fertiliser and a balanced mineral fertiliser; high-pressure water jets damage the leaves and trunk.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

In southern California, Jungle Music considers it one of the most attractive green cycads, recommending full or partial sun on the coast and inland and filtered light or shade in desert areas, a free-draining topsoil, and describing the species as relatively tolerant of water but not of excessive wet. It finds it a relatively fast grower that may flush once or twice a year depending on climate, suited to both tropical-style and water-wise gardens, and advises setting it well back from paths and seating, since it needs a lot of space. In Mediterranean and comparable climates, the plant should go where it will never have to be moved, in a deep, moist but free-draining soil, in light shade or sun.

Container cultivation

Young plants can be grown in deep containers, but the eventual size of the species and its intolerance of root disturbance mean that it is best planted in its final position while still young, as PlantZAfrica's warnings on transplanting suggest. A grower in Poland who had sown two Encephalartos paucidentatus at the end of 2009 reported on the French forum Fous de palmiers in September 2013 that his potted plants had produced one or two new leaves, rather late in the season; he grows all his cycads in pots. The Orto Botanico in Naples grew five specimens in 1993–1994 (Gaudio et al., 1997); their current presence is not confirmed.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

BGCI PlantSearch lists 26 institutions holding the species. In the United Kingdom, only the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, declares it, which is fitting for a species whose first plant was sent to Kew by its discoverers. In the United States, BGCI lists Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden, the Huntington, Ganna Walska Lotusland, the San Diego Botanic Garden, the San Diego Zoo, Descanso Gardens, the Fullerton Arboretum at Cal State Fullerton, Honolulu Botanical Gardens, the National Tropical Botanical Garden and the University of Hawaii at Hilo; the Montgomery Botanical Center's online catalogue lists no accession. In Australia, the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria and the University of Melbourne's System Garden declare it. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not a living plant on display today. The most informative American result is the outdoor trial at the Juniper Level Botanic Garden in Raleigh, North Carolina (see Cold hardiness). Jungle Music usually offers several sizes of this rare species and describes it as somewhat affordable.

Propagation

Seed is the usual method; a well-pollinated female cone yields red seeds about 4 cm long. Trunks sometimes branch at the base (Dyer, 1965; PlantZAfrica), but no published source describes the removal of suckers in this species; given how poorly moved plants re-establish, the operation looks risky. The community nurseries set up in South Africa raise plants from seed (Donaldson, 2003). The species is dioecious, so a male and a female coning at the same time are needed, and pollination is usually done by hand in cultivation. Seeds, like plants, are covered by CITES Appendix I.

Pests and diseases

No disease specific to Encephalartos paucidentatus is documented. Dyer (1972) notes that the remains of old cones found in the Piggs Peak area were badly damaged by insects. In cultivation the main risk, as with all Encephalartos, is rot of the roots and caudex in a cold, waterlogged substrate, and the plant tolerates root damage poorly when transplanted.

Cold hardiness

Encephalartos paucidentatus comes from a cool, moist mountain region, and PlantZAfrica recommends growing it in a frost-free position. An American trial shows, however, that the plant can survive, defoliated, much harder cold.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

In North Carolina (United States), the Juniper Level Botanic Garden in Raleigh published in August 2014 the list of cycads that had survived in the ground, without protection or mulch, minimums of −13 to −16 °C (9 to 4 °F, the values given by the garden) depending on the location during the winter of 2013–2014: Encephalartos paucidentatus is among them, as is Encephalartos altensteinii, whereas other species, such as Encephalartos princeps and Encephalartos lehmannii, did not survive. Almost all the plants had lost their foliage. The age and size of the plants are not given. No hardiness data have been published for the United Kingdom or Australia, and no USDA zone is documented for the species in a reliable source.

Mediterranean and European experience

No dated result is documented for France or Italy. As with other Encephalartos, survivals in cold areas often concern young plants with an underground caudex, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil; an adult plant with an aerial trunk, as this species becomes, does not benefit from this protection. In Mediterranean climates, and in comparable climates such as coastal California or southern Australia, planting out remains a trial, best kept to a sheltered position, and a large container kept frost-free in winter is the cautious choice. A single survival in North Carolina, with the loss of all leaves, does not make the species an evergreen garden plant in cold climates.

Toxicity and precautions

Like other cycads, Encephalartos paucidentatus contains toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; these substances affect the liver and the nervous system, and the seeds are the most dangerous part. PlantZAfrica lists the species as poisonous. No study deals with the toxicity of this species in particular, but the plant must not be considered harmless. Wear gloves to clean seeds, and keep plants and seeds out of reach of children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use specific to Encephalartos paucidentatus is documented. The species is sought mainly as an ornamental, which fuels illegal collecting in the wild.

Frequently asked questions

Where does Encephalartos paucidentatus grow?

In the mountains around Barberton, in eastern Mpumalanga (South Africa), and in the neighbouring Piggs Peak district of Eswatini, in low forest on steep, rocky slopes, up to 1,800 m.

Why is it called paucidentatus?

Because its leaflets have few teeth: the Latin name means “with few teeth”.

How big does it get?

A trunk about 6 m tall and 40 to 70 cm across, with leaves 1.5 to 2.5 m long and a crown about 5 m across. Once the trunk is formed, it grows by 3 to 5 cm a year in good conditions.

How can it be told from Encephalartos heenanii?

Encephalartos heenanii is smaller and branches more, its leaflets are not recurved and its cones are covered in thick brown wool; its male and female cones look alike, whereas in Encephalartos paucidentatus the male cones are much slimmer.

Is it cold-hardy?

Better than its habitat suggests, but at the cost of its leaves: in North Carolina, plants in the ground survived, defoliated, lows of −13 to −16 °C (9 to 4 °F) in the winter of 2013–2014. In South Africa it is recommended to grow it frost-free.

Can it be moved?

Only with great care: once established, it tolerates transplanting poorly, and as many roots as possible must be kept.

Is it poisonous?

It should be treated as such, like all cycads, especially its seeds.
