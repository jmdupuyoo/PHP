Zamia pumila is a small cycad with an underground stem from the Greater Antilles, found in eastern Cuba, the Dominican Republic and southern Puerto Rico, where it grows in forest and scrub on serpentine, marble or volcanic rock. It is the type species of the genus Zamia: Linnaeus published it in 1763 as the only species of his new genus, and its type is the engraving, published in 1697, of a plant raised in Amsterdam from seeds brought from Hispaniola. Its name is also one of the most misapplied in cycads: given as early as 1789 to the coontie of Florida and extended in 1980 to all the Zamia of the Caribbean and Florida, it is still used in the trade for the coontie, now Zamia integrifolia. Zamia debilis, a name published in 1789 on the same type, is a synonym. The IUCN lists it as Vulnerable.

Key facts about Zamia pumila

  • Accepted name: Zamia pumila L. (1763), type species of the genus, accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and by GBIF; the World Checklist of Vascular Plants (WCVP), on which POWO is based, also accepts it (consulted through GBIF on 31 October 2026)
  • Former names and synonyms: Zamia debilis L.f. (1789), Zamia latifoliolata Prenlel. (1872), Zamia allison-armourii Millsp. (1900), Zamia media var. commeliniana J.Schust. (1932), Palmifolium pumilum (L.) Kuntze and Palmifolium debile (L.f.) Kuntze (1891); in the trade the name is very often borne by Zamia integrifolia (see Taxonomy)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales; Zamia pumila complex, to which the species gives its name
  • Common names: no English name in current use; “guáyiga” in the Dominican Republic and “marunguey” in Puerto Rico, indigenous names for Zamia in general; “yuquilla de sabana” (Spanish) according to Species+
  • Natural range: Cuba (east: provinces of Holguín and Guantánamo), Dominican Republic, Puerto Rico (south); absent from Haiti according to Eckenwalder (1980)
  • IUCN status: Vulnerable (VU), criterion A2acd, assessment published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (listing of the whole genus; seeds exempt under annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: no measurement for the species; a specialist nursery places it among the Caribbean Zamia that tolerate temperatures close to 0 °C (32 °F); figures published under this name usually concern the coontie (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, evergreen forest and thorny scrub on serpentine in Cuba, understorey of secondary forest on marble, serpentine or volcanic rock in Puerto Rico, forest edges and understorey in the Dominican Republic; in cultivation, a free-draining, mineral, limy compost (specialist nursery)
  • Habit and adult size: underground stem about 6 cm in diameter, 4 to 12 upright leaves 60 to 150 cm long, 4 to 10 pairs of leaflets 1 to 3 cm wide (Flora de la República de Cuba)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Montgomery Botanical Center (Florida) holds 49 accessions; many gardens declare the name to BGCI, including the Eden Project in Cornwall, but some of these plants may be coonties
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; treat it as poisonous (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Zamia pumila

It is a Zamia with a buried stem that carries a few upright leaves with few leaflets, but broad ones, toothed in their upper third. The number of leaflet pairs, fewer than ten on each side, and the size of the cones are the best botanical clues; in practice, provenance counts as much as morphology.

The most precise description found is that of the Flora de la República de Cuba (González Géigel, 2003), which treats the species in the narrow sense, from plants of eastern Cuba:

  • Stem: underground, about 6 cm in diameter.
  • Leaves: 4 to 12, upright, 60 to 150 cm long; young leaves carry a reddish-brown down that disappears with age. The petiole, 20 to 80 cm long, has no prickles and is roughly half-cylindrical, flat above.
  • Leaflets: 4 to 10 on each side of the rachis, alternate to nearly opposite, broadly linear, 10 to 30 cm by (0.8–)1 to 3 cm, stiff, papery, with a blunt tip; the margin is clearly toothed in the upper third, with sharp, well-marked teeth.
  • Male cones: cylindrical, 6 to 11 cm by 1.5 to 3 cm, slightly narrowed at the tip, on a stalk 4 to 8 cm long; scales with red-brown down.
  • Female cones: ovoid, 8 to 14 cm by 2 to 3 cm, ending in a long conical point, on a stalk 5 to 10 cm long.
  • Seeds: ovoid, 1.5 to 2.5 cm by 1 to 2 cm, with an orange to orange-red fleshy coat. Chromosome number: 2n = 16.

Linnaeus’s original publication contains no description at all: it simply refers to earlier works (see Taxonomy). Commelin’s plate, which now serves as the type, shows a young plant with a tuberous stem and a few leaves whose leaflets are toothed towards the tip, together with three seeds; the text describes smooth, elongated, narrow, glossy, stiff leaflets, finely scalloped towards the tip.

Hybrids

No hybrid has been described. In Puerto Rico, the genetic study by Meerow and colleagues (2012), based on 31 microsatellite markers and ten populations, found genetic mixing only between Zamia pumila and Zamia portoricensis, the two species of the south of the island, and none with Zamia erosa, the species of the north coast. The authors interpret the two southern species as the result of geographical separation.

Possible confusion

The confusion surrounding Zamia pumila is first of all a confusion of names, inherited from a tangled nomenclatural history (see Taxonomy). The main ones:

  • Zamia integrifolia, the coontie of Florida, Georgia, the Bahamas, the Cayman Islands and Cuba: it is the plant most often sold as “Zamia pumila”. The University of Florida’s coontie fact sheet states that this plant is known “erroneously” by that name. The Flora of Cuba separates it by much narrower leaflets, 5 to 7 mm wide, more numerous (often 14 to 22 pairs), and by female cones cut off square at the top.
  • Zamia erosa, from the north coast of Puerto Rico, Cuba and Jamaica, long called Zamia amblyphyllidia: its leaflets, also broad, are more numerous (usually more than ten pairs), with a blunt tip, and its cones are smaller (male cones 4 to 8 cm, female cones 6 to 10 cm). In Cuba, plants of this species have been published under the name “Zamia debilis”, a synonym of Zamia pumila (Flora of Cuba); in Puerto Rico, a 1989 study of the Cambalache population, on the north coast, the domain of Zamia erosa, also uses that name; and according to the Flora of Cuba, young plants of the species were wrongly named Zamia latifoliolata, another synonym of Zamia pumila.
  • Zamia portoricensis, from south-western and south-central Puerto Rico: it lives in the same areas of marble and serpentine, but its leaflets are narrow (about 5 mm in the original description) and entire; it is the most closely related species genetically.
  • Zamia angustifolia (Bahamas and eastern Cuba), Zamia stricta (eastern Cuba) and Zamia pygmaea (western Cuba): the first two have narrow leaflets a few millimetres wide, the third is a dwarf plant with small cones, which Sauvalle called “Zamia pumila” in 1873.
  • Zamia furfuracea, from Mexico, with thick, downy leaflets and a prickly petiole: not closely related, but in the original publication of Zamia pumila Linnaeus already cited illustrations that his son would assign in 1789 to Zamia furfuracea, a species now known to be Mexican.

For a collection plant, the surest clue remains a documented provenance from Cuba, Hispaniola or Puerto Rico. A plant sold as “Zamia pumila” with no origin is most often a coontie.

Taxonomy

The World List of Cycads and GBIF accept the name Zamia pumila L.; the World Checklist of Vascular Plants (WCVP), on which POWO is based, also accepts it (consulted through GBIF on 31 October 2026). It is the type species of the genus Zamia, itself the type genus of the family Zamiaceae. Its nomenclatural history is one of the most complicated in cycads: the name has designated in turn a plant from Hispaniola, the coontie of Florida and all the Zamia of the Caribbean, before being brought back to a species of the Greater Antilles. The steps below were checked in the original texts where they were accessible.

Linnaeus’s original publication (1763)

Linnaeus published the genus Zamia and its only species, Zamia pumila, in the second edition of Species Plantarum (volume 2, page 1659, 1763). The entry, read in the scan of the Missouri Botanical Garden copy, contains no description: it lists four earlier references, a phrase by Miller (“Palma fructu clavato polypyreno”), plate 58 of Commelin (“Palma prunifera humilis non spinosa insulae Hispaniolae…”), two figures by Plukenet (“Palma americana, foliis polygonati brevibus leviter serratis & nonnihil spinosis, trunco crasso”) and a plate by Trew and Ehret (“Palmifolia femina”), with the habitat “in America meridionali”. The genus name Zamia is conserved against Palma-filix Adans., published the same year (Flora of Cuba).

According to Eckenwalder (1980), an author, Smith (1961), had noted that this original publication mixes two present-day species and concluded that Miller (1768) had already fixed the name on the references of Plukenet and Trew, so that Zamia pumila should have designated the Mexican species now called Zamia furfuracea, and the Caribbean plant should be called Zamia debilis. Eckenwalder rejected this argument: Miller was describing a Palma pumila of his own, without referring to Linnaeus, which does not amount to choosing a type. Smith’s article was not consulted.

The 1789 split: integrifolia, debilis and furfuracea

In William Aiton’s Hortus Kewensis (volume 3, pages 477–478, 1789), the younger Linnaeus divided the elements of his father’s Zamia pumila between three new species, as the text shows:

  • Zamia furfuracea, given as native to the West Indies and grown at Hampton Court as early as 1691, received the references of Plukenet and Trew;
  • Zamia integrifolia, a plant of East Florida introduced in 1768 by John Ellis, received the name “Zamia pumila Sp. pl. 1659”, but “exclusis synonymis”, that is, without the references cited by Linnaeus;
  • Zamia debilis, from the West Indies, introduced in 1777 by Kennedy and Lee, received Commelin’s plate.

The Florida coontie was thus first presented as Linnaeus’s Zamia pumila stripped of its references, and Eckenwalder (1980) describes Zamia integrifolia as a “renaming” of Zamia pumila. This is the origin of the confusion that still persists in the trade. The World List of Cycads states that the name Zamia integrifolia was proposed for conservation with a conserved type (Stevenson and Reveal, 2011), a proposal recommended by the Nomenclature Committee for Vascular Plants (Applequist, 2012); the text of the proposal could not be read (publisher’s site inaccessible), so its reasons are not given here.

Typification and synonyms

In 1980 Eckenwalder chose as lectotype of Zamia pumila plate 58 of the first volume of Jan Commelin’s Horti Medici Amstelodamensis (1697), kept in Amsterdam. This choice, followed by the Flora de la República de Cuba and by The World List of Cycads, has two consequences: it ties the name to a plant from Hispaniola, and it makes Zamia debilis, based on the same plate, homotypic with Zamia pumila. Eckenwalder treated Zamia debilis as an illegitimate name, whereas The World List of Cycads simply lists it as a synonym. The Flora of Cuba does the same for Zamia media var. commeliniana J.Schust. (1932), for which it designated a lectotype in 2003.

The lists of synonyms differ between sources:

  • The World List of Cycads: Zamia debilis L.f. (1789) and Palmifolium debile (L.f.) Kuntze, Zamia latifoliolata Prenlel. (1872), Zamia allison-armourii Millsp. (1900), Zamia media var. commeliniana J.Schust. (1932), as well as Palmifolium pumilum (L.) Kuntze (1891) and Zamia pumila subsp. pumila;
  • Species+, the CITES database: the same main names, plus Encephalartos pumilus Sweet and Zamia humilis Salisb., which The World List of Cycads does not mention;
  • Eckenwalder (1980): a much longer list, since he also included Zamia integrifolia, Zamia angustifolia, Zamia media, Zamia floridana, Zamia portoricensis, Zamia erosa, Zamia lucayana, Zamia stricta and some twenty other names, now divided between several species.

The types of the two synonyms based on collections come from the Dominican Republic: Zamia latifoliolata, described from a collection by Prenleloup on the road to Punto de la Torrecilla, and Zamia allison-armourii, from a collection by Millspaugh near a stream flowing into Santo Domingo.

From Eckenwalder’s single species to the eight species of today

Over the centuries about 35 names have been published for the Caribbean Zamia, and authors have recognised anything from one to fourteen or more species (de Candolle, 1868), according to Eckenwalder. In his revision, published in the Journal of the Arnold Arboretum (volume 61, pages 701–722, issue dated October 1980) and based on about 1,500 herbarium specimens, more than 500 plants grown at Fairchild Tropical Garden and field observations in the Bahamas and Florida, Eckenwalder concluded that leaflet variation is continuous and united all the Zamia of the Caribbean, Florida and Georgia in a single species, Zamia pumila, divided into two subspecies: the widespread subspecies pumila and subspecies pygmaea of western Cuba. The World List of Cycads and the Flora of Cuba date this combination to 1981.

According to the Flora of Cuba, Stevenson (1987) showed that this reduction went too far; his article, “Again the West Indian zamias”, was not consulted. The genetic study by Meerow and colleagues (2012) treats the three Zamia of Puerto Rico as three distinct taxa and analyses their relationships. Pagán-Jiménez and Lazcano-Lara (2013) sum up the situation: depending on the taxonomic approach, the complex contains from one to nine species. The nursery Cycadales.eu lists the eight species now considered “official”, all accepted by The World List of Cycads: Zamia angustifolia, Zamia erosa, Zamia integrifolia, Zamia lucayana, Zamia portoricensis, Zamia pumila, Zamia pygmaea and Zamia stricta. Zamia amblyphyllidia has been a synonym of Zamia erosa since 2010.

In practice, for the reader: a text or label from before the 1990s that mentions “Zamia pumila” may refer to any Zamia of the Caribbean or Florida, and very often to the coontie. This is the case, for example, of the pollination study by Tang (1987), “Insect pollination in the cycad Zamia pumila”, carried out, according to its abstract, on a wild population in Florida, and therefore on what is now called Zamia integrifolia.

Etymology and history

The epithet comes from the Latin pumilus, “dwarf” or “low”, and alludes to the small size of the plant (Haynes, 2022, as given by The World List of Cycads).

The history of the species begins in an Amsterdam garden. Jan Commelin relates, in chapter 58 of his Horti Medici Amstelodamensis (1697), that in 1690 a surgeon named Batenburg gave him fruits and seeds brought from Hispaniola, with a short description: seven or eight leaves come out of the ground, and between them a reddish fruit, partly buried, made of many fruits packed like an ear of maize, with a pleasant taste like jujube. In 1691 Commelin examined these fruits, a pretty red, with an elongated, hard, triangular, whitish stone, sowed them on a hotbed of horse manure and obtained seedlings with five leaves about a span long. The engraving of this plant, the “Palma prunifera humilis non spinosa insulae Hispaniolae”, is now the type of the name Zamia pumila.

In Puerto Rico the species went through an apparent disappearance and a rediscovery. The IUCN cycad action plan (Donaldson, 2003) stated that it had been common in the south-centre of the island until about 1920, judging by the many herbarium collections, but that thorough searches had failed and that it should be presumed extinct in Puerto Rico. It is nevertheless one of the three taxa studied on the island by Meerow and colleagues (2012), and in 2013 Jaime Pagán-Jiménez and Julio Lazcano-Lara found small wild groups on volcanic rock in the south-central foothills, by searching old and recent maps for places bearing the indigenous name of Zamia, “marunguey” (see In the wild).

Common names

No English vernacular name is in current use: the scientific name is used. The known vernacular names refer to Zamia in general rather than to this species alone:

  • “guáyiga”, in the Dominican Republic, the name of the plant and of the bread made from it (Pagán-Jiménez, 2016);
  • “marunguey” or “marungüey”, in Puerto Rico, one of the indigenous names of the genus, kept in several place names such as the Camino los Marungueyes, between Ponce and Peñuelas (Pagán-Jiménez and Lazcano-Lara, 2013);
  • “guáyara”, “yuca cimarrona” and “yuquilla”, in Cuba, names given by the Flora of Cuba for the genus as a whole;
  • “yuquilla de sabana”, a Spanish name that Species+ assigns to Zamia pumila; other names in “yuquilla” apply to other Cuban Zamia.

In 1789 the younger Linnaeus called it “Dwarf Zamia” in English, but the name then referred to the Florida plant; “coontie” is the name of Zamia integrifolia.

In the wild

The World List of Cycads places the species in Cuba, the Dominican Republic and Puerto Rico. Eckenwalder (1980) noted that Zamia are absent from Haiti; Pagán-Jiménez and Lazcano-Lara (2013), on the contrary, list Haiti among the countries where the complex occurs, without naming the species.

In Cuba, the Flora of Cuba records it only in the east of the island, in the provinces of Holguín (Loma de la Estrella, La Cueva) and Guantánamo, in mesophyllous evergreen forest and in subspiny xeromorphic scrub on serpentine.

In the Dominican Republic, the field photographs gathered by The World List of Cycads come from the Distrito Nacional and the provinces of Santo Domingo, San Cristóbal, Peravia, La Romana, La Altagracia and Samaná. In January 1985 Robert Ornduff studied three populations: a semi-cultivated population on the lawns of the National Botanic Garden in Santo Domingo, a population on the edge of secondary forest at Boca Chica and a population in the shade of little-disturbed forest at Bayahibe. According to the abstract of his study, the sex ratio is balanced in the first two, but 73 % of coning plants are male at Bayahibe; females produce cones far more often where conditions are favourable (a third of the females in the botanic garden two seasons running, none at Bayahibe).

In Puerto Rico the species lives in the south of the island, like Zamia portoricensis, on scattered areas of marble and serpentine in the south-centre and south-west, separated from the limestones of the north, the domain of Zamia erosa, by the volcanic rocks of the Cordillera Central (Pagán-Jiménez and Lazcano-Lara, 2013). The groups found in 2013 near the Camino los Marungueyes, between Ponce and Peñuelas, grow on volcanic rock, a first for the species: at about 164 m on a slope of about 45° for the first, between 180 and 220 m on slopes of 30 to 70° for the other two, in the understorey of semi-deciduous secondary forest mixing exotic and native species, such as mango and the palm Acrocomia media.

These groups are small: about ten plants in the largest (four males and five females identified), five or six and six in the other two, often several tens of metres apart. The plants are mostly adult or old; the authors found rotten female cones with unfertilised ovules and no seedlings around the females, a sign that there has been no recent reproduction, perhaps because the sexes are far apart, the pollinator has disappeared locally or recruitment is low, although a small group of young plants was seen near one female. According to the elderly residents interviewed, past farming was the main cause of decline.

The pollinator of the species is not documented in the sources read. The link with the Atala butterfly (Eumaeus atala) is documented only for the Florida coontie.

Conservation status and regulation

Zamia pumila is listed as Vulnerable (VU) on the IUCN Red List, under criterion A2acd, in the assessment by J.D. Bösenberg published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads); GBIF gives the same category. This criterion corresponds to a past reduction in numbers, observed and inferred from the loss of habitat and from exploitation. The 2003 IUCN action plan rated it Near Threatened (NT), with 5,000 to 10,000 plants, a slight decline due to habitat destruction, a severe reduction of its habitat over the previous thirty years and no population known in a protected area, the species then being considered extinct in Puerto Rico.

Like all species of the genus except Zamia restrepoi, Zamia pumila falls under CITES Appendix II, where Zamia have been listed since 4 February 1977. International trade in the plants is therefore allowed, but requires an export permit from the country of origin. The listing carries annotation #4: seeds, pollen, seedlings and tissue cultures obtained in vitro and transported in sterile containers are not covered by CITES. In the European Union the species falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, whose current list is set by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026.

Species+ has a page for Zamia pumila (CITES Appendix II through the listing of the family, EU Annex B), consulted on 13 October 2026; IUCN status taken from The World List of Cycads. This information does not replace the official texts: check the regulations in force before buying or exchanging plants, ask the seller for proof of origin and prefer plants raised from seed in cultivation.

Growing Zamia pumila

The true Zamia pumila is rare in cultivation, and advice published for it alone is lacking: The World List of Cycads cites a short article on growing it in central Florida (Broome, 2009), which could not be consulted. The available guidance is for a group of species.

According to the French specialist nursery Cycadales.eu (2023), which illustrates its article with a photograph of the author beside a Zamia pumila, the eight Zamia of Florida and the Caribbean are the easiest in the genus: they tolerate temperatures close to 0 °C (32 °F), cope well with Mediterranean-type winters and with drought, and nearly all come from limy soils. The nursery recommends:

  • Compost: free-draining, slightly to strongly limy, about 80 % mineral; these species dislike acid conditions.
  • Water: any type, including hard water.
  • Winter: 1 to 10 °C (33.8 to 50 °F), rising to 15 to 20 °C (59 to 68 °F) by day; good tolerance of long cool spells if the compost stays rather dry without drying out.
  • Summer: 15 to 20 °C (59 to 68 °F) at night and 25 to 30 °C (77 to 86 °F) by day, up to 40 °C (104 °F) with generous watering; at least four to six months of summer heat a year.

These are general guidelines, not trials on this species. Ornduff’s study (1987) gives a further clue: at the National Botanic Garden in Santo Domingo, plants growing in the open on exposed lawns produced cones much more regularly than understorey plants.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

No in-ground trial of a plant of documented Caribbean origin has been published with dates and temperatures. In the wild the species lives under a tropical climate with a dry season and no frost. Where the climate is frost-free or nearly so, a sunny or lightly shaded position in free-draining, limy soil follows the nursery’s advice for the group; elsewhere, outdoor cultivation of the true species has not been tested.

Container cultivation

In the UK and other climates with frost, a pot of free-draining mineral compost, kept rather dry and frost-free in winter in a cool glasshouse or a bright conservatory, is the prudent choice for a plant of documented Caribbean origin, following the nursery’s guidelines for the group (winter 1 to 10 °C, or 33.8 to 50 °F). In France, members of the Fous de palmiers forum stressed in 2009 and 2010 how hard it was to find “true” Zamia pumila, one of them stating that the plants sold under this name by a supplier were in fact Zamia floridana, that is, coonties; a grower from the Hérault and the Ain offered in September 2009 a few seeds of “true” Zamia pumila brought back from the Dominican Republic in August and then set to germinate. No later growing result has been published.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In short: the name is widely declared by gardens in all three countries, but the identity of these plants has not been checked; the only large documented collection is at Montgomery.

  • United States: the online catalogue of Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida) lists 49 accessions under this name, one from 1981 and the others from 2008 to 2013 according to their accession numbers (consulted on 24 October 2026). More than 40 American gardens declare the name to BGCI, among them the Huntington, Lotusland, San Diego Botanic Garden, the San Diego Zoo, the Missouri Botanical Garden, Naples Botanical Garden, the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley and Marie Selby Botanical Gardens; the list also includes gardens in cold-winter cities such as Denver, Omaha and St Paul, where the plants are presumably under glass.
  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney lists the name in its online catalogue; the living-collection census of the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria lists it in outdoor beds at Melbourne Gardens (inventory of 19 October 2026); Cairns and Adelaide declare it to BGCI.
  • United Kingdom: the Eden Project (Cornwall) and the Millennium Seed Bank (Ardingly, West Sussex) declare it to BGCI; the sources do not say in which biome or in what form.

Because the name Zamia pumila has long been applied to the coontie and to the whole Caribbean complex, a garden label under this name does not guarantee that the plant is the species of the Greater Antilles. A BGCI declaration shows that a garden held the plant at some point, not that it is alive today.

Propagation

The species is propagated from seed. It has male and female plants: to obtain seeds you need both sexes and, outside its range, hand pollination. No germination data specific to the species have been published. Seeds escape CITES controls under annotation #4, but make sure of their provenance: seeds sold under this name may be those of the coontie.

Pests and diseases

No pest or disease specific to the true Zamia pumila is documented. For the Caribbean Zamia, the nursery Cycadales.eu names root rot in compost that is too wet in cool weather as the main risk, to be prevented by a mineral compost and a rather dry winter.

Cold hardiness

No reliable cold-hardiness measurement exists for the true Zamia pumila of the Greater Antilles. Hardiness data published under the name “Zamia pumila” almost always concern the coontie, Zamia integrifolia, and should not be transferred. For the Caribbean group, the nursery Cycadales.eu speaks only of temperatures “close to zero”; only Zamia integrifolia is said there to withstand frost, with leaf damage from −5 °C (23 °F).

Mediterranean and European experience

No in-ground cultivation of Zamia pumila has been reported in France, and no dated frost episode concerns it. The few cold reports published under this name on French forums concern plants of uncertain identity, very probably coonties, and are not used here. The same confusion appears elsewhere in Europe: in April 2009 a member of the Italian forum Tropicamente based in Ravenna described “Zamia pumila” as the most cold-tolerant because it supposedly comes from the south-eastern United States: that description fits the coontie.

For comparison only, and without transferring it to this species: at the Jardin zoologique tropical in La Londe-les-Maures, in the Var, a Zamia integrifolia survived −7 °C (19.4 °F) in February 2012, with damage to its leaves. If your plant labelled “Zamia pumila” is in fact a coontie, it is the data for that species that apply. The garden has published no observation of the true Zamia pumila.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No reliable cold record is documented for a plant of Caribbean provenance in any of these countries. The British nursery Trebrown gives a USDA zone 9b rating under the name Zamia pumila, its own commercial classification, without any published observation behind it and without stating the origin of its plants.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity study specific to Zamia pumila has been published. The risk is, however, documented in the genus and in other cycads: Zamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, and for Zamia furfuracea the ASPCA cites cycasin and BMAA, a neurotoxic amino acid, with vomiting, bloody diarrhoea and liver damage in dogs, cats and horses; the seeds are the most dangerous part. For the Caribbean Zamia, the danger is also borne out by history: in the 18th century the friar Iñigo Abbad y Lasierra reported that in the south of Puerto Rico, in years when people ate “marunguey” bread, many of them died (Pagán-Jiménez, 2016); in Cuba, the Flora of Cuba states, after Roig (1963), that starch extracted from the stems of Zamia was also used as rat poison; it mentions fried pieces of stem used against rats only for other species, Zamia integrifolia and Zamia angustifolia, and not for Zamia pumila. In the absence of evidence to the contrary, treat the plant as poisonous: keep seeds and leaves out of reach of children and animals, and wear gloves to clean seeds. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet straight away.

Traditional uses

Zamia were an important food in the Greater Antilles, before and after the arrival of Europeans. According to Pagán-Jiménez and Lazcano-Lara (2013), starch grains from their underground stems have been found on food-processing tools and in the dental calculus of pre-Columbian populations, from about 5,000 to 450 years before present, in Puerto Rico, the Dominican Republic, Cuba and other islands; their use as a source of starch is also described in colonial times in Puerto Rico, Hispaniola and Cuba. The starch does not reveal which species was eaten. In Cuba, the Flora of Cuba states, after Roig (1963), that in some parts of the centre and east of the island starch was extracted from Zamia stems to feed young children.

Bartolomé de Las Casas described the guáyiga bread of Hispaniola: the stem, grated into a white paste, was shaped into balls left in the sun until they turned black and swarmed with larvae, then flattened into cakes baked in an earthenware pot; in the chiefdom of Higüey this food mattered more than maize, sweet potato and cassava (Pagán-Jiménez, 2016). According to the same author, marunguey dumplings were still prepared in the same way in south-central Puerto Rico until the middle of the 20th century, and the east and south of Hispaniola still have dishes made from guáyiga starch, such as chola, guáyiga arepa and a flaky pastry. Elderly residents of the places studied in Puerto Rico also remembered the plant being eaten (Pagán-Jiménez and Lazcano-Lara, 2013). These long preparations were meant to remove the toxin: they must never be imitated.

Frequently asked questions

Are Zamia pumila and Zamia integrifolia the same plant?

No. Zamia pumila is the species of the Greater Antilles (Cuba, Dominican Republic, Puerto Rico); Zamia integrifolia is the coontie of Florida, the Bahamas, the Cayman Islands and Cuba. The confusion goes back to 1789, when the younger Linnaeus gave the coontie the name Zamia pumila stripped of its references, and it persists in the trade.

Why is it so important in botany?

Because it is the type species of the genus Zamia, published by Linnaeus in 1763: however the genus is divided in future, the name Zamia will stay attached to the group that contains it. Its type is the engraving, published in 1697, of a plant sown in Amsterdam from seeds from Hispaniola.

How can I recognise it?

By its upright leaves carrying few leaflets (fewer than ten pairs) but leaflets 1 to 3 cm wide, toothed in their upper third, a petiole without prickles and fairly large cones. A documented provenance remains the surest criterion.

Is Zamia debilis a different species?

No. This name, published in 1789 for the Caribbean plant engraved by Commelin, rests on the same type as Zamia pumila and is a synonym of it.

Where does it grow?

In eastern Cuba, the Dominican Republic and southern Puerto Rico, in forest or scrub, on serpentine, marble or volcanic rock.

Is it endangered?

Yes. The IUCN lists it as Vulnerable (2022), for a past reduction in its numbers; in Puerto Rico it was thought extinct before being found again in 2013, in small groups that no longer seem to reproduce.

Is it hardy in the UK?

That is not documented. The hardiness figures published under this name concern the coontie; for the true Zamia pumila, a specialist nursery speaks only of tolerating temperatures close to zero. A pot kept frost-free over winter is the prudent choice.

Is it poisonous?

Yes, it should be treated as poisonous. Cycads contain serious toxins, and the old food uses of Caribbean Zamia required long preparations, which did not always prevent poisoning.
