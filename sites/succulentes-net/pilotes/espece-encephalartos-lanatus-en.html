Encephalartos lanatus, the woolly or Olifants River cycad, is a South African cycad of the highveld grasslands, where it grows on sandstone slopes and in sheltered rocky valleys of the Olifants River catchment, in Mpumalanga and Gauteng. This species of the genus Encephalartos owes its name to the white wool that covers its young leaves, the top of its trunk and its cones. It forms a fairly short trunk, often blackened by grass fires, and carries grey-green leaves with a recurved tip. Described in 1926 by Stapf and Burtt Davy, it is listed as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List; its largest known population is in the Botshabelo Nature Reserve, near Middelburg. In cultivation it is slow, sun-loving and notoriously resentful of disturbance.

Key facts about Encephalartos lanatus

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos lanatus Stapf & Burtt Davy (1926), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15)
  • Former names and synonyms: no synonym; from 1933 to 1945 Encephalartos laevifolius was included in it, and collections of Encephalartos humilis long bore its name
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: woolly cycad (PlantZAfrica, Species+), Olifants River cycad (Species+); Olifantsrivier-broodboom in Afrikaans (TreeSA)
  • Natural range: South Africa, Mpumalanga and Gauteng (catchment of the Olifants, Little Olifants and Wilge rivers, Middelburg, eMalahleni (Witbank) and Bronkhorstspruit districts), at about 1,200 to 1,800 m
  • IUCN status: Vulnerable (VU B1ab(v)+2ab(v)), Red List 2022; previously Near Threatened
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I since 4 February 1977; Annex A in the European Union; positive EU opinion on imports of artificially propagated plants from South Africa with a caudex up to 15 cm in diameter (since 20 June 2011); wild collection banned in South Africa
  • Documented hardiness: no dated trial in cultivation; in the wild, dry winters with minimum temperatures of −6 °C (21.2 °F); described as frost-hardy by PlantZAfrica (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: full sun; in the wild, deep sandy soil and sandstone slopes; in cultivation, a very free-draining soil
  • Habit and adult size: upright or reclining trunk 1 to 3 m tall and 25 to 30 cm across, usually single, sometimes suckering; leaves 60 to 95 cm
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Montgomery Botanical Center (accession of 1998), Ganna Walska Lotusland, the Huntington, the Missouri Botanical Garden, Berkeley, the New York Botanical Garden and others in the United States; Adelaide and Melbourne in Australia (BGCI); no British garden declares it
  • Toxicity: seeds poisonous according to PlantZAfrica and TreeSA, with no study of the species itself (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos lanatus

Look for abundant white wool on the young leaves and cones, grey-green leaves with an abruptly recurved and often twisted tip, and narrow, entire, crowded leaflets. Henderson's revision (1945) gives this description:

  • Trunk: densely covered with pale wool in young plants, reaching 10 feet (3 m) according to the information then available; Verdoorn (1951) measured trunks of 1.5 to 4 feet (0.5 to 1.2 m), rarely 5 feet, at Botshabelo; Sigasa and colleagues (2023) give 1 to 3 m, upright or reclining, and 25 cm in diameter; PlantZAfrica describes a usually single-stemmed plant, the stem 1.5 to 2.5 m long and 25 to 30 cm across, sometimes suckering at the base
  • Leaves: covered with dense white wool when young, except beneath the leaflets, then hairless except at the base of the petiole; up to about 95 cm (60 to 80 cm according to Sigasa and colleagues and PlantZAfrica); petiole 20 to 25 cm; tip abruptly recurved and often twisted sideways
  • Leaflets: entire and pungent, crowded and overlapping in the upper part of the leaf, linear and slightly sickle-shaped, the median ones up to about 13 cm long and 8 mm wide, with a yellowish, twisted base, held in a narrow V; 12 to 14 veins beneath the median leaflets; the basal leaflets are hardly reduced
  • Female cone: single or several, oblong, almost stalkless, 28 to 35 cm tall and 13 to 15 cm across, with scales covered in thick grey-white felted wool
  • Male cone: narrow, from 17.5 cm (herbarium specimen) to about 30 cm (photograph from Botshabelo), covered with short brown wool; PlantZAfrica gives 25 to 30 cm by 5 to 6 cm
  • Seeds: about 2 by 2.3 cm; fleshy coat yellow, the seeds smaller than those of commonly grown species such as Encephalartos altensteinii (PlantZAfrica, TreeSA)

The cones, woolly and cream when young, turn yellow as they ripen; they appear in winter (Sigasa and colleagues, 2023). According to PlantZAfrica, quoting Giddy (1984), regular fires in the area mean that new leaves and cones are produced almost every year, which probably explains the large population of plants of all ages; TreeSA notes that the trunk often bears the black marks of grass fires.

Hybrids

No hybrid of Encephalartos lanatus is reported. PlantZAfrica points out that hand pollination is needed to obtain seed in cultivation, which is also the way to keep it pure where several species cone together.

Possible confusion

Encephalartos lanatus is often confused with Encephalartos laevifolius, Encephalartos humilis and Encephalartos friderici-guilielmi (PlantZAfrica). Verdoorn (1951) separates the three species of Mpumalanga as follows:

  • Encephalartos laevifolius, from the mountains around Barberton, is larger (trunk 1.5 to 3.4 m), with longer, straighter leaves that soon become hairless and slightly glaucous, about ten veins beneath the median leaflets and cones almost hairless when ripe;
  • Encephalartos humilis remains almost underground, with a trunk rising about ten centimetres above the soil, leaves 30 to 55 cm long, about nine veins and male cones no more than 15 cm long;
  • Encephalartos lanatus has an intermediate trunk, leaves that keep their wool on the rachis for a long time, 10 to 14 veins and thick, lastingly woolly male and female cones.

Outside this group, other fine-leaved Encephalartos such as Encephalartos friderici-guilielmi or Encephalartos ghellinckii also have woolly cones; their ranges are different (Eastern Cape, KwaZulu-Natal), and a plant of known origin poses no problem.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos lanatus is an accepted name in The World List of Cycads, with no synonym. Stapf and Burtt Davy published it in 1926 in Burtt Davy's Manual of the Flowering Plants and Ferns of the Transvaal with Swaziland (volume 1, pp. 40 and 99, figure 4 D), from leaves only. The type was collected near Middelburg (holotype at Kew); according to Verdoorn (1951), it came from Toevlugt, whose local name is Botshabelo.

The species long served as a collective name. In 1933 Hutchinson and Rattray included Encephalartos laevifolius in it in the Flora Capensis, and their cone measurements in fact mixed three species. Henderson (1945) reinstated Encephalartos laevifolius, but still placed under Encephalartos lanatus collections from Rosehaugh, Schoemanskloof and Schagen, between Nelspruit and Lydenburg. Verdoorn (1951) showed that these plants formed a distinct species, Encephalartos humilis, and restricted Encephalartos lanatus to the plants of the highveld. In practice, a mention of Encephalartos lanatus in a text published before 1951 may concern either of the two related species.

Etymology and history

The epithet lanatus means “woolly” in Latin, referring to the top of the trunk, which stays covered in wool (Haynes, 2022, cited by The World List of Cycads); PlantZAfrica refers to the wool on the young leaves and on the cones, and TreeSA to that of the female cones. The species was collected early around Botshabelo, a former mission station on the Little Olifants River, where the botanist Marloth collected it at 1,500 m, according to the collections cited by Henderson (1945).

Common names

In English the species is called the woolly cycad (PlantZAfrica, Species+) and the Olifants River cycad (Species+), after the river whose catchment it occupies. TreeSA adds Olifantsrivier-broodboom in Afrikaans, Mofaka in Northern Sotho and Sijekwane in Siswati; the last name is also given for Encephalartos laevifolius and probably refers to cycads in general.

In the wild

Encephalartos lanatus is endemic to South Africa, in Mpumalanga and Gauteng. It grows in highveld grassland and on sandstone outcrops of the Olifants River catchment, in the Middelburg, eMalahleni (Witbank) and Bronkhorstspruit districts (Sigasa and colleagues, 2023; PlantZAfrica). According to TreeSA, it grows on sandstone slopes and in sheltered valleys of the Olifants and Wilge catchments, often in grassland, on deep sandy soil, between 1,200 and 1,800 m; PlantZAfrica describes sheltered rocky valleys with moderately hot summers and very cold, frosty winters.

The climate is continental: according to Sigasa and colleagues (2023), winters are dry, with temperatures from −6 to 22 °C (21.2 to 71.6 °F), and summers range from 9 to 32 °C (48.2 to 89.6 °F), with thunderstorms; summer rainfall reaches 500 to 625 mm (660 to 770 mm according to TreeSA). Fire is part of the environment: at Botshabelo, the grassland is burnt every winter to promote grazing for herbivores.

The study by Sigasa and colleagues, carried out from May 2021 to September 2022 in twenty plots in the Botshabelo reserve, sheds light on the biology of the species:

  • the population holds far more adults than young plants, juveniles being the least numerous; for a very long-lived plant, this structure does not by itself indicate a decline, and only a study of population dynamics can settle the question;
  • the number of adults explains 25% of the number of seedlings, but not that of suckers: regeneration is mainly by seed;
  • plots burnt every year hold proportionally fewer plants than unburnt plots, but more cones, and more damaged cones: fire seems to stimulate coning while exposing the cones to damage;
  • fire has no effect on the proportion of males and females.

A comparable effect of fire on cone and leaf production had already been reported in Encephalartos transvenosus, according to the same authors.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos lanatus is Vulnerable (VU) on the IUCN Red List, according to the 2022 assessment (Bösenberg) cited by The World List of Cycads, under criteria B1ab(v)+2ab(v): a small range and a decline in the number of mature individuals. Earlier assessments listed it as Near Threatened (NT): this is the status given by Osborne (1995), by the IUCN action plan (Donaldson, 2003) and by the 2009 national assessment cited by TreeSA, with a population estimated at more than 10,000 mature individuals (Sigasa and colleagues, 2023); PlantZAfrica, written in 2008, also gives NT. No more recent count is documented.

Regulation, according to Species+ (consulted through its public API on 26 October 2026):

  • CITES: Appendix I since 4 February 1977, like the whole genus; artificially propagated plants are treated as Appendix II specimens;
  • European Union: Annex A, current list in Regulation (EU) 2026/1383; for imports of artificially propagated plants from South Africa (source codes A and D), the opinion of the EU Scientific Review Group has been positive since 20 June 2011, for a caudex diameter of up to 15 cm; a new consultation covering the whole genus has been open since 16 September 2026;
  • South Africa: collection of wild cycads banned throughout the country since February 2007 (TOPS regulations, then Government Notice No. 371 of May 2012), according to SANBI's documents on the genus.

These notes do not replace the official texts: check Species+ and EUR-Lex before any purchase or import, and ask for documents proving that a plant was propagated in cultivation.

Growing Encephalartos lanatus

Encephalartos lanatus has a reputation as a slow species that resents being disturbed. PlantZAfrica, written at Kirstenbosch, describes it as frost-hardy, fire-adapted and drought-resistant, a strong architectural plant for a sunny position, slow-growing and reported to be very difficult to transplant: plants die easily or take a long time to recover. French and Italian growers who have written about it agree on that last point, although none has published a cold trial.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

At Kirstenbosch, in Cape Town, PlantZAfrica reports that each plant receives a 3:1:5 fertiliser with bone meal in spring and a generous layer of compost during the year. Jungle Music, a cycad nursery in Encinitas, California, writes on an undated page that the species needs a very free-draining soil and sun on the coast and inland, adding that a plant set in shade will slowly die; in desert areas it can take sun or partial sun to filtered light. The nursery adds that large specimens are rare because the plants dig poorly and are mostly raised from seed, and that it takes ten years or more to produce a plant for a 15-gallon container. No in-ground planting in Europe is documented.

Container cultivation

The dated reports all concern young plants in pots:

  • a grower in Agen, south-west France, reported on the French Fous de palmiers forum that in January 2011 he was on his third Encephalartos lanatus, the species being “very difficult to get going again”; in October 2011 he added that his small plants coped well with the summer heat of Agen;
  • a grower in the Tarn, also in south-west France, reported on the same forum that his young potted plant, with a 3 cm caudex, produced at most one leaf a year from 2012 to 2014, including two dwarf leaves in 2012, while it spent the winter indoors by a picture window at 21 to 22 °C (69.8 to 71.6 °F); another member put the dwarf leaves down to the stress of repotting;
  • a grower in Ravenna, on the Adriatic coast of Italy, reported on the Tropicamente forum in November 2012 that his seedling had a caudex of about 2 cm and had made three leaves about 5 cm long in the year, with daily drip irrigation in the warm season; in 2013 he moved it to a deep pot so as not to have to repot it for several years;
  • a grower in Apulia, southern Italy, wrote on the same forum in November 2012 that his plants raised from seed had a caudex of only 3 cm after four or five years and produced one or two glaucous, woolly leaves a year; very slow but robust, they grew in 22 cm deep pots of a very light sandy mix, exposed to all weathers, and none had ever rotted; in February 2013 he found, on unpotting them, a taproot out of proportion to the visible part of the plant;
  • a grower near Rome reported a failure in September 2012: a plant with a 12 to 13 cm trunk, bought in leaf and in a pot, turned out on repotting to be an unrooted sucker set in volcanic gravel; it lingered for two years and then died, and he advised buying young seedlings rather than expensive large plants.

Several members of the Fous de palmiers forum advised as early as 2011 choosing a large pot from the start, as the species “hates being repotted”.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In the United States, Montgomery Botanical Center in Coral Gables, Florida, holds an accession received in 1998 according to its number; Ganna Walska Lotusland names Encephalartos lanatus among the notable species of its cycad garden, and the Huntington, Descanso Gardens, the Los Angeles County Arboretum, the San Diego Zoo, the Missouri Botanical Garden, the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley and the New York Botanical Garden declare it to BGCI PlantSearch. In Australia, the Botanic Garden of South Australia in Adelaide and the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria declare it to BGCI, but the species does not appear in the current online census of Melbourne. No British garden declares it. A BGCI listing shows that a garden has declared the species at some point, not that a plant is alive today.

Propagation

Encephalartos lanatus is propagated mainly from seed, which PlantZAfrica describes as easy. In the wild, regeneration is by seedlings rather than by suckers (Sigasa and colleagues, 2023). In cultivation, hand pollination is needed; PlantZAfrica describes the method used at Kirstenbosch: collect the pollen as soon as the male cone starts shedding, which a light tap reveals, and store it at −15 °C (5 °F); watch the female cone scales, which stay open for three days to two weeks depending on the plant; pollinate by the wet method, injecting the pollen mixed with distilled water with a syringe. The seeds are left to mature for a year after the cones are harvested, then sown at the beginning of summer in river sand on a heated bench at 24 to 28 °C (75.2 to 82.4 °F); germination should start three weeks later, although some seeds take longer. Wear gloves to handle and clean the seeds, which are poisonous. The species sometimes produces suckers, but removing them carries the same risks as repotting: according to the Apulian grower, rooted Encephalartos suckers long grow poorly because they do not rebuild the large tuberous root of the genus.

Pests and diseases

No disease specific to Encephalartos lanatus is described. PlantZAfrica notes that leaves and cones are prone to scale insects and mealybugs, and that most cycad seeds are destroyed by the weevil Antliarhinus zamiae, whose larvae eat the inside of the seeds; TreeSA also mentions this weevil. In the wild, fire damages some of the cones (Sigasa and colleagues, 2023). In pots, the main risk is root rot in a mix that stays wet.

Cold hardiness

The cold hardiness of Encephalartos lanatus has not been measured in cultivation: no dated trial has been published. PlantZAfrica calls it frost-hardy; in its habitat it withstands dry winters at −6 °C (21.2 °F) and regular fires, and growers regard it as one of the hardiest Encephalartos, with no measured value to confirm it.

Mediterranean and European experience

No in-ground planting has been documented in France or Italy, in any region: the plants mentioned are all grown in pots, and often brought in for the winter; in Apulia some seedlings stay outdoors all year, with no minimum temperature given. Several forum members rank the species among the hardiest of the genus, but these are opinions: a grower from Belley and Montpellier pointed out in 2011 that its hardiness is “relative” and that a prolonged freeze is nothing like the night frosts followed by sunny days of South Africa. As in other Encephalartos, a young plant whose caudex is still underground benefits from the thermal inertia of the soil, whereas an adult with an aerial trunk does not: a tolerance observed in seedlings cannot be extended to large plants. Mediterranean growers, like those in California or southern Australia, should keep in mind that the habitat's cold is dry and short-lived, quite unlike a wet European winter.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No dated cold report has been published from these countries, and no botanical source gives a USDA zone for the species; the zone ratings found on some websites cite no measurement. The only figures concern the habitat: dry winters with minimum temperatures of −6 °C (21.2 °F), at 1,200 to 1,800 m (Sigasa and colleagues, 2023; TreeSA). Until trials are published, the prudent course is to keep the species dry in winter, in a pot or under cover; in Britain, a cool, bright glasshouse.

Toxicity and precautions

The seeds of Encephalartos lanatus are poisonous according to PlantZAfrica and TreeSA, which cite no study of the species itself. The toxicity of Encephalartos was demonstrated for Encephalartos horridus as early as 1944 (Van der Walt), and Encephalartos contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; these substances affect the liver, the digestive tract and the nervous system, and the seeds are the most dangerous part. The ASPCA reports vomiting, diarrhoea, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses that have eaten cycads.

Wear gloves to handle seeds, keep plants and seeds out of reach of children and pets, and if any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use specific to Encephalartos lanatus is described. PlantZAfrica recalls that the genus name refers to the starchy pith of the trunks of some species, used as famine food, and the Afrikaans names in -broodboom (“bread tree”), shared by several Encephalartos, recall the same use.

Frequently asked questions

Is Encephalartos lanatus frost-hardy?

To some extent, but nobody has measured it in cultivation. It is described as frost-hardy in South Africa and withstands −6 °C (21.2 °F) in dry winters in its habitat, but no trial in the ground has been published in Europe or elsewhere. Keep it dry in winter, in a pot or under cover.

Why does Encephalartos lanatus grow so slowly?

It is a trait of the species. French and Italian growers report one or two leaves a year, and a caudex of about 3 cm four or five years after sowing. Avoid repotting, which often produces dwarf leaves.

Should I repot Encephalartos lanatus?

As little as possible. PlantZAfrica describes it as very difficult to transplant, and growers advise choosing a large, deep pot from the start. If repotting is unavoidable, keep the root ball intact, do it in spring and water sparingly until the plant recovers.

What is the difference between Encephalartos lanatus and Encephalartos laevifolius?

It lies in the cones and the foliage. Encephalartos lanatus bears thick, very woolly cones and leaves that keep their wool longer; Encephalartos laevifolius is taller, with longer leaves that soon become hairless, and cones almost hairless when ripe.

Is fire good for Encephalartos lanatus?

Partly. At Botshabelo, plots burnt every year carry more cones, but also more damaged cones, and hold proportionally fewer plants. Fire thus stimulates reproduction while also harming it.

Can Encephalartos lanatus be imported into the European Union?

Yes, under conditions. The species is in CITES Appendix I and in Annex A in the EU. The EU opinion is positive for imports of artificially propagated plants from South Africa with a caudex up to 15 cm in diameter, with export and import permits.
