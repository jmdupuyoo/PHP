The genus Ceratozamia brings together cycads of the family Zamiaceae that live in the mountain forests and tropical rainforests of Mexico, Guatemala, Belize and Honduras. It is recognised by a detail carried by every one of its cones: the scales, male and female alike, end in two small horns. The genus is under active revision: 36 species in the 2022 monograph, 45 today in The World List of Cycads, most of them described since the 2000s and almost all confined to a few mountains. The whole genus has been listed in CITES Appendix I since 1985. These cycads of light shade are still rare in cultivation in Europe and are grown mainly by specialist collectors and botanic gardens. The best-known species, Ceratozamia mexicana, has also been described as Ceratozamia longifolia and Ceratozamia intermedia, and several plants sold under its name in fact belong to other species.

Key facts about Ceratozamia

  • Accepted name (POWO): Ceratozamia Brongn. (1846), published by Adolphe Brongniart in the Annales des Sciences Naturelles (Botanique, series 3, vol. 5); type species Ceratozamia mexicana Brongn.
  • Number of species: 45, with no infraspecific taxa, according to The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15); 36 in the monograph by Martínez-Domínguez et al. (2022), 38 known for the phylogenetic study by Habib et al. (2023); 43 in the World Checklist of Vascular Plants (WCVP, the database behind POWO, read via GBIF), which places Ceratozamia dominguezii under Ceratozamia subroseophylla and does not yet list Ceratozamia popolucana
  • Former names and synonyms: Ceratozamia longifolia Miq. and Ceratozamia intermedia Miq. for Ceratozamia mexicana; Ceratozamia microstrobila, placed under Ceratozamia latifolia; Ceratozamia martinezii, placed under Ceratozamia aurantiaca
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: horncone in English; bamboo cycad for Ceratozamia hildae; tepezmaite, cícada, pata de gallo and chamalillo in Mexico, depending on the species
  • Natural range: Mexico to Honduras (WCVP, the database behind POWO), with the great majority of species in Mexico; from sea level up to 2,100 m (about 6,900 ft)
  • CITES: Appendix I for every species of the genus since 1 August 1985 (Appendix II from 1977 to 1985); Annex A in the European Union (Regulation (EC) No 338/97, annexes in force set by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383)
  • IUCN status: 30 species assessed, all in 2022 and all threatened: 7 Critically Endangered (CR), 20 Endangered (EN) and 3 Vulnerable (VU); 15 species, mostly described recently, are Not Evaluated (NE), according to The World List of Cycads (see Regulation and conservation)
  • Documented hardiness: no data published by a garden or institution. On a French growers' forum, Ceratozamia mexicana planted out under fleece or a cloche came through the February 2012 cold spell in Nantes (−9.5 °C, 14.9 °F, in the garden; intact) and near Paris (−8.5 °C, 16.7 °F; defoliated, then regrew); without protection, a Ceratozamia brevifrons “Palma Sola” withstood −6 °C (21.2 °F) in the south of France in winter 2011–2012. In Florida, the University of Florida extension service (UF/IFAS) gives about −9 °C (15 °F) as the survival limit of Ceratozamia hildae. Amateur observations, indicative only (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: light shade to partial shade, humus-rich but well-drained soil
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the genus; all cycads are regarded as poisonous (see Toxicity and precautions)

Characteristics of the genus Ceratozamia

A Ceratozamia is identified with certainty by its cones: each shield-shaped fertile scale (sporophyll) carries two horn-like projections. No other cycad genus has this character, which gave the genus its name.

  • Trunk: fairly slender, ovoid to cylindrical, rarely branched; depending on the species, it stays partly buried or rises above the ground. The smallest species barely exceed a few tens of centimetres, the largest reach about a metre of trunk.
  • Leaves: pinnate, arranged in crowns. The leaflets are jointed at the base to the rachis, and the petiole and rachis often carry small prickles. Young leaves come in different colours depending on the species: bronze, coppery, cherry red or greenish white, a character used to describe recent species.
  • Cones: plants are dioecious, male or female. The male cone is cylindrical; the female cone, ellipsoid, carries scales with two widely spaced horns.
  • Chromosomes: the species studied have 2n = 16 chromosomes, with a similar karyotype from one species to another.

The genus closest in appearance is Zamia, which shares the same range in Central America. Without cones the two genera are hard to tell apart; with a cone, the presence or absence of the two horns is enough.

Taxonomy and classification

Ceratozamia is a genus accepted by POWO, placed in the family Zamiaceae. The number of its species has more than doubled in twenty-five years, and specialists do not always agree on their limits.

How many species?

The monograph by Lilí Martínez-Domínguez and colleagues, published in 2022 in PhytoKeys, described 36 species. It was sharply criticised the following year by Jody Haynes in Phytotaxa, who pointed out numerous inconsistencies, disputed the status of Ceratozamia osbornei and the placing of Ceratozamia dominguezii in synonymy under Ceratozamia subroseophylla, and formally placed Ceratozamia martinezii under Ceratozamia aurantiaca. The World List of Cycads now accepts Ceratozamia osbornei without reservation, also accepts Ceratozamia dominguezii, explicitly rejecting its synonymy with Ceratozamia subroseophylla until convincing evidence is published, and recognises Ceratozamia reesii, separated from Ceratozamia latifolia in 2022. Since then, new species have been described almost every year: Ceratozamia rosea in 2023, Ceratozamia alba, Ceratozamia chinantlensis, Ceratozamia gigantea and Ceratozamia guatemalensis in 2024, then Ceratozamia popolucana in 2026. The World List of Cycads now accepts 45, with no subspecies or varieties (version of 15 August 2026). Older figures, 27 or “about 35”, are out of date.

The Ceratozamia mexicana complex

For a long time the name Ceratozamia mexicana was used for most populations of central Veracruz. In 2016 Vovides and colleagues showed, in Botanical Sciences, that two quite distinct forms grew there: they reinstated Ceratozamia tenuis, first described as a variety of Ceratozamia mexicana, and clarified the types of the two species. In 2025 a study combining phylogeography, morphometrics and climatic niches recognised seven lineages in this complex: Ceratozamia brevifrons, Ceratozamia delucana, Ceratozamia decumbens, Ceratozamia mexicana, Ceratozamia morettii, Ceratozamia tenuis and a cryptic species still to be named. For the reader, the consequence is practical: many plants labelled Ceratozamia mexicana in gardens correspond to the old broad sense of the name, and growers also report frequent confusion with Ceratozamia latifolia.

What the phylogeny shows

The study by Habib et al. (2023), based on 3,954 nuclear genes from 30 species, divides the genus into three major clades, named Matudae, Miqueliana and Mexicana, with one, two and three subclades respectively. These groups are not formal sections, but they follow the relief: their limits coincide with the great mountain ranges. The authors confirm the unity of some species complexes, including those of Ceratozamia norstogii and Ceratozamia miqueliana. The index below is arranged by region.

Etymology and history of the genus

The name Ceratozamia combines the Greek keras (genitive keratos), “horn”, with the genus name Zamia: it is a “horned Zamia”, in allusion to the two horns on the cone scales.

The French botanist Adolphe Brongniart described the genus and its type species, Ceratozamia mexicana, in 1846 in the Annales des Sciences Naturelles. He did so not from a wild plant but from plants grown at the Jardin des Plantes in Paris, raised from material sent from Mexico in 1845 by the collector Ghiesbreght. The type specimen is kept in the herbarium of the Muséum national d'histoire naturelle (P). According to The World List of Cycads, Ceratozamia mexicana in the strict sense is restricted to Veracruz.

The genus also has a European history. Fossil leaflets attributed to Ceratozamia have been found in the Oligocene of Flörsheim (Germany), Budapest and Trbovlje (Slovenia), and in the Lower Miocene of Austria and Bohemia (Ceratozamia hofmannii). Their well-preserved epidermis confirmed that they belong to the genus, which therefore lived in Europe before surviving only in Central America.

Common names

The English name “horncone” is used by the Gymnosperm Database, which also gives derived names for each species. In horticultural English, Ceratozamia hildae is called the “bamboo cycad”, because its narrow leaflets, grouped in clusters, recall bamboo; the University of Florida uses this name. In Mexico, the UNAM online flora gives the names “chamalillo”, “cícada” and “pata de gallo” for Ceratozamia hildae; “tepezmaite” is used in central Veracruz for Ceratozamia brevifrons, Ceratozamia morettii and Ceratozamia tenuis, and “teosintle” for Ceratozamia fuscoviridis in Hidalgo. No other established English name is attested.

Biogeography and ecology

Ceratozamia live from north-eastern Mexico to Honduras. According to the 2022 monograph, they grow from sea level up to 2,100 m (about 6,900 ft), and most species are concentrated at altitude in the Mexican mountains.

  • Main centres of diversity: the Sierra Madre Oriental, the Sierra Madre del Sur, the Trans-Mexican Volcanic Belt and the highlands of Chiapas, the last region alone holding eight species according to the 2022 monograph.
  • Habitats: mostly cloud forest and oak or pine-oak forest in the mountains, but also, at low altitude, evergreen tropical forest on karst. Species of the Ceratozamia miqueliana complex, for example, often grow on limestone rocks between 100 and 600 m in southern Veracruz, Tabasco and northern Chiapas.
  • Small ranges: many species are known from a single massif, valley or two localities. Ceratozamia zaragozae, in San Luis Potosí, was known from a single population of about fifty individuals.
  • Outside Mexico: a few species reach Guatemala and one species is endemic to Honduras. In Belize, only Ceratozamia osbornei is recorded: the 2022 monograph described it from Belizean plants until then referred to Ceratozamia robusta, which it restricts to Chiapas and Guatemala.

In the American cycads whose pollination has been studied, Zamia in particular, beetles carry the pollen: for example Pharaxonotha portophylla, of the family Erotylidae, described as a pollinator of a Zamia in Puerto Rico (Franz & Skelley, 2008). In Ceratozamia, beetles of the same family live in the male cones, but their role as pollinators has not been demonstrated in the sources read. In 2022 William Tang and Héctor Gómez Domínguez described two new species, Pharaxonotha perezi and Ceratophila chipjonesi, from adults collected in a male cone of Ceratozamia santillanii in Chiapas. Their descriptions mention neither feeding nor pollen transport; they report similar Pharaxonotha in the male cones of Ceratozamia chamberlainii, Ceratozamia hildae and Ceratozamia latifolia. A genetic analysis of these beetles (Tang et al., 2018) places at the base of their tree a genus then undescribed, specific to Ceratozamia; the same year, the genus Ceratophila was described for beetles of Ceratozamia male cones.

Regulation and conservation

Every species of Ceratozamia has been listed in CITES Appendix I since 1 August 1985; from 1977 the genus had been in Appendix II with the other cycads. It shares this status with Encephalartos. International commercial trade in wild plants is prohibited; only artificially propagated plants move, with the required documents. Species+ names only 27 species of Ceratozamia, from the 2013 standard reference list, but the listing covers the whole genus and therefore also the species described since. Seeds have no exemption. The United Kingdom, the United States and Australia apply CITES through their own national legislation and permit systems. For readers in the European Union, Regulation (EC) No 338/97 places CITES Appendix I species in its Annex A, the list in force being set by Commission Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026. Before buying, importing or selling a Ceratozamia, ask the seller for documents of origin and check the regulations in force with the national CITES authority: this general information does not replace the official texts.

On the IUCN Red List, every species assessed is threatened. According to The World List of Cycads, 30 of the 45 species were assessed, all in 2022: 7 are Critically Endangered (Ceratozamia hildae, Ceratozamia hondurensis, Ceratozamia huastecorum, Ceratozamia kuesteriana, Ceratozamia mexicana, Ceratozamia santillanii and Ceratozamia zoquorum), 20 Endangered, including Ceratozamia fuscoviridis, Ceratozamia miqueliana, Ceratozamia matudae and Ceratozamia morettii, and 3 Vulnerable (Ceratozamia latifolia, Ceratozamia totonacorum and Ceratozamia vovidesii). The other 15, mostly described since 2020, have not yet been assessed; some received a preliminary assessment in their description, such as Ceratozamia popolucana, proposed as Critically Endangered. The main threats are the loss of cloud forest and lowland forest, cleared for farming and grazing, and the collection of wild plants. Harvesting is not always harmful: among the Totonac people of Santiago Ecatlán (Puebla), the leaves of Ceratozamia totonacorum decorate Day of the Dead altars, and a 2023 study showed that traditional harvesting, done without damaging the plants, maintains a stable population. The IUCN statuses cited come from The World List of Cycads; the CITES status was checked on Species+ on 1 October 2026. Check them on these databases before any regulatory use.

Growing Ceratozamia

Ceratozamia are understorey cycads: unlike many Dioon or dry-country Encephalartos, they need some shade, a more humus-rich soil and more regular moisture, without ever tolerating standing water.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Light: light shade to partial shade, like their native forests. Some species do tolerate sun: UF/IFAS (University of Florida) states that Ceratozamia hildae grows best in partial shade but can also be grown in moderate sun or deep shade. In full sun the foliage often turns pale, and growers' reports differ with the climate (see Cold hardiness). In a Mediterranean-type climate, give afternoon shade in summer.
  • Soil: rich in organic matter but very free-draining; soil that stays sodden in winter rots the roots and trunk.
  • Watering: regular and moderate during growth, much reduced in winter. UF/IFAS specifies that the soil should never stay saturated. It adds that Ceratozamia hildae needs less water than most plants and tolerates dry spells, while liking a little more moisture than other cycads.
  • Growth: slow, as in all cycads. Young plants cope badly with lack of water: a grower who gives Belley (Ain) and Montpellier (Hérault) as his locations reports on the Fous de palmiers forum (France) that he lost several young Ceratozamia to drought, and advises burying the caudex well (message of January 2013, episode not dated). Too much water kills too: a grower in Ravenna (Emilia-Romagna, Italy) reports on the Tropicamente forum that he lost a Ceratozamia “Palma Sola” to rot after torrential rain at the end of summer 2012.
  • Propagation: mainly from seed, offsets being rare. The fairly large seeds germinate slowly; the young taproot is fragile and needs a deep pot.

Container cultivation

Outside frost-free climates, Ceratozamia are collection plants grown mostly in pots, in a glasshouse or conservatory. Use a deep pot for the taproot and a humus-rich but free-draining mix, keep the plant in bright shade, water regularly during growth and sparingly in winter, and bring it into a frost-free place for the cold season. Most reports of potted plants left outdoors concern light frosts, such as −2 °C (28.4 °F) near Rome in December 2009 or −3 °C (26.6 °F) under shelter from rain and wind in Ravenna; one potted plant sheltered by bamboo in the Gironde came through a winter when a nearby station recorded −6 to −8 °C (21.2 to 17.6 °F) (see below). In the UK, the botanic gardens that grow Ceratozamia do so under glass (see In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia).

Cold hardiness

No results of outdoor cultivation have been published by a garden or institution; the University of Florida only gives a general limit for Ceratozamia hildae (see below). The other data come from amateur growers, mostly on the French forum Fous de palmiers and the Italian forum Tropicamente. They describe a few plants, nearly always Ceratozamia mexicana or Ceratozamia latifolia, that came through short frosts under light protection or under a tree canopy. Most species come from tropical forest and should be treated as frost-tender. An outdoor trial is an experiment, to be reserved for a sheltered site under canopy, in free-draining soil, with protection from cold winter rain.

North America

The University of Florida extension service (UF/IFAS) states that Ceratozamia hildae does very well in USDA zones 9 to 11 and can survive temperatures as low as about −9 °C (15 °F), while preferring warmer, humid climates; it does not say how old the plants were or how long the cold lasted.

Reports from Europe

At the Jardin zoologique tropical in La Londe-les-Maures (Var, south-eastern France), a Ceratozamia “Palma Sola”, a population from Alto Lucero placed under Ceratozamia brevifrons, grown in the ground for more than ten years under a eucalyptus canopy, withstood −6 °C (21.2 °F) without protection in winter 2011–2012, with no frost damage at all; watered by sprinkler in summer, it grows slowly. This Mediterranean experience may help readers in comparable climates, such as coastal California or southern Australia, without giving an exact equivalent for any particular place. Among the European reports below, the Atlantic ones (Nantes, Brittany, Paris region) are the closest guide for mild, damp climates such as south-west England.

  • A grower in the south-eastern suburbs of Paris (banks of the Marne, France; USDA zone 8b according to him) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his Ceratozamia mexicana, planted out in 2009 in a free-draining bed, came through winter 2010–2011, its second outdoors, without trouble at −7 to −8 °C (19.4 to 17.6 °F) at the lowest, under P17 horticultural fleece and bubble wrap.
  • The same grower reports that his Ceratozamia mexicana in the ground survived −8.5 °C (16.7 °F) in February 2012, after about a week without thaw, under basic protection: the plant was defoliated, but its bulb stayed sound, and it produced a new leaf in July 2012. He considers a peak of −10 °C (14 °F) the limit of the species in a dry Mediterranean climate.
  • A grower in Nantes (Loire-Atlantique, western France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his two young Ceratozamia mexicana in the ground, planted in spring 2010, kept intact foliage after many frosts of −6 °C (21.2 °F) in November and December 2010. They grow under the dense canopy of Brahea armata, against a south-east-facing wall, with simple horticultural fleece.
  • The same Nantes grower reports that his Ceratozamia mexicana in the ground stayed intact in February 2012, apart from very slight burns on an old leaf, while the garden dropped to −9.5 °C (14.9 °F), with 3 or 4 days without thaw. The thermometer under the protective cloche showed at least −6 °C (21.2 °F).
  • A grower in Concarneau (Finistère, Brittany, France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his Ceratozamia mexicana in the ground suffered no damage in February 2012 at −5 °C (23 °F), reached for a few hours: “foliage nice and green”. Protection is not stated.
  • A grower in the Gard (southern France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his Ceratozamia mexicana in the ground, with a caudex of 4 to 5 cm, did not flinch in winter 2012–2013, after brief frosts down to −4 °C (24.8 °F).
  • A grower in Saint-Médard-de-Guizières (Gironde, south-west France; USDA zone 8a according to him) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his Ceratozamia in a pot, with no protection, under the cover of a clump of bamboo, survived winter 2014–2015 and produced a new flush. The species is not restated in the message, posted in a thread on Ceratozamia latifolia. The Cubzac-les-Ponts weather station recorded −6 to −8 °C (21.2 to 17.6 °F), and the grower thinks it was 2 or 3 °C (3.6 to 5.4 °F) colder at the foot of the plant; he himself considers the figure approximate.
  • A grower in Roussillon (southern France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that he saw, in a private collection in Corsica, a Ceratozamia hildae intact after −0.5 °C (31.1 °F) in winter 2011–2012, the hardest in ten years according to him. Growing conditions are not stated, and the witness himself stresses that this proves nothing about the hardiness of the species.
  • A grower in the Castelli Romani (Lazio, Italy) reports on the Tropicamente forum that his potted Ceratozamia latifolia, outdoors, coped well with −2 °C (28.4 °F) in December 2009, while his bananas froze.
  • A grower in Ravenna (Emilia-Romagna, Italy; USDA zone 8a/8b according to him) reports on the Tropicamente forum that his Ceratozamia latifolia, received as Ceratozamia microstrobila, suffered no damage in February 2012 at −2 °C (28.4 °F), in a cold double-skinned polytunnel heated for a few hours, while it was −8 °C (17.6 °F) outside. He had already seen a potted Ceratozamia latifolia, outdoors but sheltered from rain and wind, withstand −3 °C (26.6 °F) in a winter before 2009.

Reports on sun and wind differ. A grower in the Tarn (south-west France, USDA zone 8b according to him) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that in spring 2014 his newly planted Ceratozamia mexicana, in partial shade, had leaves snapped at the base by a 100 km/h (62 mph) wind, then sun scorch and two lost leaves; a large potted Ceratozamia latifolia, shaded until early afternoon, saw its leaves turn pale and then scorch. Conversely, a grower in Ensuès-la-Redonne (Côte Bleue, Bouches-du-Rhône, France), facing the sea, reports on the same forum that in spring 2014 his Ceratozamia mexicana tolerated full sun, and that a potted Ceratozamia latifolia produced a full flush there without trouble.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In short: in Britain Ceratozamia are glasshouse plants; in the United States and Australia the main collections are in Florida, California and the large botanic gardens. A species listed by a garden in the BGCI PlantSearch database (consulted in October 2026) shows that it has been declared there at some point, not that a living plant is there today.

  • United Kingdom: the Royal Botanic Garden Edinburgh's online Garden Explorer (updated 18 October 2026) lists Ceratozamia kuesteriana, whose accession dates from 1933, and Ceratozamia robusta; the Edinburgh glasshouses are closed for restoration. The Cambridge University Botanic Garden catalogue lists Ceratozamia latifolia (accession of 2020). Kew, Oxford and Glasgow, among others, declare Ceratozamia hildae, Ceratozamia kuesteriana, Ceratozamia latifolia, Ceratozamia mexicana or Ceratozamia robusta in BGCI PlantSearch. No Ceratozamia growing in the open ground in Britain is documented in a verifiable source.
  • United States: Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden's Garden Explorer (updated 16 October 2026) has 21 Ceratozamia entries, and Montgomery Botanical Center's catalogue holds more than twenty species. Lotusland (Montecito, California) names Ceratozamia latifolia and Ceratozamia fuscoviridis among the notable plants of its cycad garden. At the University of California Botanical Garden in Berkeley, a 2015 Plant Collections Network profile describes the San Francisco Bay climate as “marginal” for Ceratozamia.
  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney has 5 Ceratozamia entries in its Garden Explorer (updated 1 October 2026). At Melbourne, the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria census places Ceratozamia norstogii in the Cycad House, with three more plants in an outdoor bed, and Ceratozamia mexicana in the Tropical House.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity study specific to Ceratozamia is documented, but the risk is documented in other cycads, particularly Zamia and Cycas. Their tissues, and above all their seeds, contain cycasin and macrozamin, which digestion converts into methylazoxymethanol, toxic to the liver and the nervous system, as well as a neurotoxic amino acid, BMAA. Dogs, livestock and people can be seriously poisoned. In the absence of evidence to the contrary, treat Ceratozamia as poisonous: keep seeds and leaves out of reach of children and animals, and wear gloves to clean the seeds. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Species of the genus Ceratozamia

The 45 species below are accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15), which gives the authors, the ranges cited and the IUCN statuses (all assessments dated 2022); the status of a few remains disputed by other authors (see Taxonomy). They are arranged by region, from north to south.

Sierra Madre Oriental: Tamaulipas, San Luis Potosí, Querétaro, Hidalgo

  • Ceratozamia kuesteriana Regel: described in 1857 without a precise locality, rediscovered in southern Tamaulipas (municipalities of Gómez Farías, Ocampo and Tula), on karst rocks in pine-oak forest and cloud forest, between 1,100 and 1,500 m; Critically Endangered (CR, 2022).
  • Ceratozamia zaragozae Medellín: San Luis Potosí, near Río Verde; regarded as one of the most threatened in the genus; Endangered (EN, 2022).
  • Ceratozamia hildae G.P.Landry & M.C.Wilson: Sierra Gorda (Querétaro and San Luis Potosí), in evergreen tropical forest on karst rocks, between 300 and 1,200 m; narrow, clustered leaflets, the bamboo cycad; Critically Endangered (CR, 2022).
  • Ceratozamia latifolia Miq.: San Luis Potosí according to The World List of Cycads; includes the former Ceratozamia microstrobila; Vulnerable (VU, 2022).
  • Ceratozamia reesii Vovides, Pérez-Farr. & Gut.Ortega: separated from Ceratozamia latifolia in 2022; municipality of Xilitla (San Luis Potosí); Not Evaluated (NE).
  • Ceratozamia chamberlainii Mart.-Domínguez, Nic.-Mor. & D.W.Stev.: Querétaro, San Luis Potosí and Hidalgo, cloud forest on rocks between 900 and 1,200 m; Endangered (EN, 2022).
  • Ceratozamia sabatoi Vovides, Vázq.Torres, Schutzman & Iglesias: Hidalgo and Querétaro, two known localities; Endangered (EN, 2022).
  • Ceratozamia fuscoviridis W.Bull: Hidalgo and Veracruz; Endangered (EN, 2022).

Central and northern Veracruz, Puebla

  • Ceratozamia mexicana Brongn.: type species, central Veracruz in the strict sense, in cloud forest on karst between 500 and 1,100 m; Critically Endangered (CR, 2022).
  • Ceratozamia tenuis (Dyer) D.W.Stev. & Vovides: central Veracruz, cloud forest on volcanic soils between 1,200 and 1,850 m; reinstated in 2016; Endangered (EN, 2022).
  • Ceratozamia brevifrons Miq.: micro-endemic of central Veracruz; Endangered (EN, 2022).
  • Ceratozamia morettii Vázq.Torres & Vovides: cloud forest of Veracruz; Endangered (EN, 2022).
  • Ceratozamia decumbens Vovides, Avendaño, Pérez-Farr. & Gonz.-Astorga: a small massif in central Veracruz, on karst, between 450 and 1,100 m; Endangered (EN, 2022).
  • Ceratozamia delucana Vázq.Torres, A.Moretti & Carvajal-Hern.: Sierra Norte de Puebla and Veracruz; Endangered (EN, 2022).
  • Ceratozamia totonacorum Mart.-Domínguez & Nic.-Mor.: Sierra Norte de Puebla, Hidalgo and Veracruz, on rock faces between 600 and 1,050 m; leaves used for the Day of the Dead; Vulnerable (VU, 2022).
  • Ceratozamia huastecorum Avendaño, Vovides & Cast.-Campos: endemic to the Sierra de Otontepec, in the Huasteca of northern Veracruz, in cloud forest between 800 and 1,300 m; Critically Endangered (CR, 2022).

Southern Veracruz, Tabasco and northern Oaxaca: lowland tropical forest

  • Ceratozamia miqueliana H.Wendl.: Veracruz, Tabasco and Chiapas, on shallow soils in rainforest from 19 to 1,000 m; Endangered (EN, 2022).
  • Ceratozamia euryphyllidia Vázq.Torres, Sabato & D.W.Stev.: southern Veracruz and the borders of Oaxaca (Uxpanapa, Chimalapas); very broad leaflets; Endangered (EN, 2022).
  • Ceratozamia dominguezii Pérez-Farr. & Gut.Ortega: karst forests of Uxpanapa (south-eastern Veracruz); accepted by The World List of Cycads, which rejects its synonymy with Ceratozamia subroseophylla proposed in 2022 and followed by the WCVP; Not Evaluated (NE).

FAQ about Ceratozamia

How do you tell a Ceratozamia from a Zamia?

By the cones. In Ceratozamia each scale carries two small horns; in Zamia it has none. Without a cone the two genera look very much alike, and only the original label can settle the question.

How many species of Ceratozamia are there?

It depends on the source and the date. The World List of Cycads accepts 45 (version of 15 August 2026), against 36 in the 2022 monograph. New species are described almost every year, mainly in Chiapas, Oaxaca and Veracruz.

Can a Ceratozamia be grown outdoors in the UK or a cool climate?

Only as an experiment. No results published by a garden or institution are documented, and British gardens grow them under glass. On a French forum, amateurs report Ceratozamia mexicana in the ground that came through the February 2012 cold spell: intact under a cloche in Nantes (−9.5 °C, 14.9 °F, in the garden), defoliated and then regrowing near Paris (−8.5 °C, 16.7 °F), unharmed in Brittany (−5 °C, 23 °F). These are indicative observations, mostly under protection. Elsewhere, a pot brought into a frost-free place for winter remains the rule.

Does a Ceratozamia tolerate full sun?

Some tolerate moderate sun, such as Ceratozamia hildae according to the University of Florida. But they are understorey plants. In full sun the foliage turns pale and can scorch, especially after planting: a grower in south-west France lost leaves in spring 2014, while another, facing the sea near Marseille, grows Ceratozamia mexicana in sun without trouble. Light shade or bright partial shade suits them better, with afternoon shade under a hot summer sun.

Can I buy a Ceratozamia freely?

No, not without documents. The whole genus has been in CITES Appendix I since 1985 (Annex A of the EU Regulation). Only artificially propagated plants can be sold internationally, with proof of origin, under the national rules of the UK, the US, Australia or the EU. Ask the seller for these documents and keep them.

Is my Ceratozamia mexicana really a Ceratozamia mexicana?

Not necessarily. The name long covered several Veracruz species that are now separated, such as Ceratozamia tenuis or Ceratozamia brevifrons, and growers report confusion with Ceratozamia latifolia. Leaf colour is no help either: the 2022 monograph describes green young leaves in Ceratozamia mexicana, whereas Vovides and colleagues (2016) describe reddish-brown emerging leaflets in the El Mirador population, close to the type. Only a plant of known wild origin, or examination by a specialist, can identify it with certainty.

Are Ceratozamia poisonous?

You should assume so. No study specific to the genus is documented, but the cycads studied contain toxins that seriously harm the liver and the nervous system, especially in the seeds. Keep them out of reach of children and animals.
