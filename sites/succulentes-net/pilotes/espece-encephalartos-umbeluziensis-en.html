Encephalartos umbeluziensis, the Umbeluzi cycad, is a small cycad with an underground trunk from southern Mozambique and Eswatini, where it grows in the valley of the Umbeluzi River, in the shade of trees in ravines and along watercourses. Above ground, this species of the genus Encephalartos shows only two to five narrow-leafleted, dark green leaves 1 to 2 m long. Described in 1951 by R. Allen Dyer, it was treated in 1961 as a variety of Encephalartos villosus, under the name Encephalartos villosus var. umbeluziensis, which still appears in some sources. The IUCN lists it as Endangered, and it is rare in cultivation.

Key facts about Encephalartos umbeluziensis

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos umbeluziensis R.A.Dyer (1951), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15), GBIF and Species+
  • Former names and synonyms: Encephalartos villosus var. umbeluziensis (R.A.Dyer) J.Lewis (1961), according to IPNI; the name “Encephalartos striatus” was misapplied to this species in a 2004 checklist of Mozambique (Flora of Mozambique)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Umbeluzi cycad (Species+)
  • Natural range: southern Mozambique (around Goba, in the former district of Lourenço Marques) and Eswatini, in the Umbeluzi valley
  • IUCN status: Endangered (EN B1ab(iii,v)+2ab(iii,v); C1), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated 15 May 2020 and published in 2022; Vulnerable in the IUCN action plan of 2003
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I since 1977, like the whole genus; Annex A in the European Union, with a negative EU opinion since 2013 for artificially propagated plants from South Africa
  • Documented hardiness: no measured or reliably reported value; PlantZAfrica calls it frost-hardy without giving a figure (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, dry deciduous forest with sparse undergrowth, ravines and stream banks, under tall trees; in cultivation, light shade (PlantZAfrica) or filtered light (Jungle Music)
  • Habit and adult size: underground trunk about 30 cm long and 20 to 25 cm in diameter; 2 to 5 leaves 1 to 2 m long (Dyer, 1951 and 1965)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: no British garden declares it to BGCI; in the United States, the Montgomery Botanical Center (six accessions), Fairchild, the Huntington, Lotusland, the San Diego gardens and a few others; in Australia, Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria
  • Toxicity: not studied for the species; seeds of other Encephalartos are toxic (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos umbeluziensis

It is recognised by its buried trunk, its few leaves with no row of prickles at the base, and its narrow, dark green leaflets set at an angle to the rachis, like a venetian blind three-quarters open. R.A. Dyer's original description (1951), in The Flowering Plants of Africa, completed by his 1965 revision of the cycads of southern Africa, gives the following characters:

  • Trunk: underground, sometimes with the crown slightly exposed, up to about 30 cm long and 20 to 25 cm in diameter, covered with the close-set, very woolly bases of old leaves and extended below into a stout tuberous root; unbranched, or occasionally branched, perhaps after injury (Dyer, 1965);
  • Leaves: 2 to 5, produced together, 1 to 2 m long, slightly recurved in shade, almost erect and straight in exposed positions; woolly when young, then hairless; very woolly basal swelling 4 to 5 cm by 7 to 8 cm; petiole fairly slender, 10 to 30 cm long; rachis rounded beneath and not grooved above;
  • Leaflets: light green, soon dark green on both surfaces, about 1 cm apart and slightly twisted at the base, the upper surface turned upwards; the median ones linear, 5 to 20 cm (rarely 30 cm) long and 8 to 15 mm wide, sharply pointed, with a terminal spine and one to three lateral spines; towards the base of the rachis the leaflets become smaller without being reduced to true prickles;
  • Male cones: one to three per plant, olive green turning sulphur yellow, subcylindrical, up to about 30 cm (rarely 35 cm) long and 6 to 8 cm in diameter, on a stalk 10 to 12 cm long; pollen without an unpleasant smell;
  • Female cones: green tinged with yellow and pink before maturity, then sulphur yellow, up to about 30 cm long and 12 cm in diameter, on a stalk up to 15 cm long, with about a dozen spirals of tightly packed scales;
  • Seeds: scarlet, about 3.5 cm long and 2 cm wide, with six unequal faces.

Dyer notes that in the wild, sheltered by tall trees, the leaves spread and arch, whereas in cultivation they become more erect and stiffer.

PlantZAfrica (SANBI) adds or gives slightly different figures: glossy bright green leaves covered with a silky layer of hairs when young, median leaflets 10 to 20 cm long and 1 to 1.5 cm wide, the lowest 15 to 20 cm of the leaf stalk bare, up to four cones per plant, male cones 20 to 30 cm long and 8 to 10 cm in diameter, female cones 25 to 30 cm long and 12 to 15 cm in diameter. It also states that the cones “remain green at maturity” and that the surface of the seed kernel is covered with small knobs, a feature it describes as unique among the cycads of southern Africa. The colour of the cones differs between sources: sulphur yellow at maturity for Dyer, green for PlantZAfrica, and, in cultivation in California, yellow female cones and green male cones according to Jungle Music.

Hybrids

No natural hybrid of Encephalartos umbeluziensis is known. Dyer (1951 and 1965) stresses, on the contrary, that no plant intermediate with Encephalartos villosus had been found, despite the examination of many plants in cultivation and in the wild. In collections, cross-pollination remains possible: to obtain pure seed, female cones must be isolated and the origin of the pollen controlled.

Possible confusion

The main confusion is with Encephalartos villosus, a more widespread species of South Africa and Eswatini, also with an underground trunk. Dyer (1951, 1965) gives the following differences:

  • in Encephalartos villosus, the lower leaflets are reduced to a series of conspicuous prickles along the base of the leaf; in Encephalartos umbeluziensis there are at most one or two, and the petiole remains smooth and rounded;
  • Encephalartos villosus is generally more robust, with longer, arching leaves and glossier, larger and more tapering leaflets lying almost in the plane of the rachis (like a closed venetian blind); in Encephalartos umbeluziensis they stand at an angle to the rachis (like a blind three-quarters open);
  • the cones and seeds of Encephalartos villosus are larger, and the face of the scales differs slightly;
  • their habitats differ: in Eswatini, Encephalartos villosus prefers relatively high, cool and moist areas, Encephalartos umbeluziensis the hot, drier Umbeluzi valley, with no known overlap.

Dyer (1965) also reports that Professor P. Greguss, of the botanical institute in Szeged (Hungary), had found anatomical differences between the two species. PlantZAfrica adds that Encephalartos umbeluziensis is often confused with Encephalartos villosus and even more often with Encephalartos cerinus, a species of KwaZulu-Natal, without detailing the distinguishing characters.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos umbeluziensis is accepted by The World List of Cycads, GBIF and Species+. R. Allen Dyer published it in January 1951 in The Flowering Plants of Africa (volume 28, plate 1100). The type is a plant collected by B.A. Key on the banks of the Umbeluzi River, in bush near Lourenço Marques (now Maputo), in September 1945 according to The World List of Cycads, and registered at the National Herbarium in Pretoria under number 28429; the holotype is in Pretoria (PRE), with isotypes in Grahamstown (GRA), Munich (M), Kirstenbosch (NBG) and Durban (NH). Dyer also cites collections from south-west of Goba, in a ravine below the monument, and many plants grown in Mozambique, Eswatini and South Africa.

The rank of the plant has been disputed. In 1961 J. Lewis, in the Flora Zambesiaca (volume 1, p. 565), reduced it to a variety of Encephalartos villosus, as Encephalartos villosus var. umbeluziensis (IPNI). In his 1965 revision Dyer rejected this treatment: the lack of intermediate plants, the differences in leaves, cones and anatomy, and the separation of the habitats justified, in his view, the rank of species, which is the one accepted today. In addition, the Flora of Mozambique notes that a checklist of the vascular plants of Mozambique published in 2004 (da Silva, Izidine and Amude) listed this species under the wrong name Encephalartos striatus, a name that The World List of Cycads places in the synonymy of Encephalartos villosus.

Etymology and history

The epithet umbeluziensis refers to the Umbeluzi (Mbeluzi) River, along which the species grows (Haynes, 2022, via The World List of Cycads).

According to Dyer (1951), the plant from the Lourenço Marques district had been under study for several years, the discussion turning on its relationship with Encephalartos villosus. The evidence for its distinctness was gathered largely thanks to B.A. Key, of Johannesburg, who died before the description was published. In 1965 Dyer defended the rank of species again against the varietal treatment proposed in the meantime in the Flora Zambesiaca (see Taxonomy).

Common names

Species+ gives the English name “Umbeluzi cycad”, which is also used on this page. No Portuguese, Swati or Ronga name is attested in a verifiable source.

In the wild

Encephalartos umbeluziensis lives in the valley of the Umbeluzi River, in southern Mozambique and neighbouring Eswatini. According to Dyer (1965), it seems to be confined to the dry forest with little undergrowth of southern Mozambique and extends up the Umbeluzi valley into Eswatini, where it may be locally common. Dyer (1951) describes it sheltered by tall trees, in ravines and along the streams that run down to the Umbeluzi. Herbarium specimens available through GBIF, notably those in Munich, mention Goba, “in a ravine, in shade, below the hill with the monument”. PlantZAfrica describes sparse to dense deciduous low forest and low scrub, under hot and dry conditions, with an annual rainfall of 625 to 750 mm falling mainly in summer.

PlantZAfrica gives the pollen-shedding period as May to September, and states that the female cones break up spontaneously between September and December; as in other Encephalartos, the cones are pollinated by insects and the fleshy seed coat is eaten by animals (monkeys, birds, rodents, bats), which carry the seeds away from the parent plant. No published study describes the pollinators of this species.

Species+ includes South Africa among the countries of the species, in addition to Eswatini and Mozambique. Neither The World List of Cycads nor Dyer records a wild population there; the South African records in GBIF mostly concern plants grown in gardens and collections in Gauteng and Limpopo, plus a few specimens that actually carry a Mozambican locality. This page therefore does not treat South Africa as part of the natural range.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos umbeluziensis is listed as Endangered (EN) on the IUCN Red List. The assessment, by J.D. Bösenberg, is dated 15 May 2020 and was published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, GBIF, Crossref), under criteria B1ab(iii,v)+2ab(iii,v); C1: a small extent of occurrence and area of occupancy, a limited number of locations, a continuing decline in habitat quality and in the number of mature individuals, and a population of fewer than 2,500 mature individuals, itself declining.

The IUCN cycad action plan (Donaldson, 2003) then listed it as Vulnerable, under criteria A4cd; B1ab(i,ii,iii,iv,v)+2ab(i,ii,iii,iv,v); C1, with a population of 1,000 to 1,500 plants, a strong decline, two main threats, over-collection and habitat destruction, and a moderate reduction of habitat; part of the population was in a reserve, and the species was held in general collections, in a gene bank and in private collections. Its status has therefore worsened from Vulnerable (2003) to Endangered (2022). The PlantZAfrica page summarises the same change (Vulnerable in 2003, Endangered today, five locations, decline due to the removal of plants and loss of habitat), but its conservation paragraph names Encephalartos cerinus by mistake.

Regulation, according to Species+ (consulted through its public API on 26 October 2026):

  • CITES: Appendix I since 4 February 1977, like the whole genus;
  • European Union: Annex A, current list in Regulation (EU) 2026/1383 (in force since 29 June 2026); for artificially propagated plants from South Africa (source codes A and D), a negative EU opinion has applied since 7 February 2013, without the small-plant exception granted to some other species, and a new consultation on the whole genus has been open since 16 September 2026;
  • Mozambique: past suspensions of trade in Zamiaceae, no longer in force: CITES suspended all exports from 2012 to 2019 (lifted by notification 2019/027), and the European Union, through decisions taken from 2008 to 2017, suspended imports of wild specimens.

Seeds are covered in the same way as plants. Any plant offered for sale must come from cultivation and carry the CITES documents required by the countries concerned (and, in the European Union, the EU documents). These notes do not replace the official texts: check Species+ and the national authorities before any purchase or import.

Growing Encephalartos umbeluziensis

This is a small shade cycad, slow-growing and rarely offered. PlantZAfrica describes it as a relatively slow grower that grows best in light shade, and lists its gardening skill as “easy”. Its natural habitat, shady ravines and stream banks in a hot, fairly dry valley, and its underground trunk point to a plant for light shade that tolerates some dryness. Dyer (1951) shows that it was already grown in the 1940s in several South African towns and in Mozambique, and notes that the leaves become more erect and stiffer in cultivation than under trees.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Light: light shade (PlantZAfrica); Jungle Music, a nursery at Encinitas near San Diego, writes that in all areas, coastal, inland and desert, the species needs filtered light or shade and does not tolerate full sun;
  • Soil: loam according to the PlantZAfrica attributes; a loamy soil suits it according to Jungle Music, provided it is not excessively wet;
  • Growth: slow (PlantZAfrica), “semi-slow” for Jungle Music, which advises placing it at the front of a planting, where its small size will not be lost;
  • Climate: PlantZAfrica recommends it for South Africa's frost-free coastal zones, in both summer- and winter-rainfall regions, as well as for the Karoo with light frost, the Highveld with winter frost and the bushveld with light frost; no source documents how the plant behaves in these zones.

Container cultivation

PlantZAfrica counts it among good pot plants. Its small size and underground trunk make it a cycad that can be kept in a container for a long time; the main risk, as in all Encephalartos, is rot of the roots and underground trunk in a compost that stays wet in cool weather. The pot also allows the plant to be sheltered in winter where frosts occur.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

BGCI PlantSearch lists 20 institutions declaring Encephalartos umbeluziensis. None is in the United Kingdom. In the United States, the Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida) lists six accessions in its online catalogue, whose numbers point to 1987, 1996 (three) and 2015, plus one older, undated accession; BGCI also lists Fairchild, the Huntington, Lotusland, Descanso Gardens, the San Diego Botanic Garden, the San Diego Zoo, the Marie Selby and Naples gardens in Florida and the University of Hawaii garden at Hilo. In Australia, Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria declares it. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not a living plant on display today. Jungle Music describes the species as very rare and difficult to find, with seed unavailable for some time, and usually offers only small plants.

In Europe, the 1984 list of rare plants in botanic gardens published by the IUCN Threatened Plants Unit at Kew recorded it in nine gardens, including two in France: the “Les Cèdres” botanic garden at Saint-Jean-Cap-Ferrat, on the Riviera, and the Muséum national d'histoire naturelle in Paris. In Naples, five specimens from Eswatini and Mozambique were grown in 1993–1994 (Gaudio et al., 1997); the species also appears in the cycad list of the Messina botanic garden (2009) and in the species list of the Barcelona botanic garden (2012, “Sabana” sector). These sources say nothing of the growing conditions, nor whether the plants still exist.

Propagation

Propagation is from seed, which requires plants of both sexes and, in collections, hand pollination; PlantZAfrica notes that the plant rarely produces suckers. PlantZAfrica gives the method used for the species:

  • collect and clean the seeds, removing all the flesh, which may contain germination inhibitors and encourage fungi (wear gloves, as the flesh contains slow-acting poisons); soak hard, dry seeds for a day or two before cleaning;
  • store the clean seeds in a paper bag at 10 to 15 °C (50 to 59 °F) for six months or more, so that the embryo can mature;
  • soak them for a few days before sowing, changing the water daily; viable seeds sink;
  • lay them on their side, half buried in washed sand or potting mix, at about 28 °C (82.4 °F), keeping the medium moist but not wet;
  • pot each seedling into a tall, narrow bag (about 24 × 12 cm) when its root is 10 to 20 mm long, or at the one- or two-leaf stage, keep the young plants in shade for the first years and plant them out at three to five years.

The one-leaf seedlings are susceptible to damping off, which kills the single leaf at ground level (PlantZAfrica). The IUCN action plan (2003) records a gene bank for the species. For general advice, see the article on growing cycads from seed.

Pests and diseases

No disease or pest specific to Encephalartos umbeluziensis has been described. PlantZAfrica cites, for cycads in general, scale insects, which suck the sap from the leaves, and beetles that attack the emerging leaves, and recommends horticultural oil against scale; in seedlings, damping off is the main risk. In cultivation, a free-draining compost and careful watering in cool weather are the best prevention against rot.

Cold hardiness

No reliable figure documents the cold hardiness of Encephalartos umbeluziensis. PlantZAfrica calls it “frost hardy” and recommends it for South African horticultural zones with light frost, and even for the Highveld with frost in winter, but gives no temperature and no observation. The species lives in a hot, fairly dry valley (Dyer, 1965), and no published source gives the minimum temperatures of its habitat. As in all Encephalartos, survivals in cold areas often concern plants whose caudex is underground, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil; this species keeps its trunk underground even as an adult, but its leaves remain exposed, and no measurement says to what temperature.

Mediterranean and European experience

No outdoor trial is documented in France or Italy. The presence of the species at Les Cèdres, on the French Riviera, and at the Paris Muséum in 1984, and in Naples in 1993–1994, says nothing of its growing conditions or of the winters it went through.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No dated, measured report was found for the United States, Australia or the United Kingdom, and no British garden declares the species. Jungle Music gives no cold limit for it. In the absence of data, a container overwintered frost-free is the prudent option wherever frosts occur.

No USDA hardiness zone is documented for Encephalartos umbeluziensis in a botanical source.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity study has been published for Encephalartos umbeluziensis, but the plant must not be considered safe: the toxicity of Encephalartos horridus was demonstrated as early as 1944, and Encephalartos seeds contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; these substances affect the liver, the digestive tract and the nervous system. PlantZAfrica also warns that the flesh of the seeds contains slow-acting poisons and recommends gloves when cleaning them. Keep plants and seeds out of reach of children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use of Encephalartos umbeluziensis is documented.

Frequently asked questions

Where does Encephalartos umbeluziensis grow in the wild?

In the valley of the Umbeluzi River, in southern Mozambique (Goba area) and in Eswatini. It lives in dry forest with sparse undergrowth, in ravines and along watercourses, in the shade of trees.

How can it be told from Encephalartos villosus?

By the base of the leaves. In Encephalartos villosus the lower leaflets are reduced to a row of prickles; in Encephalartos umbeluziensis there are one or two at most, and the narrower leaflets stand at an angle to the rachis.

Is it a variety of Encephalartos villosus?

No. It was treated as one in 1961 in the Flora Zambesiaca, but Dyer defended the rank of species in 1965, and The World List of Cycads accepts it as a species today.

Is it endangered?

Yes. The IUCN lists it as Endangered (assessment published in 2022). In 2003 its population was estimated at 1,000 to 1,500 plants, declining strongly because of collecting and habitat destruction.

Is it frost-hardy?

Not documented. PlantZAfrica calls it frost-hardy without giving a figure, and no grower's measurement has been published. Grow it in a pot, sheltered from frost in winter, wherever frosts occur.

How big does it get?

It stays small: its buried trunk is about 30 cm long, and it carries only 2 to 5 leaves 1 to 2 m long. The cones hardly exceed 30 cm.

Can it be grown in full sun?

Preferably not. PlantZAfrica recommends light shade, and Jungle Music finds that it does not tolerate full sun in California, whether on the coast or inland.

Can it be bought legally?

Yes, but only plants raised in cultivation, with the CITES documents (and, in the European Union, the EU documents). It is in CITES Appendix I and in Annex A in the European Union, which keeps a negative opinion on artificially propagated plants from South Africa.
