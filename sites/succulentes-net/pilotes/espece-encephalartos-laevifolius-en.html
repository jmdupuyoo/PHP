Encephalartos laevifolius, the Kaapsehoop cycad, is a slender-trunked cycad of South Africa and Eswatini that grows in full sun on the rocky, grassy slopes of the mist-bound mountains of Mpumalanga, mainly around Kaapsehoop, between Mbombela (Nelspruit) and Barberton. This species of the genus Encephalartos is recognised by its straight, blue-green leaves with narrow, entire leaflets that are woolly only when young. Described in 1926 by Stapf and Burtt Davy, it was merged with Encephalartos lanatus from 1933 to 1945 before being reinstated. It is now Critically Endangered: the main population, around Kaapsehoop, fell from about 1,700 plants in 1997 to 54 in 2010 because of poaching, and the 2017 national management plan reported fewer than five adult plants left there.

Key facts about Encephalartos laevifolius

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos laevifolius Stapf & Burtt Davy (1926), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15)
  • Former names and synonyms: no synonym; treated as Encephalartos lanatus by Hutchinson and Rattray (1933)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Kaapsehoop cycad (Species+, PlantZAfrica); Kaapsehoop-broodboom in Afrikaans and mayiphuku in Siswati (PlantZAfrica)
  • Natural range: South Africa (mainly Mpumalanga; also recorded from Limpopo, KwaZulu-Natal and the Eastern Cape, where it has disappeared according to SANBI) and Eswatini (Piggs Peak area)
  • IUCN status: Critically Endangered (CR A2acde; C1+2a(i,ii); D), Red List 2022; nationally CR since 2009
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I since 4 February 1977; Annex A in the European Union; negative EU opinion on imports of artificially propagated plants from South Africa since 7 February 2013; specially protected in Mpumalanga and Limpopo
  • Documented hardiness: no dated observation in cultivation; “very frost-hardy” according to PlantZAfrica, without a figure (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: full sun, grassland or scrub on steep rocky slopes and exposed outcrops; in cultivation, full sun or light shade, a well-drained, aerated soil
  • Habit and adult size: slender trunk 3 to 4 m tall and 25 to 30 cm across, sometimes branching at the base, older stems often lying down; leaves about 1 m long according to Henderson, up to 1.5 m according to PlantZAfrica
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Kew (BGCI); the Huntington, Berkeley and two Hawaiian gardens in the United States (BGCI); no Australian garden declares it
  • Toxicity: not studied for the species; seeds poisonous in other Encephalartos (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos laevifolius

Look for a slender trunk with small, even leaf bases and long, almost straight, blue-green leaves whose narrow, entire leaflets soon lose the wool of their youth. Henderson's revision (1945), based on plants at Kirstenbosch and on herbarium collections, describes it as follows:

  • Trunk: rather slender, up to about 10 feet (3 m); according to SANBI's PlantZAfrica account (Sepheka, 2012), often 3 to 4 m tall and 25 to 30 cm across, with small, regular, smooth leaf bases arranged in bands; older stems are often prostrate, with the growing tip curving upwards
  • Leaves: covered when young with pale brown wool except beneath the leaflets, then hairless and blue-green, the oldest yellowish green; up to about 100 cm long, petiole up to 25 cm; almost straight, often twisted at the tip
  • Leaflets: linear, up to 12 cm long and 6 mm wide, briefly pungent, with a yellowish base, entire (sometimes a minute tooth near the tip of the uppermost ones), with about ten fine veins beneath the median leaflets; the basal leaflets are reduced, sometimes almost to prickles
  • Male cones: several together (three on the Kirstenbosch plant), narrowly cylindrical, curved, about 38 by 10 cm, covered with a fine white down, greyish green; 30 to 43 cm by 8 to 11.5 cm according to PlantZAfrica
  • Female cones: several together, at first covered with short pale brown wool, almost hairless when ripe; about 20 by 10 cm on the plant described by Henderson, 40 to 45 cm by 15 to 19 cm according to PlantZAfrica
  • Seeds: orange-yellow according to Palmer and Pitman (1972)

Verdoorn (1951) adds that in the field the cones, especially the female ones, become glaucous and hairless, except for a small woolly patch in the centre of each scale. PlantZAfrica gives leaves up to 1.5 m long, bluish green with a silvery bloom above, a yellow petiole 6 to 25 cm long, median leaflets 10 to 15 cm long and 5 to 7 mm wide with 10 to 12 clearly visible veins beneath, and one to five yellow-green cones per stem, soon losing their whitish hairs. The differences in cone size between these sources probably reflect the small number of cones measured by Henderson.

Hybrids

No hybrid of Encephalartos laevifolius is reported. In collections, where it may cone alongside related species, seed is only reliable after hand pollination with pollen of known origin.

Possible confusion

Encephalartos laevifolius is often confused with two related species of north-eastern South Africa, Encephalartos lanatus and Encephalartos humilis, according to Palmer and Pitman (1972). The characters given by Henderson (1945) and Verdoorn (1951) separate them:

  • Encephalartos lanatus, from the highveld around Middelburg, has a shorter trunk (about 0.5 to 1.2 m, rarely more), stiffer leaves, more pungent leaflets with 10 to 14 veins, and thick, lastingly woolly cones;
  • Encephalartos humilis remains almost underground, with leaves 30 to 55 cm long, leaflets with about nine veins and male cones no more than 15 cm long;
  • Encephalartos laevifolius has the tallest trunk of the three (5 to 11 feet, or 1.5 to 3.4 m, according to Verdoorn), longer and straighter leaves that soon become hairless, slightly glaucous, about ten veins and cones almost hairless when ripe.

The epithet, “smooth-leaved”, recalls precisely the lack of wool on the adult leaflets, in contrast to Encephalartos lanatus. Claims that Encephalartos laevifolius belongs to a group of mist-belt cycads with other blue-leaved species of the escarpment are not supported by any published study; the 2017 management plan simply lists it, with Encephalartos hirsutus, among the species known from several widely separated localities whose genetic differences should be studied before any reintroduction.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos laevifolius is an accepted name in The World List of Cycads. Stapf and Burtt Davy published it in 1926 in Burtt Davy's Manual of the Flowering Plants and Ferns of the Transvaal with Swaziland (volume 1, pp. 40 and 99). The type is a collection by Todd made in January 1919 at Moodies, near Barberton (holotype at the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew; isotype at the Durban herbarium); Henderson (1945) cites a collection “Todd 2043” as the probable type collection. According to Henderson, the authors described it more fully than usual, including male and female cones.

In 1933 Hutchinson and Rattray reduced the species to Encephalartos lanatus in the Flora Capensis. Henderson (1945) reinstated it, showing that the two species differ in foliage and cones, and wondering how the authors of the Flora Capensis could have reconciled the dry scales of Encephalartos lanatus with the description of the cones of Encephalartos laevifolius. Verdoorn (1951) confirmed the separation with field observations and, on that occasion, described a third species of the group, Encephalartos humilis. In practice, plants of Encephalartos laevifolius may appear under the name Encephalartos lanatus in works published before 1945.

Etymology and history

The epithet laevifolius comes from the Latin laevis, “smooth”, and folium, “leaf”, referring to the leaflets without wool, compared with those of Encephalartos lanatus (Haynes, 2022, cited by The World List of Cycads; PlantZAfrica). Palmer and Pitman (1972) simply translate it as “smooth leaves”.

The recent history of the species is one of plunder. In April 1998 the Philadelphia Inquirer told how a researcher of the Mpumalanga parks board had fitted microchips two years earlier to 1,400 cycads in the Berlin state forest, near Ngodwana, before finding that most of them had been dug up and sold; two marked plants were found 150 miles away, in the garden of a Johannesburg landscaper (article cited by The Gymnosperm Database, which links it to Encephalartos laevifolius). According to SANBI (2015), the syndicate responsible for the disappearance of the Kaapsehoop population had 57 members, who were arrested and convicted; the plants were sold to nurseries.

Common names

In English the species is called the Kaapsehoop cycad (Species+, SANBI), after the small town of Kaapsehoop on the edge of the Mpumalanga escarpment. SANBI's PlantZAfrica account gives Kaapsehoop-broodboom in Afrikaans and mayiphuku in Siswati. The South African website TreeSA gives Sijekwane in Siswati, a name it also gives for Encephalartos lanatus, so that it does not designate this species alone.

In the wild

The World List of Cycads records Eswatini and, in South Africa, Mpumalanga, Limpopo, KwaZulu-Natal and the Eastern Cape. According to SANBI (2015) and the 2017 management plan, the species now grows mainly in the Kaapsehoop range, in Mpumalanga, with an isolated colony further north; it has disappeared from KwaZulu-Natal and the Eastern Cape, as well as from the Blyderivierspoort Nature Reserve, and Eswatini also reports removals. PlantZAfrica restricts it to the catchment of the Kaapsehoop Mountains, in the Nelspruit district, and to the Piggs Peak area of Eswatini. Palmer and Pitman (1972) place it in the Kaapsehoop mountains, in the Crocodile River basin and near the Havelock mine, in Eswatini. Henderson (1945) cites collections around Barberton, the Godwan River (900 to 1,200 m) and the Munnik-Myburgh mine near Kaapsehoop.

The plants grow in full sun, in grassland or scrub, on steep rocky slopes and exposed outcrops; most sites are at altitude and often in mist (SANBI, 2015). Annual rainfall exceeds 1,000 mm and falls mainly in summer (PlantZAfrica). No temperature data have been published for these sites. According to PlantZAfrica, cones appear in May, pollen is shed from September to November and the female cones break up from January to March, each releasing 350 to 385 seeds.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos laevifolius is Critically Endangered. The World List of Cycads cites the 2022 IUCN Red List assessment (Bösenberg), under criteria A2acde; C1+2a(i,ii); D, which notably reflect a very small number of mature individuals. The national assessment of 2009 (Raimondo and colleagues, cited by PlantZAfrica) already considered it Critically Endangered under all five IUCN criteria.

The figures gathered by SANBI (2015) show the scale of the poaching:

  • 1,700 plants were microchipped in the Kaapsehoop area in 1997; only 54 were left at the last count, in 2010, a decline of 97%;
  • the subpopulations monitored in South Africa numbered about 26, 54 and 15 plants;
  • at Blyderivierspoort, a subpopulation fell from 26 to 7 plants, and the last 7 were stolen at once in 2007–2008;
  • 18 microchipped plants were found during an investigation in Gauteng in 2008, and 59 plants, 14 of them marked, in Nelspruit; many plants whose microchip had been removed were seen in Gauteng;
  • about 60% of the plants dug up die within the following years;
  • 960 specimens were exported from South Africa from 1986 to the end of 2011, 75% of them after 1997, just as the wild population was collapsing.

The Biodiversity Management Plan for Critically Endangered and Endangered Encephalartos species, published in the South African Government Gazette on 21 April 2017, reports fewer than five plants remaining at Kaapsehoop, a site it no longer considers suitable for recovery because of expanding settlements, habitat destruction and open access. It notes that the Kaapsehoop subpopulation is represented in viable ex situ collections in the national botanical gardens and that three plants from Blyderivierspoort, recovered from poachers, were planted at a secure site. It plans to identify three secure reintroduction sites in Mpumalanga, to establish a genebank from the recovered Blyderivierspoort plants and to plant 2,000 seeds in the three sites, and calls for genetic research on the different localities.

According to TreeSA, a Fusarium fungus attacks the cones and reduces seed viability, pine plantations have reduced the habitat and invasive plants hinder growth; these points have not been confirmed by a primary source.

Regulation, according to Species+ (consulted through its public API on 26 October 2026) and SANBI:

  • CITES: Appendix I since 4 February 1977, like the whole genus; artificially propagated plants are treated as Appendix II specimens;
  • European Union: Annex A, current list in Regulation (EU) 2026/1383; the EU Scientific Review Group gave a negative opinion on imports of artificially propagated plants from South Africa (source codes A and D) on 3 December 2010, again on 20 June 2011 and on 7 February 2013; this last opinion is in force and applies whatever the size of the plant; a new consultation covering the whole genus has been open since 16 September 2026;
  • South Africa: collection of wild cycads banned since February 2007 (TOPS regulations, then Government Notice No. 371 of May 2012); the species is specially protected under the Mpumalanga Nature Conservation Act (No. 10 of 1998) and the Limpopo Environmental Management Act (No. 7 of 2003); SANBI considers that trade in artificially propagated plants has been detrimental and allows exports only from nurseries registered with CITES that contribute to the conservation of the species.

These notes do not replace the official texts: check Species+ and EUR-Lex before any purchase or import, and ask for documents proving that a plant was propagated in cultivation.

Growing Encephalartos laevifolius

Encephalartos laevifolius is rarely grown outside South Africa, and published reports are few. According to PlantZAfrica, it grows well in full sun or light shade, tolerates any soil provided it is well drained and aerated, grows relatively slowly, resists drought and, like all cycads, prefers moderate watering.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

In the wild it grows in full sun, in a summer-rainfall mist belt with more than 1,000 mm of rain a year: a sunny, free-draining position with water in summer is the logical choice. Jungle Music, a cycad nursery in Encinitas, California, writes on an undated page that the species does well in full to partial sun on the coast and inland, and in filtered light or shade in desert areas; that it needs a soil that drains well and does not tolerate excessive moisture around the roots, so that planting on a mound is advisable; and that it is very slow-growing and can be finicky if it is not in the right spot. No in-ground planting outside South Africa is documented with dates and results.

Container cultivation

The only dated grower's report comes from the Czech Republic: a collector reported on the French Fous de palmiers forum in September 2015 that a large plant grown in a pot and brought indoors in winter lost its leaves after transplanting, before producing about twenty new ones. SANBI likewise notes that plants dug up in the wild transplant badly. A member of the same forum, quoting a book by Adolf Fanfoni on the Encephalartos of South Africa, suggested that mountain species with fine leaflets dislike drying out and appreciate some air humidity in summer; this is an opinion, not the result of a trial.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

The species is rare in public collections. In the United Kingdom, the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, whose herbarium holds the holotype, also declares the species to BGCI PlantSearch. In the United States, the Huntington, the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley, the National Tropical Botanical Garden and the Botanical Garden of the University of Hawaii at Hilo declare it to BGCI; Montgomery Botanical Center lists no accession in its online catalogue. No Australian garden declares it. A BGCI listing shows that a garden has declared the species at some point, not that a plant is alive today.

Propagation

Encephalartos laevifolius is propagated from seed, after hand pollination, and from suckers, as the plant branches at the base. SANBI points out that cycads regenerate mainly from seed and that suckers play a minor role in the wild. PlantZAfrica describes the method: clean the seeds wearing gloves, as the flesh is poisonous, and store them in a paper bag at 10 to 15 °C (50 to 59 °F) for six months or more so that the embryo can finish developing; soak them for a few days, changing the water daily (viable seeds sink); then lay them on their sides, half buried in washed sand or potting mix, at about 28 °C (82.4 °F), keeping the medium moist but not wet. Once the radicles are 10 to 20 mm long, or after one or two leaves, pot the seedlings singly in tall, narrow bags, keep them in shade for the first few years and plant them out at three to five years old. Suckers are removed with a clean, sharp knife or spade; the wound is treated with a fungicide and left to dry for about a week before planting in a sterile medium. A plant or sucker can only be passed on with documents proving its legal origin.

Pests and diseases

The only disease reported for Encephalartos laevifolius is an attack of the cones by a Fusarium fungus, which reduces seed viability, according to TreeSA. PlantZAfrica mentions, for cycads in general, scale insects, controlled with horticultural oil, and beetles that damage the emerging leaves.

Cold hardiness

The cold hardiness of Encephalartos laevifolius has not been measured: PlantZAfrica calls it “very frost-hardy”, without a temperature, and no dated observation of plants in the cold has been published anywhere.

Mediterranean and European experience

No report of growing it in the ground or in a pot through a winter has been published from France or Italy; on the Fous de palmiers forum, the thread devoted to the species contains only the photographs of the Czech collector, who keeps his plants in pots and brings them in for the winter. A grower in Agen, south-west France, listed it in January 2011 among the fine-leaved Encephalartos he imagined to be hardier, without having tried it; that is an opinion, not an observation. As in other Encephalartos, a degree of hardiness seen in a young plant with an underground caudex, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil, does not apply to an adult with an aerial trunk several metres tall.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No dated cold report has been published from these countries. The mountain habitat, often in mist, probably experiences frost, but no published source gives minimum temperatures, and no botanical source gives a USDA zone for the species; the zone ratings found on some websites cite no measurement. Until trials are published, grow it in a container and protect it from prolonged frost and winter rain; in Britain, that means a cool glasshouse.

Toxicity and precautions

No study has dealt with the toxicity of Encephalartos laevifolius, but it should not be considered harmless: the toxicity of Encephalartos was demonstrated for Encephalartos horridus as early as 1944 (Van der Walt), and Encephalartos contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; these substances affect the liver, the digestive tract and the nervous system, and the seeds are the most dangerous part. PlantZAfrica points out that all parts of cycads are toxic. The ASPCA reports vomiting, diarrhoea, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses that have eaten cycads.

Wear gloves to handle seeds, keep plants and seeds out of reach of children and pets, and if any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

SANBI (2015) notes that the literature mentions a medicinal use of Encephalartos laevifolius, without describing it. No other traditional use specific to the species is documented.

Frequently asked questions

Why does the EU refuse imports of Encephalartos laevifolius from South Africa?

Because the trade in “artificially propagated” plants was used to launder wild plants. The EU Scientific Review Group gave a negative opinion as early as 2010, confirmed on 7 February 2013; it applies whatever the size of the plant. SANBI itself considers this trade detrimental to the species.

How many Encephalartos laevifolius are left in the wild?

Very few. Around Kaapsehoop, 54 plants were found in 2010 out of the 1,700 marked in 1997, and the 2017 management plan reported fewer than five adults left there. The other monitored subpopulations numbered about 26 and 15 plants according to SANBI.

How can I tell Encephalartos laevifolius from Encephalartos lanatus?

By the habit, the leaves and the cones. Encephalartos laevifolius has a taller trunk, longer and straighter leaves that soon become hairless, and cones almost hairless when ripe; Encephalartos lanatus stays lower, has stiffer leaflets and bears thick, very woolly cones.

Is Encephalartos laevifolius frost-hardy?

Probably to some extent, but nobody has measured it. It is described as very frost-hardy in South Africa, but no measured value or dated trial has been published. Keep it in a container and protect it from prolonged frost and winter rain.

What does the name mean?

“Smooth-leaved”. The epithet comes from the Latin laevis, smooth, and folium, leaf: the adult leaflets have no wool, unlike those of the related Encephalartos lanatus, “woolly”.

Are Encephalartos laevifolius and Encephalartos lanatus the same species?

No. They were merged by Hutchinson and Rattray in 1933, but Henderson (1945) and then Verdoorn (1951) showed that they differ in foliage and cones. The World List of Cycads accepts both species.
