Macrozamia johnsonii is a large trunked cycad from northern New South Wales, in eastern Australia, confined to the wooded slopes around Dalmorton, in the Clarence valley south-west of Grafton. It is one of the 41 species of the genus Macrozamia and the largest of those growing in New South Wales. It was described only in 1992: before then, these plants were filed under Macrozamia moorei, as its New South Wales “green form”. Although growers regard it as a fairly hardy collector's plant, it is rare and threatened in the wild, and published cultivation reports are few.

Key facts about Macrozamia johnsonii

  • Accepted name: Macrozamia johnsonii D.L.Jones & K.D.Hill (1992), published in Telopea 5(1): 31, accepted by The World List of Cycads and the Australian Plant Census
  • Former names and synonyms: no formal synonym; before 1992 the plants were referred to Macrozamia moorei F.Muell. (“pro parte”, according to the flora of New South Wales)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: “Giant Burrawang” (Flora of Australia), “Johnson's Cycad” (Atlas of Living Australia)
  • Natural range: Australia, northern New South Wales, Dalmorton district (Clarence River catchment), south-west of Grafton; absent from Queensland
  • IUCN status: Vulnerable, VU B1ab(iii,v)+2ab(iii,v), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated August 2020 and published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union; Endangered under the Australian EPBC Act (since 5 October 2022) and under the New South Wales Biodiversity Conservation Act 2016
  • Documented hardiness: one dated report from western France, under fleece: foliage yellowed but the plant recovered after a night at about −10 °C (14 °F) in February 2012 (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, wet to dry sclerophyll forest, on shallow, rocky, poor soils on steep slopes; in cultivation, free-draining soil (the best exposure is not documented)
  • Habit and adult size: massive aerial trunk 0.3 to 3 m tall according to the Flora of Australia (2 to 5 m according to the flora of New South Wales), 50 to 90 cm across; crown of 70 to 150 leaves 1.5 to 3 m long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: in the living collections of the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney (since 1979) and Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria; declared to BGCI by 22 institutions, including RHS Garden Wisley and ten gardens in the United States
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; seeds and young leaves toxic throughout the genus (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Macrozamia johnsonii

It is recognised by its thick trunk, its very numerous flat leaves, mid-green and highly glossy, and its lower leaflets reduced to spines almost down to the base of the leaf. The measurements below come from the Flora of Australia (Hill, 1998) unless stated otherwise.

  • Trunk: aerial, massive, 0.3 to 3 m tall and 50 to 90 cm across. The flora of New South Wales (PlantNET) gives “mostly 2 to 5 m” and 30 to 70 cm; the New South Wales Scientific Committee (2012) describes a short trunk, up to 1.5 m at maturity; the Palm and Cycad Societies of Australia (PACSOA), up to 3 m. According to the PACSOA, the plant passes through three stages: an underground caudex, an aerial extension of the caudex, then a true columnar trunk.
  • Leaves: 70 to 120 in the crown (up to 150 according to PlantNET and the PACSOA), 1.5 to 3 m long, flat in section, mid-green, highly glossy, with an untwisted rachis. Young leaves stand almost vertically before arching with age; some plants keep a “skirt” of dead leaves (PACSOA).
  • Petiole: very short, 2 to 8 cm, 25 to 28 mm wide at the lowest leaflet; PlantNET gives 0 to 10 cm for the spine-free part of the petiole, because some forty lower leaflets are progressively reduced to spines.
  • Leaflets: 150 to 250 per leaf, simple, 20 to 40 cm long and 5 to 11 mm wide, with a sharp point; the PACSOA notes prominent whitish calluses where they join the rachis.
  • Male cones: spindle-shaped, 25 to 40 cm long and 8 to 10 cm across; the PACSOA has counted up to ten or more per plant.
  • Female cones: ovoid, 35 to 65 cm long and 13 to 18 cm across (40 to 90 cm according to PlantNET), with apical spines up to 5 cm (6 to 7 cm according to the PACSOA). According to the PACSOA, a plant carries up to six, often three or four; a ripe cone can weigh 12 to 14 kg and hold more than 200 seeds.
  • Seeds: flattened-ovoid, 40 to 55 mm long and 25 to 30 mm wide, with red flesh (sarcotesta). The PACSOA calls them the largest of any New South Wales Macrozamia, while giving smaller measurements (up to 4 cm long and 2.5 cm across).

The seedlings differ clearly from those of Macrozamia moorei in several characters, set out in the original description (Jones & Hill, 1992, as cited by the New South Wales Scientific Committee).

Hybrids

No natural or garden hybrid involving Macrozamia johnsonii has been documented. According to the PACSOA, even offsets are very rare: its authors have never seen a divided trunk in the wild.

Possible confusion

The most frequent confusion is with Macrozamia moorei, the species from which it was separated.

  • Macrozamia moorei: both species share broad, spiny petioles. Macrozamia moorei has a tall, massive trunk and grey-green leaves, whereas Macrozamia johnsonii has flat, glossy leaves and a more modest stature (Flora of Australia; New South Wales Scientific Committee). Origin also helps: Macrozamia moorei is a Queensland species, Macrozamia johnsonii a New South Wales one.
  • Macrozamia communis: according to the PACSOA, young trunkless Macrozamia johnsonii look rather like the large Macrozamia communis of the far south coast of New South Wales, but their leaves are longer, a lighter green and more upright at first. The petiole of Macrozamia communis is 10 to 40 cm long (PACSOA), far longer than that of Macrozamia johnsonii.
  • Macrozamia lucida: according to the Flora of Australia, old New South Wales collections attributed to Macrozamia lucida were in fact young Macrozamia johnsonii. PlantNET contradicts itself on this point, referring sometimes to young Macrozamia moorei, sometimes to young Macrozamia johnsonii.

The confusion persists in the trade: on the French Fous de palmiers forum, growers reported in 2010 large plants imported from Australia and sold as “Macrozamia moorei or Macrozamia johnsonii”, without certainty as to their identity.

Taxonomy

Macrozamia johnsonii D.L.Jones & K.D.Hill is accepted by The World List of Cycads and the Australian Plant Census. It was described in 1992 by David L. Jones and Ken D. Hill in the journal Telopea (5(1): 31–34), in a paper entitled “Macrozamia johnsonii, a new species of Macrozamia section Macrozamia (Zamiaceae) from northern New South Wales”. The type (holotype at the Australian National Herbarium, Canberra, CANB) was collected on 18 October 1990 by D.L. Jones and C.H. Broers (no. 6748) beside the Nymboida River, east of Dalmorton.

This is not a change of name but a separation: before 1992, the New South Wales populations were treated as a form of Macrozamia moorei, the “green form” of enthusiasts according to the PACSOA. Harden's Flora of New South Wales (1990) still illustrates it under that name, and PlantNET lists “Macrozamia moorei F.Muell. p.p.” as a partial synonym. For the reader, this means that a New South Wales plant or document labelled Macrozamia moorei before 1992 may concern Macrozamia johnsonii.

As its title states, the original description places the species in section Macrozamia, that of the large species with spiny lower leaflets. The phylogenomic study by Habib et al. (2022), based on 4,740 nuclear genes from 39 species, concludes that this traditional division into sections does not reflect the evolutionary history of the genus.

Etymology and history

The epithet honours Lawrence Alexander Sydney Johnson (1925–1997), Australian botanist, former director of the Royal Botanic Gardens Sydney and a student of Macrozamia in the 1950s (Haynes, 2022, as given by The World List of Cycads). The Flora of Australia recalls that he was the author of the major reclassification of Australian cycads in 1959.

The PACSOA (an article by Craig Thompson and Paul Kennedy, revised from a text published in 1992 in Palms & Cycads) describes large plants in gardens in Grafton and, at Nimbin, two “enormous” plants that virtually overshadow their owner's house. It also reports that a seed-raised plant in a raised bed at Manly West, in suburban Brisbane, produced several offsets; when it was moved in September 2005, it took three adult men to carry the caudex.

In France, the species reached the amateur trade in the early 2010s: seeds were offered in 2012 by the seed bank of the Fous de palmiers forum, and young plants in 2-litre containers appeared in a garden centre in the Gironde in 2013, according to forum posts.

Common names

The Flora of Australia gives “Giant Burrawang”, and the Atlas of Living Australia uses “Johnson's Cycad” as its preferred common name. The word “burrawang” refers first to Macrozamia communis and, more broadly, to several Macrozamia of New South Wales, so it is ambiguous. No common name is attested in other languages.

In the wild

Macrozamia johnsonii lives only in New South Wales, in a small area of the Dalmorton district on the north coast of the state (Flora of Australia). It does not occur in Queensland. The flora of New South Wales places it in the Clarence valley, at the foot of the ranges, in tall wet sclerophyll forest and on rainforest margins, usually on steep slopes; the Flora of Australia describes wet to dry sclerophyll forest, on shallow, rocky, poor soils. According to the PACSOA, the species grows on the ranges between Grafton and Glen Innes under a eucalypt canopy, mainly on the northern side of a fast-flowing river but also on its southern side.

The New South Wales Scientific Committee (2012), drawing on Binns & Meek (2008), records at least 287 sites, an extent of occurrence of 22,200 ha and an area of occupancy of at least 10,100 ha. About 59% of the population would be in State Forest, 30% in national park and 11% on private land. Adult plants and older juveniles survive fire: their crowns resprout.

For the climate, the PACSOA refers to Glen Innes, at 1,062 m: 849 mm of rain a year over 98 days, winter lows of −3.9 °C (25.0 °F) and summer highs of 30.0 °C (86.0 °F), each reached at least once a week in July and January, and 71 frost days a year. This tableland station lies higher than the valley where the species grows: these figures are only a guide.

Conservation status and regulation

Macrozamia johnsonii is threatened. The IUCN Red List classes it as Vulnerable (VU B1ab(iii,v)+2ab(iii,v)), in the assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated 14 August 2020 and published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, GBIF and Crossref, checked on 26 October 2026): the range is small, and both habitat and numbers are declining.

In Australia, the species has been listed as Endangered under the federal EPBC Act 1999 since 5 October 2022, and as Endangered under the New South Wales Biodiversity Conservation Act 2016 (Flora of Australia, PlantNET, Atlas of Living Australia). In 2012, the New South Wales Scientific Committee rejected a proposal to remove it from the list of endangered species: it found a continuing decline caused by logging, with modelling predicting that 21 to 32% of its habitat in State Forest would be logged within the next hundred years. It also mentions unquantified threats: changes in fire regime and root rot caused by Phytophthora cinnamomi, for which soil carried by machinery is a vector.

For international trade, like other Macrozamia, it falls under CITES Appendix II, with annotation #4, which exempts seeds among other items (listing in force on 5 March 2026), and under Annex B in the European Union (Species+, checked on 26 October 2026). Plants sold abroad must come from seed or legal cultivation. Regulations change: check them before any purchase or import.

Growing Macrozamia johnsonii

Macrozamia johnsonii can be grown in a pot when young and in the ground in the mildest gardens, but no published source describes its cultivation in detail: the pointers below come from its habitat, from specialist societies and from growers' forums.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Soil: very free-draining. In the wild, the species grows on poor, rocky, sloping soils that do not hold water.
  • Light: not documented for the species in cultivation. In the wild, it grows under a eucalypt canopy.
  • Water: at the Jardin zoologique tropical in La Londe-les-Maures, in the Var (southern France), experience with the genus Macrozamia shows that watering once a week in summer, when there is no rain, is a minimum and twice a week ideal, and that frost on waterlogged soil is more dangerous than dry frost. This Mediterranean experience, gained with other species of the genus, is useful for gardens with a similar climate, such as coastal California or southern Australia.
  • Space: allow plenty of room. The crown of an adult, with leaves 1.5 to 3 m long and spines on the lower part of each leaf, rules out planting near a path. The PACSOA notes how hazardous it is to collect seed from cones sitting in the crown.

Container cultivation

Young plants are trunkless and grow slowly, which makes them easy to keep in a pot for some years. Use a deep container with a very free-draining mix, water thoroughly in summer and keep the plant on the dry side in winter. No published report describes container growing over the long term.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney grows it in several beds; its Garden Explorer lists eleven plants, some of wild origin, from accessions made in 1979, 1992, 1997 and 2016 (updated 1 October 2026). The census of Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria, downloaded on 19 October 2026, records four plants: three at Cranbourne (two planted in 2012, one in 2024) and one in the cycad collection at Melbourne. The Australian Botanic Garden Mount Annan, the Australian National Botanic Gardens in Canberra, the Botanic Garden of South Australia in Adelaide and the North Coast Regional Botanic Garden in Coffs Harbour also declare it to BGCI.
  • United States: ten institutions declare it to BGCI, among them the Huntington (San Marino), Lotusland (Santa Barbara), the Los Angeles County Arboretum, Descanso Gardens, San Diego Botanic Garden, the San Diego Zoo, the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley, Naples Botanical Garden in Florida and the University of Hawaii at Hilo. Montgomery Botanical Center also declares it to BGCI, but the species does not appear in its online catalogue.
  • United Kingdom: RHS Garden Wisley, in Surrey, is the only British garden that declares it to BGCI.

Twenty-two institutions in all declare the species to BGCI (checked on 26 October 2026), the others in China, Spain, France and the Netherlands. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not necessarily a living plant on display today. No English-language grower's report with a date, a place and a temperature has been found.

Propagation

Propagation is by seed; offsets are exceptional. Clean the seeds wearing gloves, as the flesh is toxic, then sow them in a warm, free-draining mix. A grower in France reported on the Fous de palmiers forum in January 2013 that two of three seeds received from the forum's seed bank germinated less than a week after sowing in vermiculite. A grower in Ravenna (Emilia-Romagna, Italy) reports on the Tropicamente forum that in October 2009 the kernels of his seeds rattled in their shells on arrival; after soaking them for a few days, he placed two on his internet router for a little bottom heat, and one had already germinated a few days later.

As in other Macrozamia, a male plant, a female plant and, outside Australia, hand pollination are needed to obtain seed. The PACSOA cites a plant grown in Brisbane that produced several offsets, perhaps after being damaged.

Pests and diseases

No pest specific to the species is documented. The New South Wales Scientific Committee notes that some cycads are susceptible to root rot caused by Phytophthora cinnamomi, although the susceptibility of Macrozamia johnsonii is unknown. In cultivation, poorly drained soil remains the main cause of rot in the genus, and scale insects are the pest most often mentioned on growers' forums for Macrozamia.

Cold hardiness

Data are scarce: a single dated report, obtained under protection, in western France. No value has been measured by a botanic garden or an institution, and no source gives a USDA zone for the species.

Mediterranean and European experience

  • A grower in Héric (Loire-Atlantique, France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his Macrozamia johnsonii, planted in the garden and protected by fleece or an upturned pot, survived a night at about −10 °C (14 °F) in February 2012, the other nights staying at about −8 °C (17.6 °F), with three days without thaw. The leaves had turned yellow and brown by mid-February, and the plant produced its first new leaf at the end of May 2012. He wondered whether the damp trapped under the protection had done more harm than the cold.

This report comes from the Atlantic side of France, north of Nantes. No dated report is documented for the Mediterranean coast. In 2014, a member of the same forum relayed the ranking of an Australian nurseryman specialising in cycads, who placed Macrozamia johnsonii among the “relatively hardy” species of the New South Wales coast, behind the inland species; the ranking gives no temperature. The prudent course is therefore dry protection during frosts and perfectly drained soil.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No dated cultivation result has been published. In the wild, the species meets frosts on the ranges between Grafton and Glen Innes, but the climate of Glen Innes (71 frost days a year, weekly lows of −3.9 °C (25.0 °F) in July) describes the tablelands above its range, not what the plant can withstand.

Toxicity and precautions

Yes, it should be treated as toxic, even though no data have been published for this species in particular. Macrozamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a powerful toxin of the liver; the seeds are the most dangerous part, and the leaves, especially young ones, cause irreversible nervous damage to the hindquarters of cattle (Healy, 1967). Keep the plant and its seeds out of reach of children, dogs and cats, wear gloves to clean seeds and, in case of ingestion, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No use specific to Macrozamia johnsonii is documented. The historical sources brought together by Asmussen (2011) show that Macrozamia seeds were eaten in New South Wales after long processing, but they do not single out this species, described only in 1992.

Frequently asked questions

Does Macrozamia johnsonii grow in Queensland?

No. Macrozamia johnsonii is endemic to northern New South Wales, in the Dalmorton district south-west of Grafton. Some profiles wrongly place it in the Queensland highlands. Its close relative Macrozamia moorei is the Queensland species.

How can it be told from Macrozamia moorei?

By the leaves. Those of Macrozamia johnsonii are flat in section, mid-green and highly glossy; those of Macrozamia moorei are grey-green, and that species forms a taller, more massive trunk. The seedlings differ too. Without a reliable label, the geographical origin of the seed remains the best clue.

Is Macrozamia johnsonii frost-hardy?

Partly. The only dated report, from western France, records survival of a night at about −10 °C (14 °F) in February 2012, under fleece, at the cost of damaged foliage and a recovery in May. A single observation is no guarantee: protect the plant from wet as soon as frost is forecast.

How big does it get?

It is one of the large Macrozamia. The trunk reaches 3 m according to the Flora of Australia, more according to other sources, with a diameter of 50 to 90 cm, and the leaves reach 3 m. Growth is slow, especially in the early years.

Is the species endangered?

Yes. The IUCN classes it as Vulnerable (2022), and it is listed as Endangered in Australia, both federally and in New South Wales. Its range covers only a few thousand hectares, much of it in production forest.

Is Macrozamia johnsonii poisonous?

Yes, like all Macrozamia. The seeds and young leaves are the most dangerous parts, for people as for animals. Handle the seeds with gloves and keep them out of reach of children and pets.
