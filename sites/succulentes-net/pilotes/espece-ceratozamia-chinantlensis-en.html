Ceratozamia chinantlensis is a cycad of the limestone cliffs of La Chinantla, in northern Oaxaca, Mexico, where it grows in tropical rainforest between 100 and 700 m (about 330 to 2,300 ft). Described in 2024, this species of the genus Ceratozamia stands out for its bronze young leaves, its very leathery, long and broad leaflets and its copper-coloured leaflet bases. Only four small populations are known, and its describers consider it Critically Endangered.

How to recognise Ceratozamia chinantlensis

It is recognised by its young leaves, bronze and bloomy when they emerge before turning avocado green, by its few, very long, broad and extremely leathery leaflets, and by the leaflet articulation, which turns light copper on the upper side. The original description (Pérez-Farrera and colleagues, 2024) gives the following measurements:

  • Trunk: short, cylindrical and thick, rarely forked, upright in young plants and lying down with age, 15 to 80 cm tall and 10 to 19 cm across; the plant grows on rock.
  • Leaves: 3 to 8 per crown, forming an open crown, upright at first and then drooping, 1.65 to 3.31 m long and 33 to 64 cm wide, bronze when they emerge; petiole 52 to 143 cm, papaya orange at the base, armed with short, thick prickles; rachis 77 to 184 cm, sometimes wavy.
  • Leaflets: 6 to 14 pairs, long, linear to oblanceolate, extremely leathery, with rolled-under margins; median leaflets 28.8 to 51 cm by 5.8 to 8.3 cm, 5.6 to 18.2 cm apart; 38 to 46 conspicuous veins; articulation light green at first, then light copper.
  • Male cones: solitary, conical, upright, 21.4 to 30.5 cm long and about 4 cm across (measured dry).
  • Female cones: solitary, cylindrical, upright, 17 to 18 cm by about 9 cm, beige-brown, with an acuminate tip; scales avocado green, with horns pointing downwards.
  • Seeds: ovoid, with cream flesh before maturity.

Hybrids

No natural or garden hybrid has been documented.

Possible confusion

Its geographical neighbours look little like it; the most similar species grow far away, in Chiapas:

  • Ceratozamia santillanii and Ceratozamia zoquorum have slender, elongated trunks, green young leaves, yellow articulations and shorter, narrower leaflets; these are the two species with which it could be confused, and the describers compared it with them;
  • Ceratozamia aurantiaca, its neighbour in northern Oaxaca, has a robust trunk, large, very prickly leaves and orange young leaves;
  • Ceratozamia miqueliana has oblong, papery leaflets and a more massive trunk;
  • Ceratozamia whitelockiana has green young leaves, papery leaflets and a different kind of trunk.

Taxonomy

Ceratozamia chinantlensis is one of the most recently described species of the genus. It is accepted by The World List of Cycads and by the WCVP, which records its publication in Taxonomy 4, page 738.

In early 2024, while searching for populations of Zamia magnifica, another cliff-dwelling cycad of La Chinantla, Miguel Angel Pérez-Farrera and his team found an unknown Ceratozamia; S.M. Ramírez-Oviedo, the second author of the article, had found the same population independently in mid-2023. The authors visited four populations six times, compared 22 adult plants with 19 individuals of Ceratozamia santillanii and 19 of Ceratozamia zoquorum, and measured eleven vegetative characters. Their discriminant analysis separates the three species almost completely: the only errors were one plant of Ceratozamia chinantlensis classified as Ceratozamia santillanii, and one the other way round. The research was funded by the Plant Exploration Fund of Montgomery Botanical Center.

The species was published in 2024 in the journal Taxonomy. Its position in the family tree of the genus has not yet been studied genetically, and the authors call for further analyses.

Etymology and history

The epithet refers to La Chinantla, the region of Oaxaca the species comes from (original description, quoted by The World List of Cycads).

The type specimen, a male plant, was collected on 28 June 2024 at La Chinantla, in the district of Tuxtepec, by Miguel Angel Pérez-Farrera, A.G. Rocha-Loredo, M.G. Martínez-Martínez and G. Moreno-Méndez (number 4466), according to the original description; The World List of Cycads names S.M. Ramírez-Oviedo instead of A.G. Rocha-Loredo among the collectors. The holotype is kept in the HEM herbarium, with isotypes at XAL and MEXU. The authors stress that local people actively protect these plants and that their work was only possible with their permission.

Common names

No vernacular name has been recorded, in English, Spanish or the Chinantec language.

In the wild

Ceratozamia chinantlensis appears to be restricted to La Chinantla, a mountainous region of northern Oaxaca at the foot of the Sierra Juárez, home to one of the largest tropical rainforests in Mexico. The climate is extremely wet, with 3,600 to 5,800 mm (about 140 to 230 in) of rain a year according to the authors. The species grows on carbonate cliffs and rocks, in evergreen tropical forest, between 100 and 700 m; all individuals grow on rock. The associated flora includes Brosimum alicastrum, Cedrela odorata, Clusia guatemalensis, several Chamaedorea, the palm Astrocaryum mexicanum, and species of Anthurium and Philodendron. The WCVP gives its range as south-western Mexico.

The authors see La Chinantla as a refuge for ancient lineages isolated by the drying of the climate since the Miocene; Ceratozamia chinantlensis and Zamia magnifica, very different from their neighbours, would be relict species.

Conservation status and regulation

Ceratozamia chinantlensis has not been assessed by the IUCN: The World List of Cycads gives it as Not Evaluated (NE), and GBIF relays no Red List category for it (checked on 30 October 2026). Its describers propose the category Critically Endangered (CR): only four populations are known, each with fewer than 50 adults, within an area of no more than 15 km² (about 6 square miles). Most populations nevertheless show seedling recruitment. The threats cited are habitat destruction, linked in the past to the extraction of Dioscorea mexicana and vanilla (Vanilla planifolia), and today to coffee and maize growing.

Like the whole genus, it has been listed in CITES Appendix I since 1 August 1985, and in Annex A of Regulation (EC) No 338/97 in the European Union (annexes now set by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026). Species+ does not list this recent name separately, but the listing covers the whole genus, seeds included (checked on 30 October 2026).

This general information does not replace the official texts: check the regulations in force before buying or exchanging plants, ask the seller for proof of origin, and prefer plants raised from cultivated seed.

Growing Ceratozamia chinantlensis

No cultivation of this species has been documented, in botanic gardens or private collections, and no commercial offer is known. Its habitat, cliffs in hot, shaded and extremely wet forest, would be hard to reproduce outside a tropical glasshouse; this is deduced from the habitat.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

No outdoor planting has been documented. Only humid tropical gardens, without frost or dry spells, would come close to its habitat; this is inferred, not tested.

Container cultivation

No container-growing report has been published. A plant of rock faces with few, very long leaves, it would need a heated, humid glasshouse and a very free-draining, stony compost; this is deduced from its habitat.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

No botanic garden declares the species to BGCI PlantSearch, and it does not appear in the online catalogue of Montgomery Botanical Center (both checked on 30 October 2026). The species, described in 2024, does not appear to be in cultivation in the UK, the US or Australia.

Propagation

No data. The plant has separate male and female individuals; the description reports seedlings in most populations, which shows that seeds germinate in the wild. Seedlings bear two first leaves (eophylls).

Pests and diseases

No pest or disease has been documented.

Cold hardiness

There are no data on its cold tolerance. The species lives at low altitude, in a very rainy tropical climate: nothing suggests any tolerance of frost.

Mediterranean and European experience

No cultivation has been reported in France or Italy. At the Jardin zoologique tropical in La Londe-les-Maures (Var, on the French Riviera), no observation of it has been published.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No cold record and no commercial hardiness rating exist. Anywhere outside the humid tropics, it would need a heated glasshouse.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data exist for this species or the genus. The risk is documented in other cycads, whose seeds contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a compound harmful to the liver and nervous system. Treat the plant as poisonous to people and pets; if any part is swallowed, call a poison centre, a doctor or a vet straight away.

Traditional uses

No use has been documented.

Frequently asked questions

When was Ceratozamia chinantlensis discovered?

In 2023 and 2024, during botanical explorations in La Chinantla, northern Oaxaca. It was described in 2024.

Where does it grow?

Only in La Chinantla, on limestone cliffs in very wet tropical forest, between 100 and 700 m.

How can it be recognised?

By its bronze young leaves, its 6 to 14 pairs of very leathery leaflets, 29 to 51 cm long and 6 to 8 cm wide, and its copper-coloured articulations.

Is it endangered?

Very probably. It has not yet been assessed by the IUCN, but its describers propose Critically Endangered: four populations of fewer than 50 adults each, within 15 km² at most.

Can it be grown?

No cultivation is documented. A cliff plant of extremely wet forest, it would need a hot glasshouse; any plant should come from artificial propagation, with CITES documents.

Is it cold-hardy?

Nobody knows, but nothing suggests it: it lives at low altitude in a very rainy tropical climate.
