Encephalartos pterogonus is a green-leaved cycad from central Mozambique, known with certainty from a single mountain: Mount Muruwere (Monte Urueri), a gneiss inselberg north of the town of Chimoio in Manica Province. This species of the genus Encephalartos was collected in 1949 and described in 1969, when two South African botanists split up the Encephalartos manikensis complex. Its most distinctive feature is found on the male cones, whose scales are drawn out into wing-like, often toothed angles. Heavily collected from the wild, it is assessed as Critically Endangered by the IUCN (assessment published in 2022), and some 1,000 seedlings were planted back on the mountain in 2003–2004.

Key facts about Encephalartos pterogonus

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos pterogonus R.A.Dyer & I.Verd. (1969), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15), IPNI, GBIF and Species+
  • Former names and synonyms: no synonym; the plants were previously included in Encephalartos manikensis in the broad sense
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: toothed-cone cycad (Flora of Mozambique, Species+); “winged cycad” is used by a nursery and an online compilation
  • Natural range: Mozambique, Manica Province: Mount Muruwere, the only confirmed population; endemic to Mozambique
  • IUCN status: Critically Endangered (CR B1ab(ii,iii,v)), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated April 2020, published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I since 1977, like the whole genus; Annex A in the European Union, with a negative EU opinion (2013) on artificially propagated plants from South Africa
  • Documented hardiness: a single, incomplete report: foliage damaged after one night at −1 °C (30.2 °F) in Apulia, southern Italy, in December 2009 (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, gneiss rock outcrops, mainly at forest margins; in southern California, full or partial sun on the coast and inland, filtered light in desert areas, well-drained soil (Jungle Music)
  • Habit and adult size: stout trunks branching from the base, up to 1.5 m tall and about 40 cm in diameter; leaves about 1.5 m; male cones about 38 cm, female cone about 40 cm long (original description, Dyer and Verdoorn 1969)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Montgomery Botanical Center (accession of 1984), Fairchild, the Huntington, Lotusland, the San Diego Botanic Garden, the San Diego Zoo and the National Tropical Botanical Garden in the United States; Royal Botanic Garden Sydney and Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria (outdoor bed in Melbourne) in Australia; no British garden declares it
  • Toxicity: not studied for the species; seeds toxic, as in all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos pterogonus

The character that defines the species is on the male cones: the lateral angles of the swollen outer part of the scales are acute, sometimes drawn out into wings and toothed. Haynes (2022), followed by The World List of Cycads, explains the name in exactly these terms. Without cones the plant looks very like Encephalartos manikensis, and the origin of the plant then becomes the best clue.

The original description by Dyer and Verdoorn (1969, pp. 151–152) gives the following characters:

  • Trunks: stout, branching from the base, up to about 1.5 m tall and 40 cm in diameter, covered with alternating series of leaf bases and cataphylls; cataphylls narrowly ovate, acuminate, woolly when young
  • Leaves: about 1.5 m long, oblong-elliptic in outline; rachis at first straight and almost erect, spreading with age; bare base of the petiole 9 to 18 cm, more or less spiny, woolly below, then glabrous; basal swelling (pulvinus) 6 to 7 cm, woolly on the outside
  • Leaflets: green, fairly thick, crowded and overlapping, especially in the upper half of the leaf, held in a V towards the tip; the median ones inserted 1.5 to 2 cm apart, 15 to 18 cm long and 2 to 2.5 cm wide, with a slightly asymmetrical base and three or four strong spines on each margin, usually towards the base; tip usually turned up and pungent
  • Male cones: narrowly ovoid-oblong, up to about 38 cm long and 11 cm in diameter, on a woolly stalk about 10 cm long; scales very tightly packed, hiding the axis, even more crowded than in Encephalartos manikensis; median scales horizontal, elongated-triangular, about 3.5 cm long and 4.5 cm wide, with acute, wing-like lateral angles, often toothed or lobed, which project beyond the terminal facet (about 2 by 1.5 cm, slightly downy)
  • Female cone: subcylindrical or barrel-shaped, up to about 40 cm long and 17 cm in diameter near the base, on a woolly stalk about 8 cm long; median scales about 4 cm long, with an outer face 5.5 cm wide and acute, sometimes slightly toothed lateral angles
  • Seeds: scarlet or orange-red, 3.5 to 4 cm long and 2 cm in diameter including the fleshy coat

The authors note that the terminal facet, on a rather short “nose”, appears set back, and that it has the same size and shape in both sexes. In their key to vegetative characters, the species is recognised by its fairly regularly overlapping leaflets, closed or almost closed like a shutter, often held in a V, and by their pronounced spines, usually more than 5 mm long. The online compilation LLIFLE gives similar figures, but slightly smaller seeds (30 to 35 mm), exceptional trunks of 2.1 m and two or three cones per plant, details that the original description does not give.

Jungle Music, a nursery in Encinitas, southern California, describes two-toned leaflets, dark green above and paler beneath, petioles often covered with white wool, moderately sharp leaflets and petioles, green female cones with a slight bluish tinge and yellowish-green male cones with wing-like appendages. It gives leaves of 1 to 1.5 m, which tend to spread widely, and stems that rarely exceed 1.5 to 2 m, single or multiple.

Hybrids

No hybrid of Encephalartos pterogonus has been described, either in the wild or in cultivation.

Possible confusion

Encephalartos pterogonus is above all confused with Encephalartos manikensis, of which it long formed part, and with the other species of the same complex, such as Encephalartos munchii, described in the same 1969 paper. Dyer and Verdoorn separate it from Encephalartos manikensis by the shorter outer face of the scales, the more acute lateral angles of the female cone scales, the winged and sometimes toothed male cone scales and the less prominent terminal facets; its tightly packed male cones do not resemble the loose cones of Encephalartos munchii and Encephalartos chimanimaniensis. According to LLIFLE, its foliage is very similar to that of Encephalartos manikensis and its cones recall those of the Zimbabwean form of that species; only the winged scales of the male cones allow a sure identification. Jungle Music places it in what it calls the “manikensis alliance”, a group of similar plants spread mainly between Mozambique and Zimbabwe, and warns that nursery plants are hard to verify before they are old enough to cone. Plants without cones and without a known origin should therefore be labelled with caution.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos pterogonus is an accepted name in The World List of Cycads, IPNI, GBIF and Species+. R.A. Dyer and I.C. Verdoorn described it in 1969 in the journal Kirkia (7(1): 151), in the paper “Encephalartos manikensis and its near allies”, which separated several new species from Encephalartos manikensis, including Encephalartos munchii. The type (holotype) is R.C. Munch's number 451, collected in October 1949 on Mount Mruwere, north of Vila Pery (now Chimoio); it is kept at the National Herbarium in Pretoria (PRE), with a duplicate at the National Herbarium of Zimbabwe in Harare (SRGH). The original description associates three collections by Dyer and Verdoorn (5862 to 5864, PRE) with it and illustrates a male plant from the type locality grown in Munch's garden near Rusape. The World List of Cycads gives no synonym.

Its rank is still debated. Kew's fact sheet on Mount Muruwere reports that some authors, including P. Capela (2006), consider that the species lacks characters constant enough to be kept separate and see it as a mere form of the manikensis complex; Kew retains it as a species, since it is accepted by most authorities. No genetic study has been published to settle the question.

Etymology and history

According to Haynes (2022), followed by The World List of Cycads, the epithet combines pteron (“wing”) and gonas, which the author renders as “seed”, a reference to the acute, sometimes wing-like and toothed lateral angles of the cone scales, especially those of the male cones. The meaning of the second element has not been checked in other works: the original description only says that the epithet alludes to the acute, sometimes wing-like and toothed lateral angles of the outer face of the scales, especially in the male cones, without discussing the roots. Jungle Music translates it as “wing and reproductive part”.

The plant was collected in 1949 by Raymond C. Munch, a cycad collector from Rusape in Zimbabwe, after whom Encephalartos munchii is named; it was only described twenty years later. According to Dyer and Verdoorn, Munch had found a uniform colony on Mount Mruwere, and it was at his suggestion that they took up the study of the complex again in 1966, working mainly from the living plants in his large rock garden, without seeing the plants in the wild. LLIFLE, which cites the 2010 IUCN assessment, states that most plants were later removed by collectors and that the species was declared extinct in its habitat as early as 1975; this date could not be checked at source. Kew's fact sheet reports that in the mid-2000s at least 246 adult plants still grew on the mountain, and that a reintroduction programme was launched by the “Plantas de Moçambique” project, which planted 1,000 seedlings (in 2003 or 2004, depending on the passage of the fact sheet), of which 300 to 400 survived (Capela, 2006). Kew adds that no monitoring has been published since.

Common names

The Flora of Mozambique and Species+ give the English name toothed-cone cycad. “Winged cycad”, used by Jungle Music and by LLIFLE, is a trade or informal name not attested by a reference source. No name in a local language is attested.

In the wild

Encephalartos pterogonus is known with certainty only from Mount Muruwere. The fact sheet of the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, on the Muruwere-Bossa Tropical Important Plant Area, by S. Richards and I. Darbyshire, describes two inselbergs 30 km north-north-east of Chimoio, in Manica District: Mount Muruwere, the higher, and Mount Bossa. Their rock is a gneiss derived from granite. The species grows on the rock outcrops of Muruwere, particularly at forest margins; the forests occupy mainly the ravines and the plateaus with deeper soils.

Other sites remain to be confirmed: the species may also grow on Mount Dengalenga, 5 km to the west (Donaldson, 2010, cited by Kew and by Jungle Music). LLIFLE gives an altitude of 700 to 1,000 m, an extent of occurrence of 35 km² and an area of occupancy of less than 10 km². The study of the endemic plants of Mozambique (Darbyshire et al., 2019) lists the species as strictly endemic to the country, in Manica Province.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos pterogonus is assessed as Critically Endangered (CR) on the IUCN Red List under criterion B1ab(ii,iii,v): a very small range, with a continuing decline in area of occupancy, habitat quality and number of mature plants. The assessment, by J.D. Bösenberg, is dated 9 April 2020 and was published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, GBIF, Crossref). The Flora of Mozambique gives the same category. Jungle Music cites an unattributed report of fewer than ten plants left in the wild, a figure that Kew's data do not support.

The main threat is collecting: Kew points out that it is a rare, ornamental cycad sought after by collectors, and that such a small population risks reproductive failure. The steep slopes of the inselberg give some passive protection, but the surrounding plain was largely converted to farmland between 2004 and 2009, leaving the mountain forests more exposed. The site lies in no protected area; Kew considers that it could qualify as an Alliance for Zero Extinction site. A new survey was planned under a University of Kent project supported by the Mohamed bin Zayed Species Conservation Fund.

Regulation, according to Species+ (consulted through its public API on 26 October 2026):

  • CITES: Appendix I since 4 February 1977, like the whole genus;
  • European Union: Annex A, current list in Regulation (EU) 2026/1383 (in force since 29 June 2026); negative opinion of the EU Scientific Review Group of 7 February 2013, still current, on artificially propagated plants imported from South Africa (source codes A and D); past EU and CITES trade suspensions for wild plants from Mozambique (2008–2018), no longer in force.

Any plant offered for sale must come from cultivation and carry the CITES documents required by the countries concerned (and, in the European Union, the EU documents). These notes do not replace the official texts: check Species+ and the national authorities before any purchase or import, and ask for proof that the plant was raised in cultivation.

Growing Encephalartos pterogonus

No reference source describes the cultivation of the species in detail, and the few available accounts come from a nursery, a compilation and growers' forums. LLIFLE presents it as vigorous, suited to subtropical climates, growing in sun or partial shade in a deep, well-drained soil with regular watering and feeding; it describes it as very hard to bring into cone and sensitive to frost. These statements have not been confirmed by another source.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

In southern California, Jungle Music reports that plants are doing well in a variety of gardens. It recommends full or partial sun on the coast and inland, filtered light or shade in desert areas, and a well-drained soil, adding that the species tolerates watering. As the leaves spread widely, it advises planting it back from the edges of a bed; it describes its growth as moderate, with new leaves flushed every year. In Australia, Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria lists the species in an outdoor bed of its Melbourne Gardens (living collection census, October 2026), which shows that it can be grown in the open in a mild temperate climate, though the census gives no planting date or winter data.

Container cultivation

In Europe, the species is rare in collections and the few documented plants are young and kept in pots under cover in winter. A grower in Agen (Lot-et-Garonne, south-west France) reported on the Fous de palmiers forum that he had almost lost a young seedling in February 2009, then that in December 2009, with his cycads brought indoors for the winter, the plant was producing a new leaf under shelter. A grower in Dirinon (Finistère, Brittany) mentioned a seedling of the species among his cycad seedlings in January 2017. In Italy, a member of the Tropicamente forum showed in 2013 a plant with a caudex about 16 cm across, bought the year before, and reported in July 2015 that it had just produced new leaves after two years without growth; the place of cultivation is not given.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

BGCI PlantSearch lists 15 institutions holding the species, none of them in the United Kingdom. In the United States, the Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida) lists one accession, of 1984, in its online catalogue; BGCI also lists Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden, the Huntington, Lotusland, the San Diego Botanic Garden, the San Diego Zoo and the National Tropical Botanical Garden in Hawaii. In Australia, the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney and Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria list it in their online catalogues. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not a living plant on display today. In Italy, the Orto Botanico of Messina included it in the list of its cycad collection published in February 2009. Jungle Music describes the species as extremely rare in the trade, usually available only in small sizes and in limited numbers, and expensive.

Propagation

Propagation is by seed, and basal suckers, when the plant produces them, can be separated; Jungle Music notes that plants may be single-stemmed or multi-stemmed. The reintroduction programme on Mount Muruwere shows that seedlings can be raised in numbers, but the origin of the seed is not stated. No protocol specific to the species has been published; the general methods used for Encephalartos seed apply (cleaned seed, a free-draining sowing medium kept warm and moist but not wet). Seeds, plants and suckers are all covered by CITES Appendix I.

Pests and diseases

No data specific to Encephalartos pterogonus have been published. Like other cycads, it is above all at risk from root and trunk rot in a waterlogged medium, especially in cool weather.

Cold hardiness

No minimum temperature has been published for Encephalartos pterogonus, and the only dated report concerns damage at a slight frost. As with all Encephalartos, survivals in cold areas often concern young plants whose caudex is still small or underground, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil and sometimes by snow or a cover; adult plants with an aerial trunk do not benefit from this protection.

Mediterranean and European experience

  • Italy: a grower in Nardò (Apulia, southern Italy) reports on the Tropicamente forum that the foliage of his Encephalartos pterogonus was damaged in December 2009 after a single night at −1 °C (30.2 °F), which came between two days close to 20 °C (68 °F); Encephalartos manikensis, Encephalartos concinnus, Encephalartos chimanimaniensis, Encephalartos munchii and Encephalartos ferox, then in growth, were affected in the same way. He put the damage down to the sudden drop in temperature. Whether the plants were in pots or in the ground, and protected or not, is not stated. A year earlier, the same grower counted pterogonus among the species that, in his experience, do not survive a frost; this general opinion, without a dated episode, is indicative only.
  • France: no result in the ground has been documented; the few messages on the Fous de palmiers forum concern young seedlings kept under cover in winter.

These reports suggest treating the species as frost-tender in Mediterranean climates such as coastal California and southern Australia, and growing it under glass in the United Kingdom.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

  • United States: Jungle Music reports that the species does well in southern California gardens, without giving temperatures.
  • Australia: a plant grows in an outdoor bed in Melbourne (Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria census), without published winter data.
  • United Kingdom: no hardiness data have been published; no British garden declares the species.

LLIFLE classes the species as frost-sensitive, while noting that it survives in warm temperate regions, sometimes losing its leaves in hard winters. No USDA zone is documented for Encephalartos pterogonus in a reliable source.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data specific to Encephalartos pterogonus have been published, but the risk is documented for the genus. Like other cycads, Encephalartos contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; these substances affect the liver, the digestive tract and the nervous system, and the seeds are the most dangerous part. The toxicity of a species of the genus, Encephalartos horridus, was demonstrated as early as 1944 by Van der Walt. For another cycad, the sago palm (Cycas revoluta), the ASPCA reports vomiting, black stools, jaundice, haemorrhagic gastroenteritis, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses, caused by cycasin.

Wear gloves to handle seeds and their fleshy coat, pick up fallen seeds, and keep plants and seeds out of reach of children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use specific to Encephalartos pterogonus is documented. Today the species is grown only as a collector's ornamental.

Frequently asked questions

Where does Encephalartos pterogonus grow?

Only in Mozambique, with certainty on Mount Muruwere, north of Chimoio in Manica Province. Another site, Mount Dengalenga, 5 km to the west, remains to be confirmed.

What does pterogonus mean?

The name refers to wings. It alludes to the angles of the male cone scales, drawn out into wings that are sometimes toothed.

Is it just a form of Encephalartos manikensis?

Not according to the reference checklists. The World List of Cycads accepts it as a species, but some authors, such as P. Capela (2006), regard it as a form of the manikensis complex; no genetic study has settled the question.

Is it endangered?

Yes. It is assessed as Critically Endangered by the IUCN (2022), after heavy collecting from the wild; 1,000 seedlings were planted back on the mountain in 2003–2004, of which 300 to 400 survived.

How can it be identified without cones?

With difficulty. Its leaves look like those of Encephalartos manikensis; only the winged scales of the male cones and a known origin allow a sure identification.

Is it frost-hardy?

No, according to the only report available: a single night at −1 °C (30.2 °F) damaged its foliage in Apulia in 2009. Grow it frost-free, outdoors only in mild, frost-free climates such as coastal southern California.

Can it be grown in a pot?

Yes, at least while young: the documented European plants are young potted plants, brought under cover in winter. Use a very free-draining mix and avoid waterlogging.

Is it poisonous?

Yes, very probably. The species itself has not been studied, but Encephalartos are toxic, especially their seeds.
