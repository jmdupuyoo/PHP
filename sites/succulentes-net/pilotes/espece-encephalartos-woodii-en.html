Encephalartos woodii, Wood's cycad, is a large South African cycad that is extinct in the wild, known from a single male clump found in 1895 by the botanist John Medley Wood on the edge of the Ngoye forest, in what is now KwaZulu-Natal. Every plant grown in the world comes from offsets of that clump: they are clones, all male, and no female plant has ever been found, so the species cannot reproduce from seed. This species of the genus Encephalartos, first named Encephalartos altensteinii var. bispinnus by Wood himself, is also one of the most vigorous in cultivation. Its nature is still debated: a genetic study finds it very close to Encephalartos natalensis and suggests a hybrid origin. It is sometimes called “the loneliest plant in the world”.

Key facts about Encephalartos woodii

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos woodii Sander (1908), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and Species+
  • Synonym: Encephalartos altensteinii var. bispinnus J.M.Wood (1907), according to The World List of Cycads
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Wood's cycad (PlantZAfrica, Species+); Wood-se-broodboom in Afrikaans (PlantZAfrica)
  • Natural range: South Africa, KwaZulu-Natal: a single locality, a steep south-facing slope on the edge of the Ngoye forest, about 30 km from Mtunzini (PlantZAfrica)
  • IUCN status: Extinct in the Wild (EW), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated 20 May 2020 and published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I since 1977, like the whole genus; Annex A in the European Union, with a negative EU opinion, in force since 2013, for artificially propagated plants from South Africa
  • Documented hardiness: no measured value; PlantZAfrica places it in frost-free or lightly frosted horticultural zones (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: full sun or partial shade, light shade in hot, dry regions; fertile, well-drained soil, ample water in summer (PlantZAfrica)
  • Habit and adult size: trunk up to about 6 m, 40 to 60 cm in diameter, widening to 90 cm at the base; leaves 1.75 to 2.5 m, even 3 m long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Kew (BGCI; plant photographed there in 2024); the Huntington, Lotusland, Fairchild, Longwood Gardens, the United States Botanic Garden and others in the United States; Melbourne (outdoor beds) and James Cook University in Australia
  • Toxicity: cycad seeds are toxic; the species produces none, for lack of a female (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos woodii

It is a massive Encephalartos, with a trunk that widens at the base and a crown of long, glossy dark green leaves curving like an umbrella. Dyer (1965) describes it as follows, completed by PlantZAfrica:

  • Trunk: single or branched from the base, sometimes also near the crown in cultivation, up to about 6 m tall and 40 to 60 cm in diameter; in old plants it widens towards the base to 90 cm, forming a kind of buttress, and the leaf bases there are so compressed that the trunk looks smooth (PlantZAfrica); crown slightly woolly;
  • Leaves: 1.75 to 2.5 m long (2 to 3 m according to PlantZAfrica), gracefully curved, spreading or widely arching, forming a dense umbrella-shaped crown, even in young plants; at first covered with pale brown wool, which falls with age;
  • Leaflets: dark green, leathery and glossy, gradually reduced to prickles towards the base of the leaf; the median ones about 20 cm long and 5 cm wide, with a sharp or sometimes slightly hooked and blunt tip; on young offsets they carry two to five teeth on one or both margins, whereas on old trunks they are often entire;
  • Male cones (the only ones known): one to several per trunk according to Dyer, six to eight according to PlantZAfrica, bright orange-yellow, almost cylindrical, 40 to 90 cm long, rarely up to 1.2 m, and 15 to 20 cm in diameter; the Kirstenbosch plant produces them every two or three years.

As early as 1908, Sander stressed the texture and bright green of the large leaflets, the broadest irregularly cut into lobes ending in a spine, which in his view were enough to distinguish the species from all other Encephalartos. The oldest plants in the Durban botanic garden, planted more than a century ago, have a trunk more than 2 m in circumference and an estimated mass of 2.5 tonnes (PlantZAfrica).

Hybrids

No natural hybrid is known, since the species was found as a single plant. In cultivation, on the other hand, its pollen is widely used: at Kirstenbosch, pollen from the garden's plant has been used to create hybrids with several species in the collection (PlantZAfrica). The most closely followed cross is with Encephalartos natalensis, its closest relative: Viljoen and van Staden (2006) studied Encephalartos natalensis × Encephalartos woodii hybrids from a South African cycad breeding centre.

PlantZAfrica describes backcrossing as the most promising project: pollinating female Encephalartos natalensis with Encephalartos woodii, then the female offspring again with Encephalartos woodii, so that each generation comes closer to it. No published source presents the results of this programme. In Corse-du-Sud, a grower on the French Fous de palmiers forum reported in 2013 that he grew first-generation hybrids raised from seed given to him by Cynthia Giddy, who took part in this project; one of them then had two heads about 17 cm in diameter and leaves about 1.5 m long. On the same forum, another grower reported in 2015 that hybrids with Encephalartos transvenosus also recall the appearance of Encephalartos woodii; the Huntington, in California, declares an Encephalartos transvenosus × woodii hybrid to BGCI, and the Montgomery Botanical Center lists an Encephalartos natalensis × woodii hybrid and an Encephalartos horridus × woodii hybrid in its catalogue.

The trade in these hybrids calls for caution: in 2012, a grower in Ravenna who thought he had bought an Encephalartos natalensis × woodii learned from South African growers that his plant was more probably an Encephalartos senticosus or an Encephalartos msinganus (Tropicamente forum).

Possible confusion

The most likely confusion is with Encephalartos natalensis, which grows in the same region and whose broad-leafleted forms can look like it. In his key, Dyer (1965) separates them as follows:

  • Encephalartos woodii: leaves curved in an arch, forming an umbrella-shaped crown; median leaflets dark green, more or less ovate-lanceolate, up to 20 cm by 5 cm;
  • Encephalartos natalensis: leaves erect, straight or slightly recurved in their upper third; median leaflets lanceolate, 16 to 23 cm by 2.5 to 4.5 cm; male cones in twos or threes, dark yellowish green, about 45 cm long at most.

Prain (1916) noted that Encephalartos gratus, from Malawi, has leaflets of similar shape and toothing, but dull dark green, whereas those of Encephalartos woodii are bright green and glossy. Finally, at Ngoye itself, the old Zulu men questioned by B.J. Huntley in 1964 confused the species with Encephalartos ngoyanus, common on the neighbouring slopes, with Stangeria eriopus and even with a tree fern (Dyer, 1965). Without reliable documents of origin, a plant sold as Encephalartos woodii may be a hybrid or another species.

Taxonomy

Medley Wood first presented the plant, in the annual report of the Natal Botanic Gardens for 1906–1907, as a variety of Encephalartos altensteinii, var. bispinnus according to The World List of Cycads (Dyer writes bispinosa). The name Encephalartos woodii was published on 25 April 1908 by the English nursery firm Sander in The Gardeners' Chronicle (series 3, 43: 257), about a plant shown by Sander & Sons at the Ghent exhibition; Sander did not yet know whether the species would be accepted as distinct from the variable Encephalartos altensteinii. As no type had been designated, Dyer and Verdoorn (1966) chose as neotype a photograph of the Ngoye plant published in a supplement to The Gardeners' Chronicle of 2 May 1908 (The World List of Cycads).

Its status as a species is debated. PlantZAfrica summarises the views: a relic of an otherwise extinct species, a mutation or robust form of Encephalartos natalensis, or a natural hybrid between Encephalartos natalensis and Encephalartos ferox. Viljoen and van Staden (2006) compared, using RAPD markers, Encephalartos woodii, several Encephalartos natalensis, hybrids between the two and Encephalartos villosus: the genetic distance between Encephalartos woodii and Encephalartos natalensis barely exceeds the variation within Encephalartos natalensis, and Encephalartos woodii falls within that group. The authors conclude that it may have arisen from hybridisation between an ancestor close to Encephalartos natalensis and another related species, which would explain why it was found as a single plant, but they recommend analysing further taxa. The World List of Cycads continues to accept it as a species.

Etymology and history

The epithet honours John Medley Wood (1827–1915), curator and then director of the Natal Botanic Gardens (now Durban Botanic Gardens), who discovered the only known plant (Haynes, 2022, via The World List of Cycads).

The history, as reconstructed by Dyer (1965) from contemporary accounts, runs as follows:

  • 1895: during an ox-wagon expedition in Zululand, Wood found an isolated clump of four stems, the tallest about 18 feet (5.5 m), with a few offsets at the base; all were male, and no other plant of the species was found nearby (Wood's letter to The Gardeners' Chronicle, 1908);
  • around 1903 (“probably”, according to Dyer; Prain dates the introduction to 1899): the curator James Wylie brought back a few small offsets, three of which were planted in the Durban garden; according to an unpublished report by Wood, others were sent to Kew and to the Sander nursery;
  • 1907: Wylie returned for two of the large stems, still to be seen near the old conservatory of the Durban garden, and found that one of the four original stems had been mutilated;
  • around 1912: the American botanist Charles J. Chamberlain, guided to the site, saw only a single stem more than 10 feet (3 m) tall;
  • 1916: the last stem, damaged by fire and by cutting, was dug up by the forestry service and sent to the Division of Botany in Pretoria: it weighed 1,300 to 1,500 lb, for a length of 12 feet and a diameter of 18 inches. Dyer believed he had found it again in 1964 in the rockery of the Union Buildings and had it replanted in the National Botanic Garden in Pretoria; according to PlantZAfrica, this stem is thought to have died in 1964.

In 1964, B.J. Huntley spent three weeks at Ngoye without finding another plant; with the help of an old Zulu man, he only located the spot from which it had been removed. The IUCN action plan (Donaldson, 2003) considers that the presence of a single individual in 1895 points either to a species naturally rare at Ngoye or to a decline earlier than collecting and habitat destruction.

The Durban garden later supplied offsets to other gardens, which in turn distributed them (Donaldson, 2003). The Kirstenbosch plant, in Cape Town, is an offset of a Durban plant sent by Wylie in 1916; it was surrounded by a cage in the 1980s to prevent further theft of offsets (PlantZAfrica). The number of cultivated plants is unknown: “probably several hundred” according to the IUCN action plan, perhaps 500 according to PlantZAfrica.

Recent research

Two routes are being explored to obtain a female plant one day. The first is to search for a female in the Ngoye forest, which has never been fully surveyed: since 2022 a team led by the University of Southampton has been flying drones fitted with a multispectral sensor over the forest and training an image-recognition algorithm to pick out cycads by their shape; by May 2024, 195 acres (about 80 ha) of the forest's 10,000 acres (about 4,000 ha) had been covered, without result (university press release, 28 May 2024). The second is to induce a change of sex: PlantZAfrica (2002) mentioned this as a remote possibility, since sex reversal has been observed in a few other species, but according to the IUCN action plan (2003) all attempts had failed; the Southampton team has been investigating chemical or physiological methods since 2024. No female had been announced when the sources were consulted (October 2026).

Common names

In English, PlantZAfrica and Species+ use “Wood's cycad”; in Afrikaans, PlantZAfrica gives “Wood-se-broodboom”. “The loneliest plant in the world”, common in the press, is a nickname rather than a common name.

In the wild

Encephalartos woodii is extinct in the wild. The only known clump grew on a steep south-facing slope on the edge of a patch of the Ngoye forest, in Zululand (Dyer, 1965; PlantZAfrica), among Stangeria eriopus and Encephalartos ngoyanus (then called Encephalartos brachyphyllus), according to Chamberlain. Viljoen and van Staden (2006) recall that other cycads, including Encephalartos natalensis and Encephalartos villosus, are common in the region. PlantZAfrica adds that the area has never been systematically surveyed and that another plant could still be hiding there.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos woodii is listed as Extinct in the Wild (EW) on the IUCN Red List. The assessment, by J.D. Bösenberg, is dated 20 May 2020 and was published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, Crossref); the 2003 action plan and Species+ (which records it as extinct in South Africa) give the same status. GBIF displays it as Not Evaluated (NE), without reference to the 2022 assessment. It survives only in cultivation, in botanic gardens and private collections, as male clones of the original plant.

Regulation, according to Species+ (consulted through its public API on 26 October 2026):

  • CITES: Appendix I since 4 February 1977, like the whole genus;
  • European Union: Annex A, current list in Regulation (EU) 2026/1383 (in force since 29 June 2026); for artificially propagated plants from South Africa, a negative EU opinion covering the whole genus, issued on 3 December 2010, was lifted on 11 March 2011, but the EU issued a negative opinion specific to this species on 20 June 2011, in force since 7 February 2013; a new consultation on the whole genus has been open since 16 September 2026;
  • South Africa: the species is also protected by provincial legislation, and a permit is needed to move, buy, sell or own adult cycads in the Western Cape (PlantZAfrica).

Since no female exists, “Encephalartos woodii seeds” can only be seeds of hybrids, or a fraud. Any plant offered for sale must come from cultivation and carry the CITES documents required by the countries concerned (and, in the European Union, the EU documents). These notes do not replace the official texts: check Species+ and the national authorities before any purchase or import.

Growing Encephalartos woodii

Paradoxically, this species is easy to grow. According to PlantZAfrica, it is the fastest-growing Encephalartos and one of the most vigorous in cultivation: it produces many suckers and new leaves every year.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

PlantZAfrica's advice:

  • Soil: fertile and well drained, drainage being essential;
  • Watering: the plant withstands fairly long dry spells but looks better with regular watering; in summer, a weekly watering that soaks the whole root zone; watering can be suspended in winter;
  • Light: full sun, or morning or afternoon sun; in hot, dry inland regions the sun burns the leaves and light shade is better;
  • Care: a mulch of well-rotted compost at least once a year and a balanced fertiliser twice during the summer.

Jungle Music, a nursery at Encinitas near San Diego, recommends full or partial sun on the coast and inland, and filtered light or shade in desert areas, in a well-drained soil, as the species is susceptible to rot. Dyer (1965) noted that the trunk of the Durban plant photographed in 1945 had branched abnormally at the top and widened at the base, probably under the weight of the crown.

Container cultivation

According to PlantZAfrica, the plant does well in a pot when young but must be planted out as it grows: it soon fills the pot with roots and can burst it if not repotted. The page suggests a free-draining mix of coarse sand, milled bark and friable loam, with watering every other day in hot, windy summer weather, never letting the compost dry out completely while letting excess water drain away.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

BGCI PlantSearch lists 20 institutions declaring Encephalartos woodii. In the United Kingdom, Kew declares it, and a University of Southampton press release (2024) shows one of its researchers beside the Kew plant. The date of its arrival at Kew varies between secondary sources: Guinness World Records says Kew received one of the original stems in 1899, and a Naples Botanical Garden article says a piece was sent by Wood in 1895, whereas Dyer (1965) only reports, from an unpublished report by Wood, that offsets were sent to Kew and to Sander after Wylie's first collection, around 1903. In the United States, BGCI lists the Huntington, Lotusland, Fairchild, Longwood Gardens, the United States Botanic Garden, the Atlanta Botanical Garden, the Marie Selby gardens, the San Diego Botanic Garden, the San Diego Zoo and the Honolulu Botanical Gardens; Lotusland, at Montecito in California, describes three Encephalartos woodii presiding over the pond of its cycad garden, and the Naples Botanical Garden in Florida received one from Fairchild in October 2022. The Montgomery Botanical Center lists one accession of the pure species, whose number points to 2025. In Australia, Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria grows four plants in three outdoor beds at Melbourne Gardens according to its living collection census, and James Cook University at Townsville declares it. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not a living plant on display today. Jungle Music writes that it very rarely has an offset for sale, that offsets are very expensive and difficult to root, and that the species is easy to grow once established.

Propagation

The pure species can only be propagated by offsets. According to PlantZAfrica, they are removed in early spring with a clean, sharp spade or knife; they should be more than 10 cm in diameter, the largest having the best chance of survival. The cut surfaces of the parent and the offset are treated with flowers of sulphur, the offset is left for about three weeks in a cool, dry place for the wound to seal, then dusted with rooting hormone before planting, and kept moist and in light shade until new leaves appear. Jungle Music warns that removed offsets are very susceptible to rot and often die during their first years. Seedlings can only be hybrids, obtained by pollinating females of other species (see Hybrids). The removal and exchange of offsets are subject to CITES rules.

Pests and diseases

No disease or pest specific to the species has been reported; PlantZAfrica recalls that the young leaves of Encephalartos are grazed by sheep, antelope, dassies and baboons. The best documented risk is theft: offsets were stolen at Kirstenbosch, which led to the plant being caged in the 1980s (PlantZAfrica), and at the Naples botanic garden, in Italy, an offset was reportedly stolen around 2007–2008 according to an Italian grower (Tropicamente forum, 2010). Jungle Music adds that the species is susceptible to rot in poorly drained soil.

Cold hardiness

No measured, dated cold limit is documented for Encephalartos woodii. PlantZAfrica places it in South Africa's frost-free coastal horticultural zones and in the bushveld with light frost; it has no true dormancy, but its growth slows down in winter. As with other Encephalartos, survivals in cold areas often concern young plants whose caudex is still underground, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil; an adult plant with an aerial trunk, like those of this species, does not benefit from this protection.

Mediterranean and European experience

  • France: no dated report is documented for the pure species. The first-generation hybrids grown in Corse-du-Sud mentioned above were growing in 2013, without the growing conditions or minimum temperatures being stated.
  • Italy: the Naples botanic garden held one specimen in 1993–1994 (Gaudio et al., 1997), still listed in 2007; in 2010 a grower in Naples reported on the Tropicamente forum that another Encephalartos woodii in the garden had lost its leaves, without knowing whether it was still alive or saying whether it grew under glass or outdoors.

Given its value and rarity, a pure plant is best grown frost-free, in a large container overwintered under cover or in a glasshouse, except perhaps on the mildest Mediterranean coasts and in comparable climates such as coastal California or southern Australia.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

  • Australia: four plants grow in outdoor beds at Melbourne Gardens (Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria census), without published winter data.
  • United States: Lotusland grows three plants outdoors around the pond of its cycad garden in coastal southern California; neither the garden nor Jungle Music gives a cold limit for the species.
  • United Kingdom: no source read says how Kew's plant is grown, and no outdoor result is documented.

No USDA hardiness zone is documented for Encephalartos woodii in a botanical source.

Toxicity and precautions

Like other cycads, Encephalartos woodii contains toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a toxin affecting the liver and the nervous system; in cycads the seeds are the most dangerous part, and PlantZAfrica recalls that the kernel of Encephalartos seeds is extremely toxic. No study deals with this species, which produces no seed, but the plant must not be considered safe. Wear gloves to handle the seeds of hybrids, and keep plants and seeds out of reach of children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No use specific to the species is documented. PlantZAfrica recalls that Thunberg and other early travellers saw local people make a fermented meal from cycad trunks. Dyer (1965) reports that in 1907 one of the four original stems had been mutilated, and a forestry document of 1916 cited by Dyer mentioned cut stems; Dyer attributed this cutting to traditional healers.

Frequently asked questions

Why is it called “the loneliest plant in the world”?

Because only one plant, a male, was ever found, in 1895, and no female is known. All cultivated plants are clones of that male and cannot reproduce with one another.

Are there any female plants?

No, not to date. No female has been found despite several searches, including a drone survey of the Ngoye forest since 2022, and attempts to change the sex of a plant have not succeeded.

Can Encephalartos woodii seeds be bought?

No, not of the pure species. Without a female there are no Encephalartos woodii seeds; seeds sold under this name are at best hybrids.

Is it a true species or a hybrid?

The question remains open. A 2006 genetic study finds it very close to Encephalartos natalensis and suggests a hybrid origin, but its authors call for further analyses; The World List of Cycads accepts it as a species.

How many plants exist?

Probably a few hundred. The IUCN action plan spoke in 2003 of “several hundred” plants in cultivation, PlantZAfrica of about 500.

Is it easy to grow?

Yes, once established. PlantZAfrica calls it fast and vigorous, given a fertile, free-draining soil and regular watering in summer; offsets are, however, difficult to root, and it dislikes frost.

Is it poisonous?

It should be treated as such, like all cycads, even though the species has not been studied.
