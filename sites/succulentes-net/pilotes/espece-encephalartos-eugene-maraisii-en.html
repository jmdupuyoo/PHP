Encephalartos eugene-maraisii is a blue-green cycad endemic to the Waterberg massif in Limpopo, South Africa, where it grows among rocks on sandstone hills and ridges in grassland and savanna. This species of the genus Encephalartos was described in 1945 by the botanist Inez Verdoorn, who named it after her uncle, the writer and naturalist Eugène Marais, who had first brought it to her attention. Listed as Endangered by the IUCN, it has fewer than 700 adult plants left in the wild, and its numbers have fallen by more than half in thirty years because of poaching.

Key facts about Encephalartos eugene-maraisii

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos eugene-maraisii I.Verd. (1945), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15)
  • Former names and synonyms: Encephalartos eugene-maraisii subsp. eugene-maraisii (1988), a synonym; the Middelburg and Mica plants once included in the species are now Encephalartos middelburgensis and Encephalartos dyerianus
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Waterberg cycad (English, SANBI and Species+); Waterbergse broodboom, bergpalm, Eugène Marais-bergpalm, wilde dadel (Afrikaans)
  • Natural range: South Africa, Limpopo, Waterberg; at least two subpopulations about 100 km apart, within an extent of occurrence of about 1,290 km²
  • IUCN status: Endangered (EN), criteria A2ad+4ad; B1ab(v), assessment published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I (whole genus, since 1977); Annex A in the European Union; negative EU opinion on imports of artificially propagated plants from South Africa; wild collection banned in South Africa
  • Documented hardiness: a young plant in the ground in Concarneau, Brittany, kept a sound caudex through the winter of 2011–2012 with a low of −5 °C (23 °F); plants died outdoors in North Carolina in the winter of 2013–2014 at −15.6 to −12.8 °C (4 to 9 °F) (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, grassland and savanna on sandstone hills and rocky ridges; in cultivation, full sun and sandy or loamy, acid to neutral soil according to PlantZAfrica
  • Habit and adult size: aerial stems up to 2 m long according to SANBI, up to 4 m according to PlantZAfrica, 30 to 45 cm in diameter, often reclining and branching at the base; rigid leaves 0.7 to 1.5 m long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Montgomery Botanical Center in Florida, several Californian and Hawaiian gardens, the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney and Adelaide Botanic Garden; no British garden declares it to BGCI
  • Toxicity: the protologue records dizziness after eating the roasted kernels and children seriously ill after eating the seeds; treat all parts as poisonous (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos eugene-maraisii

Look for rigid, pale bluish-green leaves that arch slightly downwards with the tip turned a little upwards, on aerial stems that often lean or lie on the ground and branch at the base. In the original description (Verdoorn, 1945, Journal of South African Botany), Verdoorn separated it from Encephalartos lehmannii, which she regarded as its nearest relative, by the crown of narrowly acuminate woolly scales at the stem apex, the leaf tip curved inwards, the overlapping leaflets set very obliquely on the rachis and the amber-coloured seeds. Her description, based on the two plants of Vlakfontein and a few herbarium specimens, gives:

  • Trunk: cylindrical, about 1.3 m tall and up to 30 cm in diameter, suckering a little from the base, crowned by greyish-white, narrowly ovate, acuminate woolly scales that hide the petiole bases, themselves covered in white wool
  • Leaves: hairless, slightly glaucous (flaky when young), about 75 cm long, with the tip curved inwards; petiole 16 to 18 cm, woolly at the base
  • Leaflets: stiff, leathery, linear, about 16 cm long and 1.5 cm wide, wedge-shaped at the base, set so obliquely that the upper face looks towards the petiole; tip acuminate and pungent; margins entire, or the lower margin with one tooth; veins faint but visible
  • Male cones: stalked, roughly spindle-shaped, 22 to 42 cm long and 5 to 8 cm wide (dried); scales 2.75 to 3.25 cm
  • Female cone: about 30 cm long and 18 cm in diameter; outer face of the scales dark brown, about 4.5 by 2.5 cm, finely downy and slightly wrinkled
  • Seeds: amber, barely tinged red at the top, almost four-sided, 3.5 cm long and 2 to 3 cm in diameter, with little flesh

Verdoorn herself thought that the less silvery leaves, the visible veins and the seed colour might not be true diagnostic characters. In the same volume of the journal, Henderson (1945) treated the recurved rachis, which gives the leaf a boat-shaped profile, as the most reliable character, and recorded a young plant at Kirstenbosch bearing three female cones, about 27 cm tall after five weeks, with dark reddish-brown scales on a green ground.

More recent accounts describe larger plants. SANBI's non-detriment finding (2023, updated 2025), citing Osborne (1989), gives entirely aerial, slow-growing stems up to 2 m long and 30 to 45 cm in diameter, often reclining and branching at the base into clumps, and cones produced one to four at a time, occasionally six, about every three years on average. The PlantZAfrica account (K. W. Sepheka, National Herbarium, Pretoria, July 2014) adds:

  • Stems: erect, sometimes reclining with age, usually up to about 4 m long and 45 cm in diameter, with soft whitish cataphylls at the crown
  • Leaves: rigid, 0.7 to 1.5 m long, light bluish green, slightly curved downwards, the tip usually curved slightly upwards
  • Leaflets: median leaflets 15 to 20 cm long and 13 to 18 mm wide, leathery, without nodules; margins not thickened, entire except for an occasional single tooth on the lower margin; basal leaflets reduced to one or two spines at most
  • Cones: produced in December by both sexes, reddish brown because of a dense layer of hairs over a greenish-grey surface; one to eight male cones per stem and season, 20 to 45 cm long and 6 to 10 cm in diameter, often strongly scented while shedding pollen in February and March; one to six female cones, 30 to 50 cm long and 16 to 20 cm in diameter, which do not break up on their own but dry out from May to August
  • Seeds: light brown, 35 to 44 mm long and 23 to 30 mm in diameter

The sources therefore disagree on stem length (2 m or 4 m), and the protologue's figures (trunk about 1.3 m, leaves about 75 cm) are smaller than both; it says nothing about variation within the species.

Hybrids

No natural hybrid is documented. In 2011, on the French Fous de palmiers forum, a member who had worked in a South African nursery claimed that unscrupulous sellers pollinated female Encephalartos eugene-maraisii with pollen of Encephalartos natalensis when no pollen of the same species was available, and then sold the seed under the name Encephalartos eugene-maraisii. The claim is unverified, but it is a reason to be wary of seed without a controlled origin.

Possible confusion

Confusion comes mostly from the history of the species. According to Giddy (1989), quoted in a 2019 study, “forms” of Encephalartos eugene-maraisii were then known from Middelburg, the Waterberg, the Wolkberg and the Mica district. The Middelburg plants are now Encephalartos middelburgensis, first described as a subspecies by Lavranos and Goode (1988), and the Mica plants Encephalartos dyerianus; typical plants come from the Waterberg. PlantZAfrica lists these two species, Encephalartos dolomiticus and Encephalartos hirsutus as close relatives, and separates Encephalartos eugene-maraisii by its straight leaves with recurved ends, median leaflets without spines, a clear petiole and green cones covered in fine brown wool. In cultivation, a blue plant without documented provenance cannot be assigned to any of these species with certainty.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos eugene-maraisii is an accepted name. Inez Clare Verdoorn published it in 1945 in the Journal of South African Botany (11(1): 1–3, pl. 1) under the title “A new species of Encephalartos from the Waterberg”. The type, held at the National Herbarium in Pretoria, is a specimen from Rudolf Marloth's herbarium (no. 13368) collected by Eugène Marais on the Palala plateau in the Waterberg district; The World List of Cycads dates it to December 1926 and gives an altitude of 750 m. The protologue gives neither date nor altitude for the type, but cites other gatherings from the same region, including Kafferkraal on the central Waterberg plateau at about 5,000 ft (about 1,500 m) and the summit of Hangklip at about 5,900 ft (about 1,800 m).

In 1988 Lavranos and Goode treated the Middelburg plants as a subspecies of Encephalartos eugene-maraisii, which automatically created the name Encephalartos eugene-maraisii subsp. eugene-maraisii, now a synonym. The same year, the Mica population was described as Encephalartos dyerianus. Since Encephalartos middelburgensis was raised to species rank by Robbertse, Vorster and van der Westhuizen (1989), the name Encephalartos eugene-maraisii has been restricted to the Waterberg plants.

Etymology and history

The epithet honours Eugène Nielen Marais (1872–1936 according to Haynes, 2022; 1871–1936 according to a 2019 study), the South African naturalist, poet and writer who lived in the Waterberg and drew his niece Inez Verdoorn's attention to the plant. The name of the genus comes from the Greek en (within), kephale (head) and artos (bread), a reference to the starchy pith. Johann Lodewyk Marais (2019) tells the story of the discovery from a recorded interview with Verdoorn:

  • Eugène Marais lived in the Waterberg between 1907 and 1916 and knew the plant on the heights above the Palala
  • he had sent a leaf and seeds to the botanist Rudolf Marloth in Cape Town; Marloth's label read “Nelspruit” and gave the name of Encephalartos paucidentatus, a species already known
  • a few years before his death, Marais asked Verdoorn the name of “the Waterberg cycad”; it was then thought that no cycad grew in the west of the country; Verdoorn found the specimen in Marloth's herbarium, which the National Herbarium had acquired, and corrected the label in front of him
  • Marais died on 29 March 1936 before he could take her to the site; an agricultural officer, P. S. Toerien, later found the plant, and Verdoorn went there with R. A. Dyer, to the farm Vlakfontein, where two plants grew on a small hill; according to the protologue the visit took place in October 1944, and the two plants, a few hundred metres apart on the south-eastern slope of a stony ridge, each had three or four suckers that had arisen underground, the largest about 60 cm tall
  • other botanists, including Pole Evans and Galpin, had meanwhile sent Waterberg specimens to the Pretoria herbarium

The dates do not agree: The World List of Cycads dates the type to December 1926, the corrected label reads “December 1927”, and the 1945 publication places the dispatch to Marloth in 1925. According to that publication, the specimen consisted of leaf fragments and a few kernels, and it was the first record of a cycad in the western Transvaal. Verdoorn insisted on a compound name, “eugene-maraisii”, modelled on Encephalartos friderici-guilielmi. During her research she also found in a cone a small weevil new to science, described by Jordan in 1945 as Apinotropis verdoornae; its role as a pollinator has not been established.

Common names

The English name Waterberg cycad is used by SANBI and Species+. The 2019 study records several Afrikaans names: “Waterbergse broodboom” (Waterberg cycad, after Goode, 1989), “bergpalm” (mountain palm), “Eugène Marais-bergpalm” (in the newspaper Die Volkstem in 1945) and “wilde dadel” (wild date, after Steyn and co-authors, 1948).

In the wild

Encephalartos eugene-maraisii is confined to the Waterberg massif. According to SANBI (2023, updated 2025), it grows in Highveld grassland and Bushveld savanna, on sandstone hills and rocky ridges, among well-established vegetation; PlantZAfrica describes rocky hills and steep slopes in open grassland and savanna, among low shrubs, with hot summers, very cold winters and 600 to 750 mm of rain a year, falling mainly in summer. In 1945 Verdoorn described plants wedged firmly among rocks, scattered as individuals, which she saw as relics of a species now localised and almost extinct; all the localities then known, on the Palala plateau and in the Hangklip range, were close to one another.

The remaining plants form at least two subpopulations about 100 km apart, within an extent of occurrence of about 1,290 km². Between 2,000 and 3,000 plants were once scattered across several farms and reserves; most of these small subpopulations have gone. Reproduction appears to be failing: SANBI reports that no viable seed has been found where males and females grow together and that no seedlings were seen in sixteen years of observation, which suggests a pollination problem; in 2008, in the largest subpopulation, the plants whose sex could be determined (only 28 %) were mostly males. As in other cycads, seeds rarely travel more than 20 m. One subpopulation is said to suffer from a stem-boring beetle and a moth (“leopard moth” in the text, species not given), whose damage is worsened by hot fires; SANBI states that research is under way.

The 2017 Biodiversity Management Plan for critically endangered and endangered Encephalartos (Government Gazette No. 40793) gives a different picture for one site: it reports that the subpopulation in a protected area managed by South African National Parks (SANParks) contains reproductive males and females and shows natural recruitment. The two documents do not cover the same period, and the difference is not explained.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos eugene-maraisii is listed as Endangered (EN) on the IUCN Red List (assessment by J. D. Bösenberg, published in 2022) under criteria A2ad+4ad and B1ab(v): a past and projected decline caused by exploitation, and a restricted range with a continuing decline in mature plants. The Red List page itself could not be read; the following figures come from SANBI's non-detriment finding (approved on 8 February 2023, updated in June 2025):

  • fewer than 700 adult plants remain in the wild, and a decline of more than 50 % over thirty years is suspected
  • in the densest subpopulation, helicopter surveys in 2004 and 2020 fell from 456 to 289 adult plants, a loss of more than 35 %
  • more than 2,000 stems were counted in 2008; at 22 % of the sites rechecked after reports of poaching, 47 % of the plants had gone and a further 20 % had been uprooted
  • a wave of thefts in 2010–2012 removed more than half of the known plants from the richest reserve; arrested poachers reportedly admitted taking almost 1,000 stems
  • between 2009 and 2011, 12 cases were opened and 28 suspects arrested; in 2021 two poachers were sentenced to prison

The 2017 management plan, which covers this species among eleven critically endangered and four endangered Encephalartos, gives 900 to 1,000 plants remaining in the wild after a decline of more than 50 % in thirty years, most of them on private nature reserves and in protected areas managed by SANParks, where plants are marked, measured and mapped. It notes that the species had no viable ex situ collection in the national botanical gardens and set three objectives: an ex situ genebank of at least 100 seedlings at the Lowveld National Botanical Garden, an in situ genebank on a private nature reserve made of plants recovered from poaching, and the replanting of 500 seeds or seedlings to replace the plants recently stolen from that reserve. SANBI adds that a trial of marking wild plants with microdots was funded by the Botanical Society of South Africa. Collecting wild cycads has been banned throughout South Africa since 2007 (Government Notice No. 371 of 2012 for the current text).

Legal trade concerns cultivated plants: 3,274 specimens were exported from South Africa between 1979 and 2020, and seven nurseries are registered with CITES for the species. Like the whole genus, it has been listed in CITES Appendix I since 4 February 1977 and, in the European Union, in Annex A of the wildlife trade regulations (current list: Regulation (EU) 2026/1383). According to Species+, consulted in October 2026, the EU Scientific Review Group has held a negative opinion since 7 February 2013 on imports of artificially propagated plants of this species from South Africa, and a review covering the whole genus was under consultation in September 2026. Check the current rules and ask for proof of cultivated origin before any purchase.

Growing Encephalartos eugene-maraisii

Published cultivation data are few. PlantZAfrica describes a relatively slow grower that does best in full sun, is very frost-resistant and is easily propagated from seed and suckers; it adds that cycads in general need little water and are undemanding as to soil. Growers' reports, mostly from France and Italy and all concerning young plants, stress very slow growth and the fragility of small plants after watering mistakes or transplanting.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

In the wild the species gets its rain in summer and spends a cold, dry winter. A member of the Fous de palmiers forum who had worked in a South African nursery reports that, in a planting of Encephalartos eugene-maraisii of the same age, plants near the automatic sprinkler were twice the size of those 3 m away. This single account suggests that the species benefits from summer watering, without proving it. In Concarneau, on the Atlantic coast of Brittany, a young plant set in the ground in spring 2011 produced two new leaves in 2012, after the winter described under Cold hardiness. In Raleigh, North Carolina, the Juniper Level Botanic Garden lost the species in the ground (see below). Full sun, as PlantZAfrica advises, and a winter kept on the dry side, as in the habitat, are the conditions suggested by the sources; none documents long-term outdoor cultivation of adult plants outside Africa.

Container cultivation

The container reports concern small plants. A grower on the Côte Bleue, west of Marseille, wrote in 2011 that it was “by far the slowest” of his Encephalartos: he had kept it for three years in its small original pot, in pure gravel, watered twice a week from a saucer, before repotting it, after which it produced two leaves. A grower in the Tarn, inland in south-western France, lost a small potted plant with a 3 cm caudex in 2012; it dried out after a move, despite a fungicide treatment, and he has since advised buying only plants with a caudex of at least 3 cm. On the Italian Tropicamente forum, a grower in Ravenna who kept his cycads in pots, sheltered from winter rain, listed a form labelled “Kransberg” in his collection in September 2012 and noted in June 2013 that it had not yet produced new leaves that year; another grower described a seedling still dormant at the end of June 2013. PlantZAfrica's plant attributes describe it as drought-resistant and a good pot plant; given its eventual size, long-term container growing suits young plants rather than adults.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

Montgomery Botanical Center, in Coral Gables, Florida, lists one accession (64532) in its online catalogue. The Huntington, Lotusland, the San Diego Botanic Garden, the San Diego Zoo and its Safari Park, Descanso Gardens and the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley in California, the Atlanta Botanical Garden, and the National Tropical Botanical Garden, Honolulu Botanical Gardens and the University of Hawaii at Hilo's garden in Hawaii report it to BGCI PlantSearch, which shows that a garden has declared the species at some point, not that the plant is alive today. In Australia, the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney and the Botanic Garden of South Australia in Adelaide declare it. No British garden lists it in BGCI PlantSearch. In Europe, the Naples Botanic Garden held eight plants in 1993–1994, according to an enzyme study published in its journal Delpinoa in 1997; their present state is not known.

Propagation

The species is propagated from seed and from suckers: it often branches at the base, and suckers can help a plant to persist when the main stem dies (SANBI). The sources disagree on seed treatment. SANBI states that, as in other cycads, the seeds have no dormancy and germinate a few months after dispersal, whereas PlantZAfrica recommends storing cleaned seed in a brown paper bag at 10 to 15 °C (50 to 59 °F) for at least six months so that the embryo can finish developing. PlantZAfrica's method continues as follows:

  • remove all the flesh, which may contain germination inhibitors and encourages fungi, wearing gloves because it contains slow-acting poisons; soak hard, dry seed for a day or two first
  • soak the cleaned seed for a few days, changing the water daily; viable seeds sink, non-viable ones float
  • lay the seeds on their sides, half buried in washed sand or potting mix, at about 28 °C (82.4 °F), kept moist but not wet
  • pot the sprouted seeds singly when the radicle is 10 to 20 mm long, or after one or two leaves, in tall narrow bags about 240 × 120 mm to accommodate the long taproot; keep the seedlings shaded for the first few years, watering daily with a fine spray at first, then weekly after about a month; plant out at three to five years
  • for suckers, dig around the base of the mother plant, cut the sucker off with a clean, sharp knife or spade, treat the wound with fungicide and let it dry for about a week before planting it in a sterile medium

Buy seed of controlled origin only, given the hybridisation risk mentioned above.

Pests and diseases

In the wild, one subpopulation is said to be attacked by a stem-boring beetle and an unidentified moth (SANBI). For cycads in general, PlantZAfrica names scale insects, which suck the sap of the leaves and can be controlled with horticultural oil, and beetles and other chewing insects that attack the emerging leaves. No disease specific to the species is documented in cultivation; the Tarn grower suspected a fungal disease in his declining plant, without confirmation.

Cold hardiness

No measured cold limit has been published for Encephalartos eugene-maraisii. PlantZAfrica calls it very frost-resistant, without figures; the only quantified reports are a young plant that survived −5 °C (23 °F) in Brittany and plants that died outdoors in North Carolina at between −15.6 and −12.8 °C (4 and 9 °F). In Encephalartos, survival in cold areas often concerns young plants whose caudex is still underground, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil and sometimes by snow; adults with an aerial trunk, as in this species, have no such protection.

Mediterranean and European experience

  • A grower in Concarneau (Finistère, Brittany, France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his young Encephalartos eugene-maraisii, in the ground since spring 2011, kept a “very sound” caudex through the winter of 2011–2012, with a low of −5 °C (23 °F); the leaves, produced before planting, had been shredded by a storm in December, and any protection is not stated. The plant produced two new leaves in 2012.
  • No dated in-ground report exists for the Mediterranean coast, inland France or the mountains, and the Italian Tropicamente forum mentions the species only in lists of “hardy” species, without a dated trial.

For readers in comparable climates (California, southern Australia, the mildest parts of southern England), the Concarneau report shows only that a young plant can come through a short frost of −5 °C; it says nothing about adults.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

In Raleigh, North Carolina, the Juniper Level Botanic Garden listed Encephalartos eugene-maraisii among the cycads that did not survive outdoors, unprotected and unmulched, in the winter of 2013–2014, when its lows were between 4 and 9 °F (−15.6 to −12.8 °C) depending on the site (article of 7 August 2014); the age and size of the plants are not given. Giddy (1989), quoted in the 2019 study, wrote that all the forms then included in the species, including the Waterberg one, experience very cold winters. USDA zones quoted by commercial websites (8b or 9a) are not based on any cited measurement, and no British, Australian or Californian garden publishes cold data for the species. In Britain, botanic gardens grow almost all their cycads under glass.

Toxicity and precautions

Yes, the seeds are poisonous, and the history of the species itself shows it. The 1945 protologue records two separate facts: Marais's note stated that the kernels, roasted and eaten, caused dizziness, and, according to other accounts, children on the farm Kafferkraal had been dangerously ill after eating these “dates”; Verdoorn added that the scanty seed flesh was presumably poisonous. According to the 2019 study, the label of Marais's specimen mentioned that the seeds, roasted and eaten by children, had caused dizziness; an article in Die Volkstem (1945) reports that children fell seriously ill in 1926 after eating these “wild dates”, and Steyn, Van der Walt and Verdoorn (1948) cite this account in their report on the toxicity of Encephalartos seeds. Like other cycads, Encephalartos contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; the ASPCA reports vomiting, diarrhoea, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses that have eaten cycads.

Wear gloves to clean seeds, pick up any that fall, and keep plants and seeds away from children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use specific to the species is documented. SANBI points out that all South African cycads are exposed to collection for traditional medicine, since harvesters do not distinguish between species. PlantZAfrica recalls that, in the past, people made bread from the pith of cycads, which gave the genus its name.

Frequently asked questions

Who was Eugène Marais?

A South African writer and naturalist who died in 1936. He lived in the Waterberg and told his niece, the botanist Inez Verdoorn, about the plant; she described it in 1945 and named it after him. He is also known for his observations of termites and baboons.

Is Encephalartos eugene-maraisii frost-hardy?

To some extent, but its limit is unknown. PlantZAfrica calls it very frost-resistant, a young plant in the ground in Brittany kept a sound caudex after −5 °C (23 °F), and plants died outdoors in North Carolina at −15.6 to −12.8 °C (4 to 9 °F). Between these values, nothing has been measured.

Does it grow fast?

No. PlantZAfrica describes it as relatively slow, and growers call it one of the slowest Encephalartos; small plants are fragile. It is better to buy a plant whose caudex is at least a few centimetres across.

How does it differ from Encephalartos middelburgensis and Encephalartos dyerianus?

Above all by origin. The three species have bluish leaves and were once combined, but Encephalartos eugene-maraisii is restricted to the Waterberg, Encephalartos middelburgensis to the Middelburg area and Encephalartos dyerianus to a hill near Mica. PlantZAfrica also points to its straight leaves with recurved tips and its green cones covered in brown wool.

Why is it threatened?

Because of poaching. Its numbers have halved in thirty years, and a wave of thefts in 2010–2012 removed more than half the plants from the richest reserve. Fewer than 700 adults remain in the wild according to SANBI.

Can I buy one legally?

Yes, but only with documents. The species is in CITES Appendix I and EU Annex A, and the EU has a negative opinion on imports of artificially propagated plants from South Africa. Ask for the permits and proof of cultivated origin, and check the rules before buying.

Is it poisonous?

Yes. Children on the farm Kafferkraal, in the Waterberg, became seriously ill after eating its seeds, which Marais also said caused dizziness once roasted, and Encephalartos seeds contain toxins dangerous to people and animals.
