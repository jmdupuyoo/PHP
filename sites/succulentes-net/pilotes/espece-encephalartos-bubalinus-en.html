Encephalartos bubalinus is an East African cycad that grows on both sides of the border between Tanzania and Kenya, on the quartzite hills west of Lake Natron, between Loliondo and Ol Doinyo Sambu. This species of the genus Encephalartos forms a short trunk, upright or lying on the ground, and dark green leaves 0.6 to 1.65 m long. Its name, meaning “buff-coloured”, refers to the pale brown felt, like suede leather, that covers the scale leaves of the trunk and the swollen bases of the leaf stalks. Found in 1944 and described in 1957 by the Kew botanist Ronald Melville, it is listed as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List because of its very small range.

Key facts about Encephalartos bubalinus

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos bubalinus Melville (1957), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15)
  • Former names and synonyms: no synonym
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Lake Natron cycad (English, Species+)
  • Natural range: Tanzania (Arusha region, between Loliondo and Lake Natron) and Kenya (Narok county)
  • IUCN status: Vulnerable (VU), assessment dated 11 March 2020 and published in the IUCN Red List 2024-1; the previous assessment (October 2009, published in 2010) had already listed it as Vulnerable under criterion D2
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I (whole genus); Annex A in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: no measured value; a single Italian grower's report that the species, among others, loses all its leaves after a frost but usually recovers (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, open bushland and grassland on quartzite hills at about 1,200 to 1,500 m (Melville, 1957); sun or part shade and free-draining soil recommended in cultivation
  • Habit and adult size: trunk upright or lying down, up to 137 cm tall and 33 cm across (original description); leaves 60 to 165 cm long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Montgomery Botanical Center (five accessions) and Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden in Florida, several Californian gardens, Royal Botanic Garden Sydney; Kew declares it to BGCI
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; treat it as poisonous like all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos bubalinus

The surest sign is the dense, buff-brown felt on the scale leaves of the trunk and on the swollen base of each leaf stalk, together with fairly short leaves carrying many stiff leaflets. The original description by Melville (1957) gives these characters:

  • Trunk: upright or lying down, up to 137 cm tall and 33 cm in diameter (the height is printed both in the Latin diagnosis and in the English description)
  • Scale leaves (cataphylls): on female plants triangular to ovate and long-pointed, 10 to 14 cm long and 4 to 4.5 cm wide, keeled and 10 to 15 mm thick, the outer face densely felted in buff; on male plants linear to lance-shaped, 6 to 12 cm long
  • Leaves: oblanceolate, 60 to 165 cm long and 20 to 30 cm wide, with 50 to 90 pairs of leaflets, rounded at the tip and tapering gradually to the base; rachis sparsely covered in whitish hairs; swollen base of the petiole densely felted in buff-brown
  • Leaflets: the middle ones linear, stiff and leathery, 10 to 20 cm long and 11 to 20 mm wide, with a pungent tip; two to four forward-pointing spiny teeth on the upper margin near the base, none or one on the lower margin; 24 to 35 parallel veins beneath; the lower leaflets shrink to small spines
  • Male cones: ellipsoid to almost cylindrical, 11 to 22 cm long and 5.5 to 6 cm in diameter, rounded at both ends, on a smooth stalk 1.5 to 2 cm thick
  • Female cones: not seen by Melville, who had only loose scales, their exposed face about 6 cm wide
  • Seeds: ellipsoid to broadly ovoid, 34 to 42 mm long and 21 to 26 mm wide, or almost round (30 by 30 mm)

The larger cone sizes quoted online, up to 55 cm for male cones, have not been found in any primary source. The San Diego nursery Jungle Music describes blue-green female cones, green male cones with a bluish tinge, and plants that often produce many basal suckers.

Hybrids

No natural or garden hybrid of Encephalartos bubalinus has been reported.

Possible confusion

In Melville's keys (1957), Encephalartos bubalinus stands apart from the other East African Encephalartos by its thick, keeled, triangular to ovate trunk scales, covered like the petiole bases in a close buff-brown felt. The nearest species in his keys are:

  • Encephalartos hildebrandtii, of the coast of Kenya and Tanzania: leaves 2 to 3 m long, green, petiole bases hairless when mature, trunk scales linear
  • Encephalartos tegulaneus, of the Kenyan mountains: trunk scales lance-shaped to linear, covered in a soft buff woolly tomentum; male cone scales turned down and overlapping like roof tiles
  • Encephalartos manikensis: trunk scales covered in a dense, pale fawn woolly tomentum

Further east, Encephalartos kisambo, of Kenya and northern Tanzania, is often cited as a related species.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos bubalinus is an accepted name with no synonyms. Ronald Melville described it in his paper “Encephalartos in Central Africa”, published in the Kew Bulletin in 1957 (12(2): 252, figure 4), a revision prepared for the Flora of Tropical East Africa in which he also described Encephalartos tegulaneus. The type, Bally 10600, was collected at Moluvane, near Ol Doinyo Sambu, between Loliondo and Lake Natron (2° 11′ S, 35° 40′ E), in what was then Tanganyika; the holotype is at Kew (K) and an isotype in Berlin (B).

The World List of Cycads, the checklist of the IUCN Cycad Specialist Group, accepts the species in its version of 15 August 2026 and records it from Kenya (Narok) and Tanzania (Arusha). No name change concerns it.

Etymology and history

The name comes from the Latin bubalinus, “buff”, a reference to the buff-brown tomentum on the scale leaves and leaf bases (Haynes, 2022, cited by The World List of Cycads). Melville explained that this close felt “has the appearance of buff suede leather”.

Melville tells the story of its discovery. The botanist Peter R. O. Bally found it on 17 September 1944 between Loliondo and the northern end of Lake Natron, about 7 miles (11 km) from the Kenyan border, near a place called Gwara on the maps, which the Sonjo, the local people, call Moluvane. His collection (Bally 3829) included a male cone and leaves. When he returned on 5 July 1956 to look for female cones, he found only very old male cones: the plants had not coned that year, and all that remained of the female cones were a few loose scales and broken seed shells left by baboons. He took two adult plants and four seedlings back to Nairobi to try to grow them.

Common names

Species+ gives the English name Lake Natron cycad, after the nearby lake. No name in Maa, Sonjo or Swahili specific to the species is documented; Melville cites only the local name of the collecting site, Moluvane.

In the wild

Encephalartos bubalinus grows in the Nguruman Hills, a series of quartzite hills rising to 4,000 to 5,000 ft (about 1,200 to 1,500 m) west of Lake Natron. According to local people questioned by Bally in 1956, the species extends over about 15 miles (24 km), between Loliondo and Loiyogaz in the west and Ol Doinyo Sambu in the east. It grows in open bushland and grassland, with Acacia, Ziziphus, Dombeya, Dolichos and Aloe volkensii.

For the Encephalartos of Central and East Africa as a whole, Melville describes two main causes of death. Fire easily kills seedlings, whereas adults survive almost all fires. Baboons tear cones apart, often before they ripen, damage the plants and pull up young seedlings to eat what is left of the seed; in some areas they were thought to be more destructive than fire. This is exactly what Bally saw in this species in 1956.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos bubalinus is listed as Vulnerable (VU) on the IUCN Red List. The assessment in force, by the Eastern Arc Mountains and Coastal Forests CEPF Plant Assessment Project and J. D. Bösenberg, is dated 11 March 2020 and was published in the Red List 2024-1, with no change of category (GBIF and Crossref, consulted on 26 October 2026; these sources do not give its criteria). The previous assessment, by J. D. Bösenberg, dated October 2009 and published in 2010, is the one still cited by The World List of Cycads: it listed the species as Vulnerable under criterion D2, a very small area of occupancy or number of locations, which exposes the species to rapid decline from any new threat. Some websites give it as Near Threatened (NT), which is not the category of either assessment.

No population figures are published. Jungle Music, which cites the 2010 assessment, writes that the populations seem stable but are poorly known because the terrain is difficult to reach, and that local people eat the seeds in times of famine; these statements could not be checked against the IUCN text.

Like the whole genus, Encephalartos bubalinus has been listed in CITES Appendix I since 4 February 1977, without annotation, so plants, seeds and pollen are all covered. Commercial international trade in wild-collected plants is prohibited; only artificially propagated plants can be exported, from registered nurseries and with the required permits. In the European Union the species is in Annex A of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383. For artificially propagated plants imported from South Africa, the EU's Scientific Review Group issued a positive opinion in 2011 for plants with a caudex up to 7 cm in diameter, and has held a negative opinion for larger plants since 7 February 2013. These details come from Species+, the official CITES and EU database, consulted in October 2026; check the current rules before any purchase or import, and ask for proof that the plant was propagated in cultivation.

Growing Encephalartos bubalinus

Encephalartos bubalinus is grown by collectors, mainly in warm climates, but published growing advice is limited to nursery opinion. It is a plant for sun or part shade in free-draining soil.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

Jungle Music, in San Diego, describes it as a fairly quick-growing species that many growers in southern California have planted. The nursery recommends sun or part sun on the coast and inland, and filtered light or shade in desert areas, in well-drained soil; it considers the species tolerant of water. The long leaves can sprawl, so allow room. Outdoor planting is realistic only in mild, nearly frost-free climates such as coastal California or southern Florida (see Cold hardiness).

Container cultivation

In cooler climates the species can only be grown in a pot moved under glass for the winter. Its leaves, up to 1.65 m long, make it a large container plant; choose a deep pot and a gritty, free-draining compost. On the French Fous de palmiers forum, the species appears in a few collections in France and the Czech Republic as young plants grown under cover, without detailed growing notes.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

The species is well represented in American collections. Montgomery Botanical Center, in Coral Gables, Florida, lists five accessions in its online catalogue (9019, 64570, 78400, 85379 and 95914), and Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden lists it in its Garden Explorer. The Huntington, Lotusland, San Diego Botanic Garden and San Diego Zoo in California, Naples Botanical Garden in Florida, the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley and three other American gardens report it to BGCI PlantSearch, which shows that a garden has declared the species at some point, not that the plant is alive today. In Australia, the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney lists it in its own catalogue. In Britain, only Kew declares it to BGCI.

Propagation

Encephalartos bubalinus is propagated from seed and by dividing the suckers that, according to Jungle Music, the species often produces. The seeds are large, 34 to 42 mm long, and must be fresh and fertilised; in the wild baboons destroy a large share of them. In cultivation, without the insects that pollinate cycad cones, hand-pollination is usually needed to obtain fertile seed. Buy only plants and seeds propagated in cultivation and sold with their CITES documents.

Pests and diseases

No disease or pest specific to Encephalartos bubalinus is documented in cultivation. Melville notes that fungi or insects can attack the scales of female Encephalartos cones and deform them. A study published in 2025 isolated an endophytic fungus, Amesia nigricolor, from the species, with no reported effect on the plant.

Cold hardiness

The hardiness of Encephalartos bubalinus is not documented: no lowest temperature has been measured, either in the wild or in cultivation.

Mediterranean and European experience

The only European indication comes from southern Italy. A grower in Salento (Apulia, Italy) reports on the Tropicamente forum in December 2008 that, in his experience with “fairly large and thriving” plants, the group made up of Encephalartos arenarius, Encephalartos bubalinus, Encephalartos sclavoi, Encephalartos hildebrandtii and Encephalartos kisambo loses all its leaves after a frost but has a good chance of recovering the following growing season. He gives no temperature, no date for the episodes and does not say whether the plants were in pots or in the ground. The plants reported on the French Fous de palmiers forum are young and grown under cover.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

The natural range is tropical upland, at about 1,200 to 1,500 m according to Melville. No dated, measured report from cultivation has been found in English-language sources. Survival in cold conditions among Encephalartos often concerns young plants whose caudex is still underground, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil; an adult with a trunk above ground has no such protection. No USDA zone is published for the species by a botanical source. In Britain it is a glasshouse plant; it is grown outdoors in southern Florida and coastal California, but none of these gardens publishes cold data for it.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data are published for Encephalartos bubalinus itself, but the risk is documented in the genus: the toxicity of Encephalartos horridus was demonstrated as early as 1944 by Van der Walt, and Encephalartos, like other cycads, contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; the seeds are the most dangerous part. The ASPCA reports vomiting, diarrhoea, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses that have eaten cycads. Jungle Music reports, citing the IUCN, that local people eat the seeds in times of famine; this has not been verified and does not mean the seeds are safe: in other cycads, only long traditional processing removes part of the toxins.

Treat the plant as poisonous: wear gloves to clean seeds, pick up any that fall, and keep plants and seeds away from children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

The only reported use of Encephalartos bubalinus is as food, and it is unverified: according to Jungle Music, which cites the 2010 IUCN assessment, the seeds are eaten in times of famine. No ethnobotanical source confirms this or describes how the seeds are prepared.

Frequently asked questions

What does the name bubalinus mean?

“Buff-coloured”. The Latin epithet refers to the close pale brown felt on the trunk scales and leaf bases, which Melville compared to buff suede leather.

Where does Encephalartos bubalinus grow?

On the quartzite Nguruman Hills west of Lake Natron, on the border between Tanzania and Kenya. Its range, about 15 miles (24 km) long according to information gathered in 1956, is one of the smallest of any East African Encephalartos.

Is it frost-hardy?

Nobody knows for sure. No lowest temperature has been published; a single Italian grower reports that it loses its leaves after a frost but usually recovers. Grow it in a pot and keep it frost-free in winter unless you garden in a nearly frost-free climate.

Is it endangered?

It is threatened. The IUCN has listed it as Vulnerable since 2010, a category kept in the assessment published in 2024, because of its very small range. It is in CITES Appendix I and in Annex A in the European Union; only plants propagated in cultivation can be bought legally.

How do I tell it from Encephalartos hildebrandtii?

By leaf length and felt. Encephalartos hildebrandtii has leaves 2 to 3 m long and petiole bases that become hairless; Encephalartos bubalinus has leaves 60 to 165 cm long and petiole bases covered in dense buff-brown felt.

Does it grow fast?

Fairly fast for an Encephalartos, according to a Californian nursery that has grown it for a long time. No published measurement says how long it takes to reach coning size.
