Encephalartos cerinus is a small South African cycad confined to a rocky gorge in the Tugela Ferry district of central KwaZulu-Natal, where it grows on hot, dry cliffs. This species of the genus Encephalartos is recognised by the thick bluish wax that coats its leaves and cones and gives it its name, the Waxen cycad. Described in 1989, it was plundered almost at once: of some 200 adult plants found in 1987, most were dug up within months, and in 2015 South Africa's scientific authority estimated that perhaps only four or five plants remained in the wild. The IUCN lists it as Critically Endangered.

Key facts about Encephalartos cerinus

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos cerinus Lavranos & D.L.Goode (1989), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15)
  • Former names and synonyms: no synonym
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Waxen cycad (English, Species+ and SANBI)
  • Natural range: South Africa, KwaZulu-Natal, a single gorge in the Tugela Ferry district; about 10 km² according to PlantZAfrica
  • IUCN status: Critically Endangered (CR), A2acd; B1ab(i,ii,v)+2ab(i,ii,v); C2a(i,ii); D, assessment published in 2022; some reports suggest it may already be extinct in the wild (PlantZAfrica)
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I (whole genus); Annex A in the European Union; “specially protected” in KwaZulu-Natal; collecting wild cycads is prohibited throughout South Africa
  • Documented hardiness: “tolerates light frost” according to PlantZAfrica; no measured value from cultivation (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, sunny east-facing cliffs and rocks with very thin soil; in cultivation, light shade according to PlantZAfrica, sun to part shade according to nurseries, always in free-draining soil
  • Habit and adult size: trunk underground or partly exposed, up to 30 cm long and 20 to 25 cm thick; 8 to 15 (20) leaves 90 to 120 (150) cm long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Montgomery Botanical Center and Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden in Florida, several Californian gardens, Royal Botanic Garden Sydney, an outdoor bed at the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria, Melbourne; Kew declares it to BGCI
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; treat it as poisonous like all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos cerinus

The clearest character is the wax: leaves and cones are covered in a thick, blue-grey waxy coating, which the authors of the description presented as the feature that separates the species from all its relatives. PlantZAfrica adds that rubbing a leaflet between the fingers leaves a distinct waxy smell. The original description by Lavranos and Goode (1989) gives these characters:

  • Habit: a plant branching sparingly from the base; trunk underground, or partly exposed where the plant grows on rock, up to 30 cm long and 20 to 25 cm in diameter
  • Leaves: 8 to 15 (rarely 20), 90 to 120 cm long (up to 150 cm), the leaflets pointing towards the leaf tip and slightly overlapping
  • Leaflets: dark blue-green, covered in a thick layer of wax; middle leaflets 15 to 18 cm long and 12 mm wide, tapering at both ends, with one or two teeth on the lower margin
  • Petiole: bare and without prickles, 12 to 18 cm long
  • Cones: solitary in both sexes, blue-green turning orange-yellow at maturity (except in full shade), always covered in a thick waxy bloom
  • Male cones: subcylindrical, 55 to 60 cm long and 8 to 10 cm across, on an 8 cm stalk; middle scales with a flattened end face
  • Female cones: ovoid-oblong, tapering at the top, 30 to 35 cm long and 15 to 18 cm across; scale faces smooth, the front ridge armed with lobed teeth 3 to 5 mm long pointing downwards
  • Seeds: deep pink to red

PlantZAfrica, written from plants in South African gardens, gives slightly different figures: female cones 30 to 38 cm long, seeds yellow at first and ripening bright red, and male plants in cultivation occasionally bearing two cones at once. It also states that the leaflets of mature plants lack the small teeth found on those of Encephalartos umbeluziensis, although juvenile plants have them; the original description, for its part, mentions one or two teeth on the lower margin without distinguishing ages. In both accounts the basal leaflets are not reduced to spines, and the rachis becomes very thin towards the leaf tip.

Hybrids

No natural or garden hybrid of Encephalartos cerinus has been described. As with other rare Encephalartos, the purity of seed offered for sale depends on controlled pollination in collections, which the buyer cannot check.

Possible confusion

The risk of confusion concerns three species with short trunks and few-toothed leaflets, which Lavranos and Goode regarded as its closest relatives and which PlantZAfrica also names:

  • Encephalartos umbeluziensis, its closest relative according to the authors, also grows in hot, dry places but in light shade, not on steep open cliffs; its leaves are clearly longer and not waxy, its leaflet margins carry small teeth, and its cones are neither solitary nor orange-yellow
  • Encephalartos villosus, widespread in moister grassland, has much longer leaves, an armed petiole and larger, clustered cones
  • Encephalartos ngoyanus, also from moister habitats, has dark green leaves without wax and male cones half as long (20 to 25 cm against 55 to 60 cm)

None of these three species carries the thick wax of Encephalartos cerinus on its leaves and cones, and none leaves the waxy smell on the fingers that PlantZAfrica describes.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos cerinus is an accepted name with no synonyms. J. J. Lavranos and Douglas Goode published it on 30 June 1989 in the Durban Museum Novitates (14: 153–156), in a paper entitled “Notes on Southern African Cycadales II”. The type is a plant collected in a gorge of the Tugela Ferry district at about 900 m (D. Goode 4, holotype at the KwaZulu-Natal Herbarium in Durban, NH). The World List of Cycads gives a collection date of 23 February 1954 for this specimen, which is hard to reconcile with the authors' account, who date the discovery of the species to 1970; the date could not be checked.

The authors placed the species near Encephalartos umbeluziensis, Encephalartos villosus and Encephalartos ngoyanus, three species of eastern southern Africa. No name change or revision has affected the species since its description. The World List of Cycads, the checklist of the IUCN Cycad Specialist Group, accepts it in its version of 15 August 2026.

Etymology and history

The epithet cerinus is Latin for “waxy” or “of wax”. The authors chose it for the thick waxy coating of the leaves and cones, and Haynes (2022) links it to the layer of wax that gives the leaves their bluish colour.

The history of the species is one of plunder. According to the original description, a farmer of the Tugela Ferry district noticed in 1970, at a neighbour's, a transplanted plant unlike the local cycads. He was taken to a nearby gorge where he found twelve more plants, on an almost vertical sandstone cliff high above the river. Illegal collecting soon wiped out this colony. A second, much larger colony was discovered in 1987, five kilometres from the first: about 200 coning adults and dozens of seedlings and young plants, in a rocky gorge, mostly in cracks and between boulders, sometimes near the water. By the time of publication, in 1989, almost all the young plants and about three-quarters of the adults had already been removed.

SANBI's non-detriment finding (NDF), approved in 2012 and updated in 2015, adds that most of the population, “several hundred plants”, was removed within six months of the description. International trade in cultivated plants began only in 1996.

Common names

The English name Waxen cycad is used by Species+, by SANBI and by the cycad encyclopaedia of the Palm & Cycad Societies of Florida. The name “wax cycad” also circulates, without a reference source. No Zulu name specific to the species is documented; PlantZAfrica notes that in Zulu culture most cycads are called isigqiki-somkhovu.

In the wild

Encephalartos cerinus lives only in central KwaZulu-Natal, in the Tugela Ferry district. According to SANBI, the plants grow mainly on vertical east-facing cliffs in valleys covered with dry savanna; PlantZAfrica describes them in inhospitable places, often on east-facing cliffs in dry, hot, rocky grassland, in a single rocky gorge covering about 10 km². The original description stresses this hot, dry habitat, very different from the rather moist grassland where Encephalartos villosus and Encephalartos ngoyanus live. Secondary sources (LLIFLE, Jungle Music) place the species in the Buffels River valley, LLIFLE adding an altitude of 500 to 900 m on very thin soils; these details do not come from a primary source.

The Tugela Ferry area holds other Encephalartos, such as Encephalartos msinganus and Encephalartos natalensis. How these species share the habitats of the valley has not been described.

The present population is very poorly known. SANBI wrote in 2015 that the species may be extinct in the wild, with some reports of four or five plants. PlantZAfrica (2019) cites estimates of 10 to 70 plants, regards the population at the type locality as extinct, which leaves a single subpopulation, and notes that some reports suggest the species may already be extinct in the wild. PlantZAfrica dates male pollen shedding to late summer (January to March) and the break-up of female cones to winter and spring (June to September). The pollinators and seed dispersers of this species have not been studied.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos cerinus is listed as Critically Endangered (CR) on the IUCN Red List (assessment by J. D. Bösenberg, published in 2022), under criteria A2acd; B1ab(i,ii,v)+2ab(i,ii,v); C2a(i,ii); D. These reflect a steep past decline, a tiny range, a very small number of adults and a population that may number fewer than 50 mature individuals. The category and criteria are those recorded by The World List of Cycads. PlantZAfrica adds that some experts believe very few wild plants remain and that the species may already be extinct in the wild.

SANBI's NDF judges trade in Encephalartos cerinus detrimental: 853 plants were exported in 1997, and 1,800 in total between 1996 and 2011, with no benefit to the species, while the origin of the parent plants could not always be proven. Exports are now accepted only for seedlings produced by CITES-registered nurseries. In South Africa, collecting wild cycads has been prohibited since February 2007 (now under Government Notice No. 371 of May 2012), and the species is “specially protected” under the KwaZulu-Natal Nature Conservation Ordinance (No. 15 of 1974).

Like the whole genus, Encephalartos cerinus has been listed in CITES Appendix I since 4 February 1977, so plants, seeds and pollen are all covered; commercial trade in wild-collected plants is prohibited. In the European Union the species is in Annex A of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, according to Species+, the official CITES and EU database, consulted in October 2026. Any plant offered for sale must come from cultivation and carry the CITES documents required; check the current rules before any purchase or import.

Growing Encephalartos cerinus

Published growing data are scarce and come mostly from horticultural sources. SANBI notes that Encephalartos cerinus grows well and cones quickly compared with other Encephalartos, and PlantZAfrica describes it as a fast grower provided it has deep, well-drained soil and frequent watering. PlantZAfrica adds that this small plant fits easily into tight garden spaces, and Jungle Music notes that its leaves are relatively unarmed.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

PlantZAfrica says the species grows best in light shade. The San Diego nursery Jungle Music recommends full or part sun on the coast, part sun or filtered light inland and shade in desert areas, in a soil that drains well, although it considers the species tolerant of water; it describes regular yearly flushes of leaves, but also notes that it takes over 20 years to raise a large plant. LLIFLE presents it as an adaptable plant for warm temperate and subtropical climates, able to take full sun without scorching but less tolerant of humidity than most South African cycads, and recommends gravelly, well-drained soil, generous watering in the growing season and feeding. The wild habitat, dry cliffs with very thin soil, points the same way: a mineral, free-draining compost and no standing water.

Container cultivation

In cooler climates the species is grown in pots, which allows it to be kept dry and frost-free in winter. A grower in Ravenna (Emilia-Romagna, Italy) reports on the Tropicamente forum that his plant, bought as a seedling in 2010 and grown in a pot of very free-draining compost (sand, gravel, lapilli and a little organic matter), grew vigorously until 2013, each new leaf much larger than the last; in summer he waters his potted cycads daily by drip irrigation and gives them a slow-release fertiliser. He does not say how the plant spends the winter.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

Montgomery Botanical Center, in Coral Gables, Florida, lists the species in its online catalogue (accession 97811), and Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden lists it in its Garden Explorer. The Huntington, Lotusland, San Diego Botanic Garden and San Diego Zoo in California, Naples Botanical Garden in Florida and five other American gardens report it to BGCI PlantSearch, which shows that a garden has declared the species at some point, not that the plant is alive today. In Australia, the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney lists it in its own catalogue, the census of the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria places it in an outdoor bed at Melbourne Gardens, and James Cook University in Townsville declares it to BGCI. In Britain, only Kew declares it to BGCI. PlantZAfrica calls the species “hardly used in cultivation”, but the 1,800 plants exported legally from South Africa between 1996 and 2011 suggest a wider presence in private collections.

Propagation

Encephalartos cerinus is propagated almost entirely from seed, as it rarely produces suckers. PlantZAfrica notes that it cones frequently and responds well to hand-pollination. It advises sowing fresh seed in river sand on a heated bench at 24 to 28 °C (75 to 82 °F): germination starts about three weeks after sowing, but some seeds take longer, especially without bottom heat. At the one-leaf stage, seedlings are prone to damping-off. Seed must come from cultivated, hand-pollinated plants: no wild population can supply it legally, and SANBI records only one authorisation to harvest wild seed, in 2005, for research.

Pests and diseases

No disease or pest specific to Encephalartos cerinus is documented. The main risk mentioned by horticultural sources is root rot in soil that stays wet, and damping-off in young seedlings (PlantZAfrica). Scale insects, common on cultivated cycads, can also attack it.

Cold hardiness

The hardiness of Encephalartos cerinus is poorly known. PlantZAfrica says it tolerates light frost, and LLIFLE credits it with some resistance to short, light frosts if kept dry, but no dated, measured observation of cold damage or survival has been published.

Mediterranean and European experience

No in-ground trial is documented in France, Italy or elsewhere in Europe. The only European report, the potted plant in Ravenna mentioned above, gives no temperature. LLIFLE advises keeping the plant completely dry in winter at about 10 °C (50 °F). For gardeners in mild Mediterranean climates, coastal California or southern Australia, pot culture kept dry and frost-free in winter remains the cautious choice until reliable data exist.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

In the wild, the Tugela Ferry valley is described as hot and dry; no precise climate data on the frosts that may occur there are published. Survival in cold conditions among Encephalartos often concerns young plants whose caudex is still underground, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil. The trunk of Encephalartos cerinus stays small and largely buried, but its leaves and growing point benefit from that protection only while the crown remains at ground level. No USDA zone is published for the species by a botanical source. In Britain it can only be grown under glass; it is grown outdoors in southern Florida, coastal California and at Melbourne, but none of these gardens publishes cold data for it.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data are published for Encephalartos cerinus itself, but the risk is documented in the genus: the toxicity of Encephalartos horridus was demonstrated as early as 1944 by Van der Walt, and Encephalartos, like other cycads, contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; the seeds are the most dangerous part. The ASPCA reports vomiting, diarrhoea, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses that have eaten cycads.

Treat the plant as poisonous: wear gloves to clean seeds, pick up any that fall, and keep plants and seeds away from children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use specific to Encephalartos cerinus is documented. PlantZAfrica records that in Zulu culture most cycads, called isigqiki-somkhovu, are planted at the gate of the homestead to protect it from evil spirits; this belief concerns cycads in general. The threat to the species is horticultural: SANBI attributes its near disappearance to the removal of plants for gardens and collections.

Frequently asked questions

Why is Encephalartos cerinus blue?

Because a thick layer of wax covers its leaves and cones. This character sets it apart from its close relatives and gave it its name, from the Latin cerinus, “waxy”. The blue-green cones turn orange-yellow as they ripen.

Does it still exist in the wild?

It is uncertain. In 2015 SANBI considered it possibly extinct in the wild, with some reports of four or five plants; PlantZAfrica (2019) cites estimates of 10 to 70. Of the roughly 200 adults discovered in 1987, most were dug up in the months after the species was described in 1989.

Is it frost-hardy?

Only to light frost, as far as is known. PlantZAfrica says it tolerates light frost, but no measured value has been published. Outside nearly frost-free climates, grow it in a pot and keep it dry and frost-free in winter.

How big does it get?

It stays small: its trunk, underground or barely exposed, is no more than 30 cm long and 20 to 25 cm thick. Its leaves are 90 to 120 cm long, rarely 150 cm, so the plant is much wider than its trunk.

Can I buy one legally?

Yes, if the plant was propagated in cultivation and carries the CITES documents required for a species in CITES Appendix I and, in the European Union, Annex A. South Africa allows exports only of seedlings raised by registered nurseries.

How do I tell it from Encephalartos umbeluziensis?

By the wax and the habitat. Encephalartos cerinus has thick blue wax on its leaves and cones, leaves a waxy smell on the fingers and grows on open cliffs; Encephalartos umbeluziensis has longer, green leaves with small marginal teeth and grows in light shade.

Is it poisonous?

It must be assumed so. No data exist for the species, but Encephalartos seeds contain toxins dangerous to people and animals. Keep seeds and plants out of reach of children and pets.
