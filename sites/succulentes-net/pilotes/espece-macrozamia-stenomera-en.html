Macrozamia stenomera is a small cycad from north-western New South Wales, in eastern Australia, that lives in the Nandewar Range, around Mount Kaputar, and in the Moonbi Range near Tamworth. A species of the genus Macrozamia, it stands out from almost all others by its leaflets, forked one to four times into very narrow segments, which give the leaves a feathery look. Long confused with Macrozamia heteromera, it was described in 1959; some of its plants had earlier been named Macrozamia heteromera var. tenuifolia. It grows at up to about 1,450 m on Mount Kaputar and is listed as Near Threatened.

Key facts about Macrozamia stenomera

  • Accepted name: Macrozamia stenomera L.A.S.Johnson (1959), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and the Australian Plant Census
  • Former names and synonyms: Macrozamia heteromera var. tenuifolia C.Moore (1884); Macrozamia heteromera var. dicranophylloides J.Schust. (1932), according to Johnson (1959) and the Australian Plant Census
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales; section Parazamia
  • Common names: no common name attested in English or any other language
  • Natural range: endemic to New South Wales, on the north-western slopes and the western Northern Tablelands, in and around the Nandewar and Moonbi Ranges
  • IUCN status: Near Threatened (NT B1b(v)), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated September 2020 and published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union; no listing under Australian or New South Wales threatened-species law (Atlas of Living Australia)
  • Documented hardiness: no cultivation data; in its mountain habitat, frequent frosts and occasional snow (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, dry eucalypt woodland on stony soils, and rocky, mostly north-facing basalt slopes on Mount Kaputar
  • Habit and adult size: underground stem 8 to 25 cm across; 1 to 10 leaves 40 to 90 cm long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: grown at the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney and the Australian Botanic Garden Mount Annan; declared to BGCI by 10 institutions, three of them in the United States and none in Britain
  • Toxicity: not documented for the species; seeds and leaves toxic throughout the genus (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Macrozamia stenomera

It is recognised by its leaflets divided several times into segments 1 to 4 mm wide, and by stomata present only on the underside of the leaflets. The Flora of Australia (Hill, 1998), online through the ABRS, describes it as follows.

  • Stem: underground, 10 to 25 cm across (8 to 15 cm according to Johnson and the flora of New South Wales).
  • Leaves: 1 to 7 per crown (2 to 10 according to Johnson), 40 to 90 cm long, moderately keeled, grey-green or blue, semi-glossy; petiole 7 to 15 cm long; axis not to moderately twisted.
  • Leaflets: 70 to 120 per leaf, forked, 8 to 20 cm long, much paler beneath, in segments 1 to 4 mm wide with rolled margins and a blunt, non-pungent tip; the lowest ones are not reduced to spines.
  • Male cones: narrowly ovoid, 15 to 20 cm long and 5 to 6 cm across.
  • Female cones: ovoid, 10 to 18 cm long and 6 to 10 cm across.
  • Seeds: ovoid, 26 to 30 mm long and 18 to 21 mm wide, with red flesh.

Johnson's original Latin description (1959) adds that the leaf axis is usually twisted through 90° to 360°, mostly towards the tip, and that the leaflets fork one to four times, most often two or three, into narrow, diverging segments. The primary segments are 2 to 4 mm wide, the later ones 1 to 3 mm, with 4 to 6 prominent veins beneath. The leaflet base is slightly callused, yellowish to orange. The leaflets are green, hardly glossy, not glaucous beneath, and carry stomata on the lower surface only.

Observers for the Palm and Cycad Societies of Australia (PACSOA) have counted 4 to 13 segments per leaflet; the leaflets near the leaf tip often stay undivided. Each leaflet starts as a short stalk set at about 70° to the axis and twisted at the base, so that the leaflet faces the leaf tip, before splitting into two main arms in a V and then into further segments. They describe leaves that fade with age to a washed-out greyish green, and plants with 4 to 6 leaves on average, rarely up to 14, standing 50 to 70 cm above the ground, at most 90 cm. They have seen seeds with red flesh, but also lemon-yellow seeds, sometimes with a green patch; the floras mention only red or scarlet seeds.

Hybrids

Yes, natural hybrids with Macrozamia heteromera are recorded. Johnson (1959) mentions specimens of apparent hybrids between the two species where their populations meet, and probable hybrids between Macrozamia stenomera (or perhaps Macrozamia heteromera) and the plants now called Macrozamia plurinervia. No garden hybrid has been reported.

Possible confusion

Three cycads of New South Wales have divided leaflets: Macrozamia stenomera, Macrozamia heteromera and Macrozamia glaucophylla, to which may be added Macrozamia diplomera, whose leaflets fork once. According to Johnson (1959) and the PACSOA, Macrozamia stenomera differs from Macrozamia heteromera by:

  • narrower segments and leaflets that are usually more divided;
  • no stomata on the upper surface of the leaflets, whereas Macrozamia heteromera and Macrozamia glaucophylla have them on both surfaces;
  • no glaucous bloom, and leaves usually more twisted;
  • less siliceous soils.

The PACSOA stresses that the divided leaflets overlap so much that it is hard, even in the wild, to see their structure without a close examination. The decisive stomatal character can only be seen with a hand lens or a microscope.

Taxonomy

Macrozamia stenomera is accepted by The World List of Cycads and the Australian Plant Census. L.A.S. Johnson described it in 1959 in his revision of the Australian cycads, published in the Proceedings of the Linnean Society of New South Wales (84: 106–107). The holotype, a sterile collection made by Johnson and E.F. Constable in November 1954 above Coryah Gap, at 4,500 feet (about 1,370 m), in the Nandewar Range, is held at the National Herbarium of New South Wales in Sydney (NSW 32204).

Until then these plants were not distinguished from Macrozamia heteromera, although Charles Moore had named some of them Macrozamia heteromera var. tenuifolia in 1884. Johnson explains why he did not take up that name: the existing names were all at varietal rank, had been applied confusingly and were based on a specimen rather than on a population; he therefore preferred, as the code of nomenclature allows, a new epithet with a new type. He also notes that Schuster's Macrozamia heteromera var. tenuifolia f. harmsii belongs to Macrozamia heteromera, not to Macrozamia stenomera. In 1959 the cones were not known with certainty; Johnson thought they would resemble those of the other species of section Parazamia, to which Macrozamia stenomera belongs. Its chromosome number is 2n = 18 (L.A.S. Johnson, 1963; C.J. Marchant, 1968; as given by the Flora of Australia).

Etymology and history

The name comes from the Greek stenos, “narrow”, and meros, “part”, after the narrow segments of the leaflets (Haynes, 2022, as given by The World List of Cycads). Johnson himself explains in his description that the epithet refers to the characteristically narrow segments of the leaflets. A more detailed account of the species, by Paul Kennedy, appeared in Principes minor (no. 55, March 1992) and in the Cycad Newsletter (vol. 16, no. 3, December 1993); the PACSOA page is a revised version of it.

Common names

No common name is attested for Macrozamia stenomera, in English or in any other language; the Virtual Cycad Encyclopedia of the Palm & Cycad Societies of Florida gives “common name unknown”, and the Atlas of Living Australia lists none. The scientific name is used.

In the wild

Macrozamia stenomera is endemic to New South Wales. According to the Flora of Australia, it grows on the north-western slopes and the western Northern Tablelands, in and around the Nandewar and Moonbi Ranges, scattered in dry eucalypt woodland on stony soils. Johnson (1959) placed it on stony but not highly siliceous soils, and the flora of New South Wales on stony but sandy hillsides.

The PACSOA gives a more detailed picture. The species is centred on Mount Kaputar, the highest point of the Nandewar Range (1,510 m), about 50 km east of Narrabri, with a disjunct population in the Moonbi Range near Tamworth and plants on the sandy plains nearer Narrabri. On Mount Kaputar it grows as scattered plants or small groups on rocky, generally north-facing basalt slopes at about 1,450 m, on shallow, stony, eroded basalt soils. The vegetation there combines a heath-like understorey on exposed shallow soils with sub-alpine woodland on deeper soils, and snow gums (Eucalyptus pauciflora) flourish above 1,350 m. High winds, frequent frosts and occasional snow, four or five times a year, are usual. That the same species also grows in much hotter, drier conditions on the plains near Narrabri shows its variability, in the PACSOA's words.

The climate changes sharply with altitude. At Narrabri, at 212 m, the average rainfall is 660 mm a year, on 64 days, a third of it in summer; the extremes reached at least once a week in July and January are −0.8 °C (30.6 °F) and 37.6 °C (99.7 °F), and frost occurs on 29 days a year. There is no official weather station on the range, but according to local sources quoted by the PACSOA, the upper slopes of Mount Kaputar can be up to 10 °C (18 °F) cooler than Narrabri and receive about 1,200 mm a year. Coning is irregular: females usually bear one cone, sometimes two, and males up to four.

Fire is part of this environment. The PACSOA records major bushfires on Mount Kaputar in 1951, 1957, 1974, 1986 and 2006; the last, generally of low intensity, burned for seven weeks, from November 2006 to January 2007, over about 14,500 of the park's 41,000 hectares.

Conservation status and regulation

Macrozamia stenomera is listed as Near Threatened (NT) on the IUCN Red List under criterion B1b(v), which refers to a small range with a continuing decline in the number of mature plants. The assessment, by J.D. Bösenberg, is dated 17 September 2020 and was published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, the Flora of Australia, GBIF and Crossref, checked on 26 October 2026); an earlier assessment, by P.I. Forster, was published in 2010. Some pages still call it Vulnerable, which does not match the current assessment. Part of the population lies within Mount Kaputar National Park, created in 1967 according to the PACSOA, and the Atlas of Living Australia shows no listing for the species under Australian or New South Wales threatened-species law.

Like other Macrozamia, it has been listed in CITES Appendix II since 4 February 1977, with annotation #4: seeds and pollen are exempt, but plants are not. In the European Union it falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026 (Species+, checked on 26 October 2026). Check the regulations in force before any purchase or import, and prefer seed-raised plants of known origin.

Growing Macrozamia stenomera

Macrozamia stenomera is very rare in cultivation, and no published trial gives advice specific to the species. The PACSOA shows a cultivated plant producing a new leaf, without details of its conditions. Its natural habitat, well-drained stony slopes in full light or under open woodland, points to a very free-draining mineral substrate and a bright position; these suggestions are deduced from the habitat.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Light: bright, from full sun to the light shade of open woodland, as in its habitat.
  • Soil: stony and very free-draining; in the wild, shallow basalt soils on the mountain and sandy soils on the plains.
  • Water: no published data for the species. Cycadales.eu, a European specialist nursery, describes it as easy to grow but slow, indifferent to soil type provided the substrate drains well, and fond of a good amount of water and regular feeding; this is a nursery's advice, not a published trial.

Container cultivation

No cultivation experience has been published in Europe or North America. Its small size makes it suitable, in theory, for a deep pot of mineral compost; a plant in a pot loses the protection that the soil gives to its underground stem in winter (see Cold hardiness). On the Italian Tropicamente forum, a grower in Ravenna (Emilia-Romagna, Italy) noted in November 2009 that a specialist seller offered seeds said to come from Mount Kaputar, at a very high price; the thread reports no result.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney's Garden Explorer lists four plants of wild accessions of 1990 and 1992 in bed 102i, and a plant of garden origin of 1997 (updated 1 October 2026); the Australian Botanic Garden Mount Annan lists 29 plants, many of them from accessions of wild origin received in 2023 (updated 15 October 2026). The Botanic Garden and State Herbarium of South Australia (Adelaide) and the University of Melbourne's System Garden also declare it to BGCI.
  • United States: the Huntington (San Marino), the San Diego Zoo's botanical gardens and the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley declare it to BGCI. It is not in the online catalogue of Montgomery Botanical Center.
  • United Kingdom: no British garden declares it to BGCI.

In all, 10 institutions declare the species to BGCI (checked on 26 October 2026), the others in China, the Czech Republic and France. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not necessarily a living plant today. No English-language grower's report has been found.

Propagation

Macrozamia stenomera is propagated by seed. Fresh, fertilised seed is needed, which means a male and a female plant, and hand pollination outside Australia. According to the PACSOA, the division of the leaflets is already visible in seedlings and juvenile plants, with fewer segments than in adults, which helps to check the identity of a seedling. Seed is scarce and, according to the nursery quoted above, very expensive. Wear gloves to clean off the flesh of the seeds, which is toxic.

Pests and diseases

No pest or disease specific to Macrozamia stenomera is documented. As for other cycads, too much water in winter and scale insects are the risks most often mentioned for the genus.

Cold hardiness

Macrozamia stenomera is reputed to be one of the hardiest Macrozamia, but this reputation rests on its habitat, not on cultivation trials: no measured result has been published.

Mediterranean and European experience

No cultivation result is documented in Europe, in the ground or in a pot. In 2014, a member of the French Fous de palmiers forum relayed the ranking of an Australian cycad specialist, who placed Macrozamia stenomera among the hardiest species of the genus, together with the other cycads of the Coonabarabran area and the Pilliga Scrub, without giving a temperature.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

In its mountain habitat on Mount Kaputar, the species experiences frequent frosts and snow four or five times a year (PACSOA); at Narrabri, at the foot of the range, frost occurs on 29 days a year. No minimum temperature measured at the sites of the species is documented. The plants have an underground stem, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil; a potted plant does not have this protection. The only rating found is commercial: Cycadales.eu gives “zone 9b, maybe colder”, zone 9b corresponding to an average annual minimum of −3.9 to −1.1 °C (25 to 30 °F). Until trial results are available, a pot sheltered from winter wet and hard frost is the prudent choice in Britain and in the colder parts of the United States.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data specific to Macrozamia stenomera have been published, but the risk is well documented for the genus. Macrozamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a poison of the liver; their leaves can cause a locomotor disorder of the hindquarters in cattle, linked to damage to the spinal cord (Healy, 1967). Analyses cited by Asmussen (2011) measured macrozamin in the seeds of Macrozamia heteromera, a closely related species.

Treat the plant as toxic: wear gloves to handle the seeds, pick up those that fall and keep them out of reach of children, dogs and cats. In case of ingestion, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use specific to Macrozamia stenomera is documented. Asmussen's review (2011) of the eating of cycad seeds by Aboriginal people does not mention it.

Frequently asked questions

What is special about the leaves of Macrozamia stenomera?

Their leaflets are forked. Each leaflet divides one to four times into segments 1 to 4 mm wide, up to 13 per leaflet according to the PACSOA, which gives the leaf a feathery, almost conifer-like look.

How can it be told from Macrozamia heteromera?

By the stomata and the fineness of the leaflets. Macrozamia stenomera has stomata on the lower surface only, narrower and more divided segments, and no glaucous bloom. In the field, hybrids sometimes complicate identification.

Is it the hardiest Macrozamia?

Perhaps, but nothing proves it. It grows up to about 1,450 m, with frost and snow, and an Australian specialist ranks it among the hardiest; no cultivation trial with figures has been published, however.

Is it endangered?

It is Near Threatened (NT) according to the IUCN (assessment published in 2022), because of its small range. Pages that call it Vulnerable (VU) do not reflect the current assessment.

Are its seeds yellow or red?

Both have been reported. The Australian floras describe red or scarlet flesh, but PACSOA observers have also seen lemon-yellow seeds on Mount Kaputar.

Can it be grown in Britain or the United States?

Nobody knows yet. No trial has been published, and the species is very rare in the trade. If you obtain a seed-raised plant, grow it in a very free-draining mineral mix and protect it from winter wet; in the United States, three botanic gardens in California declare it.
