Encephalartos aplanatus is a cycad without an above-ground trunk from north-eastern Eswatini (formerly Swaziland), also recorded in Mozambique, where it grows in the shade of deciduous trees in fairly dry ravine forest. This species of the genus Encephalartos bears only a few leaves, usually two to six, but they can reach 3.5 m. Its name, “not flat”, refers to its leaflets, whose wavy margins twist out of the plane of the leaf. Long confused with Encephalartos villosus, under whose name it was illustrated as early as 1947, it was described only in 1996. The IUCN lists it as Vulnerable.

Key facts about Encephalartos aplanatus

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos aplanatus Vorster (1996), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15)
  • Former names and synonyms: no formal synonym; illustrated in 1947 as Encephalartos villosus (in the sense of Dyer), a misidentification
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Swazi north-east forest cycad (English); umphanga wehlathi (Zulu), according to PlantZAfrica
  • Natural range: north-eastern Eswatini, and Mozambique according to The World List of Cycads; Species+ also lists South Africa
  • IUCN status: Vulnerable (VU), criteria A2cd; B1ab(iii,v)+2ab(iii,v), assessment dated May 2020 and published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I (whole genus); Annex A in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: no data, either in the wild or in cultivation (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: fairly dense shade of deciduous trees in dry ravine forest; shade or filtered light in cultivation
  • Habit and adult size: underground stem with the apex usually visible above the soil, plants normally solitary; 2 to 6 leaves (up to 8), at most 3.5 m long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: the Huntington, Lotusland and San Diego Botanic Garden in California (BGCI declarations); no British or Australian record
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; treat it as poisonous like all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos aplanatus

Look for very long, arching leaves, few in number, and large, glossy dark green leaflets whose toothed, wavy margins twist out of the plane of the leaf. The original description by Vorster (1996) gives these characters:

  • Habit: no above-ground trunk; the apex of the stem is usually visible above the soil; plants mostly solitary
  • Leaves: 2 to 6, sometimes 8, erect then arching, becoming almost horizontal with age in deep shade; up to 3.5 m long; petiole up to 20 cm; petiole and lower rachis covered in off-white, bristly hairs
  • Leaflets: inserted at about 75° on the rachis, the two rows almost in one plane; the lowest leaflets are gradually reduced to prickles; middle leaflets very narrowly ovate, up to 30 cm long and 4 cm wide, with an acute but not pungent tip; both margins carry a few teeth (very rarely none) and are somewhat wavy; fairly soft, striate but not ribbed beneath, glossy dark green
  • Male cones: up to three, at most 65 cm long and 8 to 10 cm wide, on a stalk up to 22 cm; green when they emerge, pale yellow when ripe
  • Female cones: up to two, about 40 cm long and 12 cm wide at most, on a short, stout stalk (at most 6 cm); dark green when they emerge, then bright orange-yellow
  • Seeds: ellipsoid with almost truncate ends, about 25 mm long and 13 to 15 mm wide, with a bright red fleshy coat

In both sexes the exposed faces of the cone scales are flat, smooth and hairless, with faint facets; according to Vorster, the lower ridge of the female scales is not fringed, unlike that of Encephalartos villosus. PlantZAfrica adds that the leaflets tend to be wider than those of Encephalartos villosus and that opposite leaflets lie at 180° to each other.

Hybrids

No hybrid of Encephalartos aplanatus has been reported. Vorster considered the possibility of a hybrid origin with Encephalartos villosus and rejected it: the known localities lie about a hundred kilometres from the nearest population of that species.

Possible confusion

The usual confusion is with Encephalartos villosus, whose cones are almost identical. According to Vorster (1996), both species lack an above-ground trunk, have a small number of long, arching leaves (fewer than ten) and lowest leaflets reduced to prickles. Encephalartos aplanatus differs in having:

  • a larger plant in all its parts, with leaves up to 3.5 m instead of 2.5 m
  • leaves often with a short petiole, instead of sessile leaves
  • larger leaflets (up to 30 by 4 cm instead of 25 by 2 cm), more toothed, with twisted or wavy margins instead of flat ones
  • a stem apex often visible above the soil, whereas it remains entirely buried in Encephalartos villosus
  • solitary plants, whereas Encephalartos villosus often forms clumps by suckering

The species grows with neither of its two neighbours in Eswatini: not with Encephalartos villosus, nor with Encephalartos umbeluziensis, from which it is separated by a clear geographical gap.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos aplanatus is an accepted name with no synonyms. Piet Vorster, of Stellenbosch University, described it in the South African Journal of Botany (62(1): 57–60), in a paper received in September 1995 and published in 1996. The type, Vorster 2963b, is kept at the National Herbarium in Pretoria (PRE); as for other much sought-after cycads, the exact locality was not published.

The species had no earlier name, but it had been misidentified. Vorster referred to Encephalartos aplanatus plates 1001 and 1002 of volume 26 of The Flowering Plants of South Africa (1947), published by R. A. Dyer as Encephalartos villosus. This is a misidentification, not a synonym in the strict sense: Encephalartos villosus remains a separate species.

Vorster also discussed the rank of the taxon. His principle was to base species distinctions on reproductive organs, but this one rests only on vegetative characters. He nevertheless judged the gap clear enough to treat it as a species, since the rank of subspecies would imply a close relationship for which there is no evidence. The World List of Cycads accepts the species in its version of 15 August 2026.

Etymology and history

The name combines the Latin planatus, “flattened”, with the negative prefix a-: “not flat”. It refers to the twisted, wavy leaflets (Haynes, 2022, cited by The World List of Cycads), a feature Vorster described as margins “twisted out of plane or somewhat undulate”. PlantZAfrica gives the same explanation.

The species was in cultivation well before it was described. Vorster noted that, a few years before 1996, a fair number of plants had appeared in gardens, probably as a result of illegal collecting. His observations of phenology were made on plants grown in Eswatini, at Nelspruit, in Pretoria and in Stellenbosch. He recorded plants in the public collections of the Pretoria National Botanical Garden and the Lowveld Botanical Garden at Nelspruit, but not at Kirstenbosch; PlantZAfrica, written at Kirstenbosch in 2009, later describes the species being grown and hand-pollinated there.

Common names

PlantZAfrica (SANBI, 2009) gives the English name Swazi north-east forest cycad, after the region where it grows, and the Zulu name umphanga wehlathi. Species+ and the original description give no common name. Other English names seen on some websites, built on the author's name or a translation of the epithet, are not attested by a reference source.

In the wild

Encephalartos aplanatus is known from a small area of north-eastern Eswatini. The World List of Cycads also records it in Mozambique, and Species+ in South Africa, without giving localities. Vorster saw it only once in the wild, in a steep ravine, in the fairly dense shade of deciduous trees, in rather dry ravine forest. PlantZAfrica (2009) states that only one colony is known.

In gardens in Eswatini, Nelspruit, Pretoria and Stellenbosch, the cones appear in January, pollen is shed in April and the female cones break up in late September or October. PlantZAfrica records the same timing and notes that the cones are visited by weevils of the genus Porthetes (Curculionidae).

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos aplanatus is listed as Vulnerable (VU) on the IUCN Red List under criteria A2cd; B1ab(iii,v)+2ab(iii,v): a past decline of the population, linked to habitat degradation and collecting, within a very small range. The assessment, by J. D. Bösenberg, is dated May 2020 and was published in 2022, according to The World List of Cycads.

Vorster's findings in 1996 were bleak. On an expedition through the known range he found only one locality, with a few scattered plants in poor condition, and he could not find the species at the places where it had been seen in the late 1940s. He concluded that numbers, probably never large, had been reduced almost to extinction by illegal collecting, and that the plant could hardly be protected effectively on site, hence the secrecy about its locality. Because the plants are solitary and do not sucker, every plant removed is one fewer individual able to reproduce.

Like the whole genus, Encephalartos aplanatus has been listed in CITES Appendix I since 4 February 1977, without annotation, so plants, seeds and pollen are all covered. Commercial international trade in wild-collected plants is prohibited; only artificially propagated plants can be exported, from registered nurseries and with the required permits. In the European Union the species is in Annex A of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383. For artificially propagated plants imported from South Africa, the EU issued a positive opinion in 2011 for plants up to 7 cm in caudex diameter and, since 7 February 2013, a negative opinion above that size. These details come from Species+, the official CITES and EU database, consulted in October 2026; check the current rules before any purchase or import, and ask for proof that the plant was propagated in cultivation.

Growing Encephalartos aplanatus

Encephalartos aplanatus is a plant for shade: its natural home is the understorey of ravine forest, and growing sources agree on shade or filtered light. PlantZAfrica describes it as easy to grow, relatively fast-growing and easy to transplant, but not common in cultivation. Its long leaves need room: allow at least the length of a leaf all round the centre of the plant.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

The San Diego nursery Jungle Music, in southern California, recommends shade or filtered light in any climate and a rich but well-drained topsoil; it considers the species fairly tolerant of water, though not of waterlogged soil. At Kirstenbosch, in Cape Town, PlantZAfrica reports that plants are fed each spring with a nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium fertiliser in a 3:1:5 ratio, bone meal and an organic fertiliser, and receive generous helpings of compost at intervals during the year. A plant raised in shade will scorch if moved abruptly into full sun.

Container cultivation

No container-growing experience specific to the species is documented. With no hardiness data, pot culture in a large container of free-draining compost, in shade, sheltered from frost and winter rain, is the prudent choice in cool climates. No experience is documented on French or Italian forums either; a collector near Agen, in south-western France, claimed on the Fous de palmiers forum in January 2011, by hearsay and without any measurement, that it was the hardiest of the green Encephalartos, a claim confirmed nowhere.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

The species is rare in public collections. The Huntington, Lotusland and San Diego Botanic Garden, all in California, declare it to BGCI PlantSearch, which shows that a garden has listed the species at some point, not that the plant is alive today. It is absent from the online catalogue of Montgomery Botanical Center, and no British or Australian garden has been found listing it.

Propagation

Encephalartos aplanatus is propagated almost only from seed: all the plants Vorster saw were solitary, PlantZAfrica states that it does not sucker, and Jungle Music that it almost never does. Seed production in cultivation requires male and female plants coning at the same time and hand-pollination. PlantZAfrica describes the method used at Kirstenbosch:

  • collect pollen as soon as the male cone sheds it when tapped, and store it at −15 °C (5 °F)
  • watch the female cones closely: the scales open for between three days and two weeks, depending on the plant
  • pollinate “wet”, with pollen suspended in distilled water and applied with a syringe, which gives a good success rate at Kirstenbosch
  • leave harvested seed to mature for a year, then sow at the beginning of summer in river sand on a heated bench at 24 to 28 °C (75 to 82 °F); germination usually starts about three weeks after sowing

The seeds, about 25 mm long, must be fresh and fertile, and all plants and seeds offered for sale must come from cultivation with CITES documents.

Pests and diseases

No disease specific to Encephalartos aplanatus is documented. PlantZAfrica notes that its leaves and cones are prone to scale and mealybug infestations. The usual risks of Encephalartos in cultivation also apply: root rot in poorly drained soil, and sun scorch in a shade plant moved suddenly into full sun.

Cold hardiness

No hardiness data exist for Encephalartos aplanatus, either from the wild or from cultivation. Values quoted on some websites rest on no observation, and no USDA zone is published by a botanical source.

Mediterranean and European experience

No growing report is documented from France, Italy or elsewhere in Europe for any climate zone, and the Jardin zoologique tropical has published no observation on this species. The hearsay claim from south-western France mentioned above is not a measurement. Gardeners in Mediterranean climates, coastal California or southern Australia should treat the species as tender until data are published.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No dated, quantified report is documented outside southern Africa. We know only that the species grows outdoors and cones in several South African gardens, from Nelspruit to Stellenbosch (Vorster, 1996), and that it is grown at Kirstenbosch (PlantZAfrica). As in other Encephalartos without an above-ground trunk, the buried stem benefits from the thermal inertia of the soil, but no limiting temperature can be deduced from this. In Britain it can only be grown under glass.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data are published for Encephalartos aplanatus itself, but the risk is documented in the genus: the toxicity of Encephalartos horridus was demonstrated as early as 1944 by Van der Walt, and Encephalartos, like other cycads, contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; the seeds are the most dangerous part. PlantZAfrica reminds growers that cycad seeds are toxic to humans and should be handled with gloves. The ASPCA reports vomiting, diarrhoea, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses that have eaten cycads.

Treat the plant as poisonous: wear gloves to clean seeds, pick up any that fall, and keep plants and seeds away from children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use specific to Encephalartos aplanatus is documented. Its only known use is ornamental, and the demand from gardens and collections is what emptied its wild populations.

Frequently asked questions

What does the name aplanatus mean?

“Not flat”. The epithet, formed from the prefix a- and the Latin planatus, describes the leaflets, whose margins ripple and twist out of the plane of the leaf. It is the simplest feature for telling it from Encephalartos villosus, whose leaflets are flat.

How does it differ from Encephalartos villosus?

Mainly in size and in the leaflets. Encephalartos aplanatus has leaves up to 3.5 m long, often with a short petiole, and leaflets up to 30 by 4 cm, toothed and wavy; it does not form clumps. The cones of the two species are almost identical.

Can it be grown in full sun?

Better not. In the wild it lives in the shade of deciduous trees, and growers recommend shade or filtered light. A plant raised in shade scorches if it is moved suddenly into the sun.

Is Encephalartos aplanatus frost-hardy?

Nobody knows. No frost observation has been published for this species. Grow it in a pot and keep it frost-free in winter.

Does it produce offsets?

Almost never. All the plants Vorster saw were solitary, and PlantZAfrica states that it does not sucker. Propagation therefore depends on seed, which requires both a male and a female plant.

Does it have a common name?

Yes. PlantZAfrica gives the English name Swazi north-east forest cycad and the Zulu name umphanga wehlathi. Other English names found online are not attested by a reference source.

Is it protected?

Yes. It is listed as Vulnerable by the IUCN, in CITES Appendix I and in Annex A in the European Union. Only plants propagated in cultivation, with their documents, can be bought legally.
