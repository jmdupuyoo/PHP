Zamia wallisii is a cycad with an underground stem from Colombia, known only from the department of Antioquia, in the Andes; it is the species of the genus Zamia with the broadest leaflets. It often carries a single leaf, with a few huge leaflets set on a short stalk and deeply furrowed between the veins. Discovered in 1873 by the German explorer Gustav Wallis and described in 1875, it was then lost sight of for about a century, before being found again in the 1980s. It is rated Critically Endangered.

Key facts about Zamia wallisii

  • Accepted name: Zamia wallisii A.Braun (1875) according to The World List of Cycads; IPNI also records a publication of the same year under the name of H.J.Veitch, the record linked to POWO (see Taxonomy); POWO could not be consulted directly
  • Former names and synonyms: Aulacophyllum wallisii (A.Braun) Regel (1876)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: no name attested, in English or in Spanish
  • Natural range: Colombia, department of Antioquia (endemic); altitude not documented in the sources consulted
  • IUCN status: Critically Endangered (CR), criterion B1ab(iii,v), assessment published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (whole genus, since 1977; seeds exempt under annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: no data (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: not documented; cloud forests of Antioquia according to The World List of Cycads, the “temperate zone” of the Andes according to Braun (1875)
  • Habit and adult size: underground stem 3 to 5 cm in diameter; usually a single leaf, sometimes 2 or 3, 0.5 to 1.5 m long, with 2 to 5 pairs of leaflets 30 to 50 cm by 12 to 15 cm (Flora de Colombia, 2001); no more than 11 leaflets per leaf (Calonje and colleagues, 2018)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: one accession, from 2001, at Montgomery Botanical Center (Florida), also declared to BGCI; no British or Australian record
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; treat it as poisonous, like all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Zamia wallisii

It is recognised by its very large leaflets, elliptic or almost oval, borne on a true stalk (petiolule) and so deeply furrowed between the veins on the upper surface that they look pleated; the plant usually has only one leaf and no visible trunk. The most complete description is the one by Dennis Stevenson in the Flora de Colombia (2001):

  • Stem: underground, 3 to 5 cm in diameter.
  • Cataphylls: oval, 1 to 2 cm long and 2 to 3 cm wide.
  • Leaves: usually one, sometimes 2 or 3, 0.5 to 1.5 m long; petiole 0.3 to 1 m, with scattered to dense prickles; rachis with 2 to 5 pairs of leaflets, sometimes armed with a few prickles in its lower third.
  • Leaflets: stalked, elliptic, 30 to 50 cm long and 12 to 15 cm wide (middle leaflets), deeply furrowed between the veins on the upper surface, with a wedge-shaped base, a drawn-out tip and a margin toothed at least in the upper third. In its commentary, the same flora speaks of leaflets up to 30 cm wide.
  • Male cones: yellowish to light brown, cylindrical to narrowly cylindrical, 5 to 8 cm by 1 to 2 cm.
  • Female cones: unknown in 2001.
  • Chromosomes: 2n = 16 (Caputo and colleagues, 1996).

The original description by Alexander Braun (1875), made from young cultivated plants, adds a few details: a stem “shaped like a tuber, half underground”, almost always with a single leaf; in young plants a single pair of leaflets, in older ones up to 8 pairs, “of unusual size and width”; a leaflet stalk about 2 inches (about 5 cm) long; a blade more than a foot long, broadly spatula-shaped in young plants, almost oval, “spade-shaped” and slightly heart-shaped at the base in adults, with a few faint teeth towards the tip; 25 to 45 veins across the middle of the leaflet, sunken above and strongly raised beneath. The conversion of the old units is approximate.

The two descriptions do not quite agree: Braun counts up to 8 pairs of leaflets, the Flora de Colombia 2 to 5 pairs. The measurements in the flora are also almost identical to those Stevenson had published in 1993 for Zamia dressleri, apart from the leaflet stalk; the sources consulted do not explain this resemblance. In describing Zamia paucifoliolata, Calonje and colleagues (2018) note that Zamia wallisii has “the widest leaflets in the genus” and no more than 11 leaflets per leaf, one of the lowest numbers in Zamia.

Hybrids

No hybrid, natural or raised in cultivation, has been documented.

Possible confusion

In Colombia, confusion is unlikely: in the key to the Colombian Zamia in the Flora de Colombia, Zamia wallisii is the only species with furrowed, pleated leaflets that combines leaflets toothed at least in their upper third, a leaflet stalk and an underground stem. The other pleated-leaved species are grouped there as tree-like plants with stalkless, entire or almost entire leaflets: Zamia roezlii, with linear leaflets, from the mangroves and coastal forests of the Pacific, Zamia amplifolia, with elliptic, entire leaflets, from the Cauca valley, and Zamia montana, with narrowly obovate leaflets, also from Antioquia but at higher altitude (1,750 to 2,080 m).

Outside Colombia, the most similar species are Panamanian. Zamia dressleri, from central Panama, also has only one leaf, with 2 to 5 pairs of large pleated leaflets, but stalkless: when describing it, Stevenson (1993) compared it precisely with Zamia wallisii, which he regarded as its sister species. Zamia imperialis, from central Panama, also has very large pleated leaflets, with middle leaflets 27 to 75 cm long and 6.5 to 21 cm wide according to Taylor and colleagues (2008), but it has a trunk and the two species live far apart.

Finally, Zamia oligodonta, from the Western Cordillera, belongs to the same small group as Zamia wallisii and Zamia montana (see Taxonomy).

Taxonomy

The World List of Cycads accepts Zamia wallisii A.Braun, published in 1875 in the Monatsberichte of the Berlin Academy of Sciences (page 376), in an appendix to Alexander Braun’s essay “Die Frage nach der Gymnospermie der Cycadeen”, presented to the Academy on 22 April 1875. Braun wrote “Zamia Wallisii (hort. Veitch)”, that is, the name used by the Veitch nursery. IPNI also records a publication of the same year in the Gardeners’ Chronicle (new series, 3: 795), attributed to H.J.Veitch; this is the record that IPNI flags as present in POWO. The plant is the same and the name does not change; only the cited author varies with the source. POWO could not be consulted directly, and the sources read do not show which of the two publications appeared first.

In 1876 Eduard Regel moved the species into the genus Aulacophyllum, created for the Zamia with furrowed leaflets, as Aulacophyllum wallisii; that genus is now included in Zamia and the name is a synonym.

The typification has a complicated history. As the original material was thought to be lost, Stevenson and Sabato designated in 1986 a neotype collected in Antioquia by Stevenson and colleagues (number 582), kept in New York (NY), with isoneotypes at FTG, HUA, JAUM, NAP and NY: this is the type given by the Flora de Colombia in 2001. The World List of Cycads and IPNI now give a lectotype, a Wallis collection kept in Stockholm, and cite a 2009 paper by Anders Lindström on the typification of Zamia names (Taxon). Under the International Code of Nomenclature, a neotype is superseded when original material is found again: this is very probably what happened, but Lindström’s paper has not been seen.

On the evolutionary side, the transcriptome-based study by Lindstrom and colleagues (2024), which sampled a plant from Antioquia, places Zamia wallisii in a strongly supported clade of the western Andes, within a morphologically uniform subclade with Zamia oligodonta and Zamia montana. According to the same authors, this subclade had been supported by morphological data in Calonje and colleagues (2019); the 2024 study confirms that Zamia montana, not sampled in 2019, belongs to it. The three species do not have the same kind of stem: Zamia montana has a tree-like trunk that can reach 1.5 m (2015 revision).

Etymology and history

The epithet honours Gustav Wallis (1830–1878), a German botanist, plant collector and explorer, who first collected the species in the cloud forests of Antioquia (The World List of Cycads, after Haynes, 2022). Braun states that Wallis discovered in 1873, in what was then New Granada, the three cycads described in his appendix, Zamia wallisii, Zamia obliqua and Zamia montana; for Zamia wallisii he notes “in the temperate zone” and adds that the traveller had not seen its cones, so that the genus remained uncertain.

According to the Flora de Colombia, the species was collected again by Kalbreyer in 1888; both collections were later lost, and the very existence of the species was doubted until its rediscovery in the 1980s. The flora nevertheless cites among the specimens examined a Kalbreyer collection kept at Kew (Kalbreyer 1927), without explaining this contradiction. In 2001 a single population was known, at the type locality, where the presence of seedlings showed that reproduction took place, apparently rarely.

Common names

No common name is attested for Zamia wallisii, in English or in Spanish. In Colombia, popular names such as “chigua” are used for Zamia in general, but none of the sources consulted applies one to this species.

In the wild

Zamia wallisii is endemic to Colombia, in the department of Antioquia (The World List of Cycads). The Flora de Colombia reported it as collected only at the type locality; Braun placed it in the “temperate zone”, that is, the middle-altitude belt of the Andes, and The World List of Cycads speaks of cloud forests. The sources consulted give neither the altitude, nor the soil, nor the climate, nor the number of plants; female cones were still unknown in 2001 and pollinators have not been documented.

The map of The World List of Cycads, deliberately imprecise (about 11 km) to protect the populations, shows observations in Antioquia documented by many photographs.

Conservation status and regulation

Zamia wallisii is rated Critically Endangered (CR) on the IUCN Red List under criterion B1ab(iii,v), in an assessment by C. López-Gallego published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads; GBIF gives the same category; the Red List itself could not be consulted). Under the IUCN definitions, this criterion corresponds, for the CR category, to an extent of occurrence of less than 100 km², a population severely fragmented or known from a single location, and a continuing decline in habitat quality and in the number of mature individuals. As early as 2001, the Flora de Colombia considered it, with Zamia montana, the most threatened Zamia in Colombia, and reported, after the botanist Rodrigo Bernal, an “extreme” destruction of habitat in the area.

Like all Zamia except Zamia restrepoi, Zamia wallisii falls under CITES Appendix II, where the genus has been listed since 4 February 1977. International trade in the plants is therefore allowed, but requires an export permit issued by the country of origin. The listing carries annotation #4: seeds, pollen, seedlings and tissue cultures obtained in vitro and transported in sterile containers are not covered by CITES. In the European Union the species falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, whose current list is set by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026.

Species+ has no separate entry for this species: it is covered by the listing “Zamia spp.”. This general information does not replace the official texts: check the regulations in force before buying or exchanging plants, and ask the seller for proof of origin.

Growing Zamia wallisii

No botanic garden has published growing advice specific to Zamia wallisii, and no amateur growing report has been found. The species, known from very few plants in the wild, is exceedingly rare in collections.

The French specialist nursery Cycadales.eu places Zamia wallisii among the cloud-forest Zamia of Panama and Colombia, which it rates “very difficult”, for experienced growers only, and extremely rare both in cultivation and in the wild. For lack of data, it suggests as a starting point a compost that always drains well, neutral to acid, neutral to acid water, and temperatures of 20 to 30 °C (68 to 86 °F), avoiding any excess of cold or heat (Lavaud, 2023). For the rest, an understorey plant of cloud forest, with an underground stem and thin, very broad leaflets, is best kept frost-free, in humid, shaded conditions; these indications follow from its habitat and have not been validated by published trials.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

No outdoor cultivation has been documented anywhere outside its native range. A frost-free, constantly humid climate without extremes of heat is the only kind in which planting out could be considered, which is a deduction from the habitat, not a tested result.

Container cultivation

No container-growing report has been published. On the information available, it is a plant for a humid, shaded glasshouse kept within the range given by Cycadales.eu, in a deep pot of free-draining compost.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In short: a single documented accession, in Florida; no British or Australian record.

  • United States: the online catalogue of Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida) lists one accession, number 20010301, from 2001 according to its numbering (consulted on 24 October 2026); the garden also declares the species to BGCI.
  • Australia: no record in BGCI or in the online catalogues of Australian gardens included in the inventory compiled on 19 October 2026.
  • United Kingdom: no record in BGCI or in the inventory of British garden catalogues (Edinburgh, Cambridge, Chelsea Physic Garden).

A BGCI declaration shows that a garden held the plant at some point, not that it is alive today. The catalogue does not say how the Montgomery plant is grown.

Propagation

No data specific to the species exist. Like all Zamia, it has male and female plants and is propagated from fresh seed after pollination; the seeds of Zamia wallisii are not described in the sources consulted, female cones having still been unknown in 2001. Seeds are exempt from CITES under annotation #4, but they must come from cultivated plants of legal origin.

Pests and diseases

No pest or disease specific to the species has been documented. In cultivation, Zamia are mainly at risk from rot in cold, sodden compost, and from scale insects. For the humid-tropical and cloud-forest groups, Cycadales.eu notes that keeping the compost drier in winter, which protects species from seasonally dry climates against rot, works poorly (Lavaud, 2023).

Cold hardiness

There are no data on the cold tolerance of Zamia wallisii, either in the wild or in cultivation.

Mediterranean and European experience

No in-ground cultivation of Zamia wallisii has been reported in France, and no dated frost episode concerns it. The Jardin zoologique tropical at La Londe-les-Maures, in the Var, has published no observation of this species; the only reference point for the genus there, a Zamia integrifolia from Florida that survived −7 °C (19.4 °F) in February 2012 with damage to its foliage, cannot be transferred to an Andean species of another group that has never been tested in cold conditions. In Italy, no living plant is documented; an isoneotype, a mere herbarium specimen, is kept in the herbarium of the University of Naples (NAP) according to the Flora de Colombia.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No cold record has been found for the species, on forums or in botanic gardens. In the absence of any trial, a heated, humid glasshouse is the only prudent option in these countries.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity study specific to Zamia wallisii has been found. The risk is, however, documented in the genus and in other cycads: Zamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, and for Zamia furfuracea the ASPCA cites cycasin and BMAA, a neurotoxic amino acid, with vomiting, bloody diarrhoea and liver damage in dogs, cats and horses; the seeds are the most dangerous part. In the absence of evidence to the contrary, treat the plant as poisonous: keep seeds and leaves out of reach of children and animals, and wear gloves to clean seeds. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet straight away.

Traditional uses

No traditional use is documented for this species.

Frequently asked questions

Does it really have the largest leaflets in the genus?

The broadest, yes. Calonje and colleagues (2018) speak of “the widest leaflets in the genus”; the Flora de Colombia gives 12 to 15 cm for the width of the middle leaflets, and up to 30 cm in its commentary. None of the sources consulted compares these leaflets with those of all gymnosperms.

How many leaves does it carry?

Few. Usually one, sometimes two or three, with 2 to 5 pairs of leaflets and no more than 11 leaflets in all. In 1875 Braun saw only one pair of leaflets in young plants.

Why was it considered lost?

Because the first collections, by Wallis (1873) and Kalbreyer (1888), were lost and nobody found it again for about a century. It was rediscovered in the 1980s in Antioquia, according to the Flora de Colombia.

Should the author be A.Braun or H.J.Veitch?

It depends on the source. The World List of Cycads writes Zamia wallisii A.Braun; IPNI also records a publication by H.J.Veitch in the same year, 1875, the one linked to POWO. The name of the plant stays the same.

Which species are its closest relatives?

Zamia oligodonta and Zamia montana, with which it forms a small subclade of the western Andes of Colombia (Lindstrom and colleagues, 2024), and, in appearance, Zamia dressleri from Panama, which Stevenson regarded as its sister species.

Can I grow it in the UK?

There is no documented experience. No plant is reported in cultivation in Britain, and the nursery Cycadales.eu ranks the cloud-forest Zamia among the most difficult; any plant would need a humid, heated glasshouse.

Is it endangered?

Yes, critically. The IUCN rates it Critically Endangered (2022), for a tiny, declining range; in 2001 only one population was known.

Is it poisonous?

It must be assumed so. No analysis concerns the species, but the cycads studied contain serious toxins, especially in their seeds.
