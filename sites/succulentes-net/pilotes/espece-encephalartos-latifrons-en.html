Encephalartos latifrons, the Albany cycad, is a tree-forming cycad endemic to the Eastern Cape of South Africa, where it grows on quartzite outcrops in the former Albany and Bathurst districts. This species of the genus Encephalartos has the broadest leaflets in the genus: dark green, glossy and armed with sharp triangular lobes, on leaves whose tips curl backwards. Lehmann described it in 1837 (the part appeared in 1838), and Miquel later reduced it to a variety of Encephalartos horridus, as Encephalartos horridus var. latifrons. It is one of the most threatened cycads in Africa: listed as Critically Endangered, it was down to about 45 wild plants in 2015 according to South Africa's scientific authority, and its best-known collection, at Kirstenbosch, was raided by thieves in 2014.

Key facts about Encephalartos latifrons

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos latifrons Lehm. (1837, issued 1838), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15)
  • Former names and synonyms: Encephalartos horridus var. latifrons (Lehm.) Miq. (1842)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Albany cycad (Species+, SANBI)
  • Natural range: South Africa, Eastern Cape (former Albany, Bathurst and Uitenhage districts), at about 115 to 725 m
  • IUCN status: Critically Endangered (CR A2acd; C1+2a(i)), Red List 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I since 4 February 1977; Annex A in the European Union; negative EU opinion on imports from South Africa, including artificially propagated plants, in force since 7 February 2013; wild collection banned in South Africa; species-specific Biodiversity Management Plan (2011)
  • Documented hardiness: no dated trial in cultivation; the habitat has on average about five frost days a year according to one study, none according to PlantZAfrica (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: full sun; in the wild, quartzite outcrops in fynbos and scrub; in cultivation, a sharply drained soil
  • Habit and adult size: trunk up to about 3 m tall and 30 to 45 cm across, usually branching at the base; leaves 0.9 to 1.5 m; median leaflets up to about 14 by 6 cm
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Kew (BGCI); Montgomery Botanical Center (accession of 2019), Ganna Walska Lotusland, the Huntington and others in the United States; Royal Botanic Garden Sydney and Adelaide in Australia
  • Toxicity: not studied for the species; seeds of other Encephalartos are poisonous (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos latifrons

Look for exceptionally broad, ovate, dark green and shiny leaflets whose lower margin carries a few deep, sharp triangular lobes, often twisted out of the plane of the leaflet, on stiff leaves with a recurved tip. The most detailed description is still that of M. P. Henderson's revision (1945), drawn up from the living collection at Kirstenbosch:

  • Trunk: erect; the tallest plant at Kirstenbosch measured 8 to 9 feet (about 2.4 to 2.7 m) in 1945; Palmer and Pitman (1972) speak of trunks over 3 m, sometimes branched at the base; PlantZAfrica gives stems up to 3 m tall and 30 to 45 cm in diameter, usually branched at the base, with several stems and suckers
  • Leaves: finely hairy when young, then hairless, dark green and rigid, 90 to 100 cm long according to Henderson (1 to 1.5 m according to PlantZAfrica), the tip more or less recurved, sometimes rolled right back; petiole up to 20 cm, its thick swollen base surrounded by a conspicuous whitish collar
  • Leaflets: overlapping, ovate to ovate-lanceolate, the median ones up to about 14 cm long and 6 cm wide, ending in a short dark brown spine; the lower margin usually carries up to four deep, pungent triangular lobes, the upper margin is entire or has one or two lobes; the leaflets at the base of the leaf are reduced, but not to prickles
  • Male cone: the only one seen in 1945 was solitary, oblong-cylindrical, about 50 cm tall and 16 to 17 cm across, almost stalkless and bluish green
  • Female cone: solitary, oblong, stalkless, about 60 cm tall and 23 cm across, green
  • Seeds: red, about 5 cm long and 2 to 2.5 cm across

PlantZAfrica describes one to three olive-green cones on short, stout stalks, the male cylindrical and the female barrel-shaped, produced in midsummer and not every year. In the wild, the dead leaves hang on the trunk for a long time and form a characteristic “skirt” below the crown (Swart and colleagues, 2019; PlantZAfrica). Swart and colleagues also rank the species among the slowest-growing cycads, with one of the longest intervals between two coning events, after Kemp (1986).

Hybrids

No natural hybrid of Encephalartos latifrons is reported. In cultivation, however, plants circulate under hybrid names: a grower in Ravenna reported on the Italian Tropicamente forum that he germinated, in 2013, seed sold as Encephalartos arenarius × Encephalartos latifrons; members of the French Fous de palmiers forum grow plants sold as Encephalartos latifrons × Encephalartos transvenosus, and Jungle Music, a cycad nursery in Encinitas, California, also offers Encephalartos transvenosus × latifrons hybrids. The parentage of such plants cannot be checked: only the record of a serious nursery and, eventually, the look of the adult plant allow a judgement. In collections where several species cone together, hand pollination with pollen of the same species is the only way to obtain pure seed, which PlantZAfrica gives as one of the reasons for pollinating by hand at Kirstenbosch.

Possible confusion

The oldest confusion is with Encephalartos horridus, another Eastern Cape species with lobed leaflets, and several old synonyms of Encephalartos latifrons stem from it. Henderson (1945) nevertheless stresses that the two species differ clearly in general appearance, leaves and cones: Encephalartos horridus has very glaucous foliage and stays much smaller, whereas Encephalartos latifrons forms a trunk and keeps dark green, glossy leaves. PlantZAfrica even suggests growing the two side by side for the contrast between grey and dark green foliage.

The second confusion is with Encephalartos arenarius, whose leaflets are also broad and lobed. Palmer and Pitman (1972) point out that it grows in dune thicket in the Alexandria district and never reaches the size of a tree, and Swart and colleagues (2019) also note that the broad leaflets of Encephalartos latifrons often cause it to be mistaken for Encephalartos arenarius. With young plants the distinction is harder still: the Ravenna grower recalled in 2012 that a seedling bought under this name may look like other species, and that it takes several years to be sure of its identity.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos latifrons is an accepted name in The World List of Cycads. Lehmann published it in the Tijdschrift voor Natuurlijke Geschiedenis en Physiologie (volume 4, part 4, p. 424, plate 9), dated 1837 but issued in 1838; no type specimen is cited, and plate 9 is given as the probable lectotype. IPNI cites the same publication (“iv. (1837-38) 424”). In 1842 Miquel reduced it to a variety of Encephalartos horridus in his Monographia Cycadearum; that name is now a synonym, and no recent study has questioned the rank of species.

The variability of the species is poorly known. Alongside the “typical Encephalartos latifrons” from Bathurst, Henderson (1945) described two Kirstenbosch plants he called “Encephalartos sp. aff. latifrons”: their leaves were longer and glaucous, their leaflets narrower, more widely spaced and less lobed, and the male cone of one of them differed slightly. He concluded that the species is probably variable and that more collections were needed to decide. These plants never received a name of their own. In the nursery trade, Jungle Music distinguishes two forms it calls “Trappes Valley”, with broad, heavily lobed, stacked leaflets and shorter, more curled leaves, and “Green Hills”, with smaller, more widely spaced leaflets and longer, duller leaves; the nursery finds the second a better grower. These are commercial names, not botanical taxa.

Etymology and history

The epithet latifrons comes from the Latin latus, “broad”, and frons, “frond” or “leaf”, referring to the broad leaflets (Haynes, 2022, cited by The World List of Cycads). The genus name Encephalartos, coined by the same Lehmann, is explained on the genus page.

The history of the species is bound up with the Kirstenbosch National Botanical Garden in Cape Town. According to PlantZAfrica, Harold Pearson, the garden's first director, wrote in 1916 that the species “appears to be on the verge of extinction”, being known from only two localities where the plants were by then very hard to find; he had collected 19 plants, which he gave to Kirstenbosch in 1913. Reports by Pearson and by Chamberlain in 1919 suggest that part of the habitat had already been lost to farming. In 1945, Henderson described at Kirstenbosch “a very fine collection of this rare species”, raised from plants from Bathurst and Grahamstown, and followed the development of a female cone: it appeared at the end of February 1944, reached its full size in September and began to break up at the end of November.

Today the Kirstenbosch collection, the largest outside the wild, holds 32 adult plants and is the flagship of the garden's cycad collection; a genetic study (da Silva and colleagues, 2012) showed that it contains genotypes no longer present in the wild (PlantZAfrica). That value made it a target. According to the Global Initiative Against Transnational Organized Crime (2021), thieves took 24 Encephalartos latifrons from the garden over two rainy nights in August 2014, together worth more than 65,000 US dollars; the most valuable specimen has since been protected by a cage.

Common names

In English the species is called the Albany cycad, after the former Albany district; the name appears in Species+ and in SANBI's documents. Palmer and Pitman (1972) give an older English name formed on a term now regarded as offensive in South Africa, which is no longer used. No name in Afrikaans or in an African language is documented.

In the wild

Encephalartos latifrons lives only in the Eastern Cape. Henderson (1945) knew the typical form only from the Uitenhage and Bathurst divisions (near Paardepoort and in the Trappes Valley); Palmer and Pitman (1972) place it in the Bathurst and Albany districts, in thicket on rocky slopes, and PlantZAfrica on rocky outcrops and hills among scrub. According to Swart and colleagues (2019), the plants grow in groups on quartzite outcrops, in Suurberg quartzite fynbos, with trees such as Oldenburgia grandis and Loxostylis alata, sometimes at the edge of forest. The region lies where two biodiversity hotspots meet, the Cape Floristic Region and the Maputaland-Pondoland-Albany hotspot.

The climate is mild. Swart and colleagues describe rainfall spread through the year, peaking in spring (September to November) and autumn (March and April), about 720 to 770 mm a year at the two sites studied, and an average of only five frost days a year in this type of vegetation; PlantZAfrica, for its part, describes hot, fairly dry summers and cool winters without frost. The populations studied lie at 115 to 176 m near the coast and at 480 to 725 m further inland. Fire passes regularly: about every two years at the inland site, at intervals of more than fifteen years at the coastal one.

Male cones shed their pollen from June to August, and the female cone breaks up in December or January, releasing 275 to 790 ovules (Swart and colleagues, 2019). From 2013 to 2017, a population discovered in 2013 showed genuine natural regeneration:

  • 97 plants, 55% of them seedlings, with two females for every male;
  • a heavy coning year in 2014–2015, when 80% of males and 56% of females produced cones;
  • no juvenile or adult plant died during the study, but 21% of the seedlings disappeared, a third of them because of fire; the other stages resprouted about three months after the fires.

This finding contradicts the long-standing statement, still on PlantZAfrica, that the species has had no natural seed set or successful seedlings for more than fifty years. Elsewhere the outlook is less favourable: SANBI notes that the plants cone infrequently and that wild sex ratios are strongly skewed towards males, at about four males to one female.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos latifrons is Critically Endangered (CR). The World List of Cycads cites the 2022 IUCN Red List assessment (Bösenberg), under criteria A2acd; C1+2a(i); the previous assessment (Donaldson, 2010), used by SANBI, also included criterion B2ab(ii,iii,v).

The figures show a long collapse:

  • comparisons of photographs taken between 1906 and 1945 with the situation in 1996, together with plants in collections, show that wild populations lost more than 80% of their numbers in a century (SANBI, 2015; PlantZAfrica);
  • Swart and colleagues (2019) blame first pineapple farming in the Bathurst district, which consumed virgin land until 1957, then collection for gardens from the 1960s: permit records show many adults disappearing between 1960 and 1990;
  • the first survey, in 1991, found only 63 adults in five populations, three of them reduced to a single plant;
  • in 2015 SANBI counted about 45 wild plants, split roughly equally between the two main localities, plus 17 confiscated plants replanted in the wild, of which 14 survived; PlantZAfrica, citing the national Red List, gives an estimate of 60 to 100 wild plants scattered over 9 km², most of them more than 1 km apart, in subpopulations of fewer than 20 plants;
  • only three populations of more than two plants are known (Swart and colleagues, 2019).

The current threats listed by SANBI (2015) are the poaching of plants, the removal of suckers, pollen, seed and cones from the remaining plants, limited bark harvesting, and planned wind farms and a lime quarry, which would make the plants easier to reach. In 2009 one case involved the illegal possession and transport of 25 adults and 151 seedlings; around 30 to 50% of cycads removed from the wild die within a few years. Microchips fitted to all the wild plants have not deterred thieves. Permits issued in 1997 and 2000 allowed artificial pollination and seed collection on condition that half the seedlings went back to the wild, but only 12 plants were reintroduced.

Regulation, according to Species+ (consulted through its public API on 26 October 2026) and SANBI:

  • CITES: Appendix I since 4 February 1977, like the whole genus; commercial international trade in wild plants is prohibited, and artificially propagated plants are treated as Appendix II specimens;
  • European Union: Annex A, current list in Regulation (EU) 2026/1383; the EU Scientific Review Group has issued negative opinions on imports from South Africa, including artificially propagated plants (source codes A and D), on 3 December 2010, 20 June 2011 and 7 February 2013, the last still in force; a new consultation covering the whole genus has been open since 16 September 2026;
  • South Africa: collection of wild cycads banned since February 2007 (TOPS regulations, replaced by Government Notice No. 371 of May 2012); listed in Schedule 3 (endangered flora) of the Cape Nature Conservation Ordinance No. 19 of 1974; a species-specific Biodiversity Management Plan, the first for a South African cycad according to PlantZAfrica, was published in June 2011 (Government Notice No. 416 of 2011);
  • exports: 1,125 specimens left South Africa between 1980 and the end of 2011, 83% of them after 1995; SANBI considers this trade detrimental and only allows seedlings to be exported from nurseries registered with CITES.

In practice, an Encephalartos latifrons can only be bought with CITES and national documents proving that it was propagated in cultivation. These notes do not replace the official texts: check Species+ and EUR-Lex before any purchase or import.

Growing Encephalartos latifrons

Encephalartos latifrons is a collector's plant, rare and very slow, for which few cultivation reports have been published. The best guidance comes from Kirstenbosch: according to PlantZAfrica, the species does well in cultivation in well-drained soil and full sun, with a cool winter without frost, but not in subtropical climates, whose high humidity and warmer winters probably do not suit it. Over 25 years, the plants of the Kirstenbosch collection have produced between one and seven cone crops and eight to fourteen flushes of new leaves each, which shows how slowly they grow.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

PlantZAfrica gives the routine used at Kirstenbosch:

  • position: full sun; large plants with a stem of 1 m or more make fine garden specimens, for instance as accent plants above lower species such as Encephalartos horridus;
  • soil: well drained, with a 50 mm mulch of well-rotted compost applied in autumn;
  • feeding: in early spring, a mixture of bone meal, organic fertiliser and a balanced inorganic fertiliser for each plant;
  • watering: regular, but never with high-pressure irrigation, whose strong jet destroys the leaves and the stem of cycads.

Jungle Music writes, on an undated page, that the species wants full sun on the coast and inland in southern California, partial sun or filtered light in desert areas, and a soil that drains well. Habitat data point the same way: open quartzite outcrops, sharp drainage. A member of the French Fous de palmiers forum advised in 2013 not to expose a young seedling too much at first, then to move it into full sun so that the crown stays compact. The Ravenna grower considered in 2012 that good feeding and good drainage help a plant that dislikes humid climates; this is a grower's opinion, not a trial. No in-ground planting in Europe is documented.

Container cultivation

Young plants make ideal container plants, and PlantZAfrica notes that a seedling becomes a fine specimen in about fifteen years. Use a sharply drained, mineral mix, water regularly in the growing season and feed once a year; keep the plant dry and frost-free in winter. In Europe the species is known only in pots: in March 2009 an Italian collector, who did not give his location, wrote on the Tropicamente forum that a plant bought in South Africa at great expense had died, without saying why, and a grower near Toulouse, in south-west France, wrote in August 2026 on the Fous de palmiers forum that he did not intend to leave his plants sold as Encephalartos latifrons × Encephalartos transvenosus outdoors in winter.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In Britain, the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, declares the species to BGCI PlantSearch; no other British garden does. In the United States, Montgomery Botanical Center in Coral Gables, Florida, holds one accession received in 2019 according to its number; Ganna Walska Lotusland names Encephalartos latifrons among the notable species of its cycad garden; the Huntington, the Los Angeles County Arboretum, the Chicago Botanic Garden, the San Diego Botanic Garden, the San Diego Zoo and the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley declare it to BGCI. In Australia, the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney lists it in its online catalogue, and the Botanic Garden of South Australia in Adelaide declares it to BGCI. In Italy, a study published in 1997 in Delpinoa examined a plant of South African origin then grown at the Naples Botanical Garden; later publications of the garden no longer mention it. A BGCI listing shows that a garden has declared the species at some point, not that a plant is alive today. Jungle Music describes the species as among the most expensive cycads in the world, rarely available because seed is so scarce.

Propagation

Encephalartos latifrons is propagated mainly from seed, by hand pollination, because its natural pollinators are absent in cultivation; plants of the coastal population have been pollinated artificially since the 1980s (Swart and colleagues, 2019). The seeds need several months for the embryo to complete its development before they germinate (Giddy, 1974).

At Kirstenbosch, where the species has been grown since 1913, germination long stayed below 10%. PlantZAfrica reports that the garden's research team solved the problem in 2014:

  • pollinate dry rather than wet, in the afternoon, when pollination drops are most abundant, since a master's study showed that wet pollination harms embryo development and germination;
  • after the cone breaks up naturally, dust the seeds with a fungicide powder and store them for 12 months in fairly dry conditions;
  • sow in summer on a bed of clean sand with bottom heat of 25 to 28 °C (77 to 82.4 °F): germination starts within five weeks, with the radicle first, then the leaves;
  • the following spring, pot the seedlings into 3-litre sleeves of a well-drained mix and water sparingly until they are established.

This protocol gives more than 50% germination. According to SANBI (2015), seedlings are almost impossible to find in South Africa, as traders prefer to wait for the plants to grow before selling them at a high price; about 3,000 seedlings raised from seed harvested without permits since 2006 were awaiting regularisation under the management plan, and 20% had died for lack of care.

The species also produces suckers at the base of the trunk. PlantZAfrica describes their removal at Kirstenbosch: choose well-developed suckers at least 250 mm in diameter, remove all their leaves, pull them carefully away from the main stem, let the wound dry, then root them in clean sand before planting out. SANBI recalls that suckers play a minor role in the regeneration of cycads and that those left behind after a plant has been dug up sometimes survive. A sucker may only be taken from a legally held plant.

Pests and diseases

Few data exist for Encephalartos latifrons itself. Swart and colleagues (2019) observed a female whose three cones were invaded by an unidentified fungus: they blackened without breaking up and their seeds were malformed and sterile, but the plant survived. In the wild, fire kills some of the young seedlings. In cultivation, PlantZAfrica mentions:

  • scale insects and mealybugs on the underside of the leaves, treated at Kirstenbosch with a systemic insecticide;
  • snout beetles attracted by coning plants, which destroy the seed;
  • a tiny mite that damages new leaves, against which Kirstenbosch applies a contact insecticide every two weeks while the leaves emerge.

Cold hardiness

The cold hardiness of Encephalartos latifrons is not documented: no dated in-ground trial has been published anywhere. Its almost frost-free habitat and its poor performance in subtropical climates suggest a plant for mild, dry winters, but no measured value allows a figure.

Mediterranean and European experience

No outdoor planting of Encephalartos latifrons is documented in France or Italy, on the coast or inland. On the Tropicamente forum, the Ravenna grower placed it in 2012 among the broad, green-leaved Encephalartos, which he considered less cold-tolerant; this is an opinion, with no observation behind it. In France the only plants mentioned are hybrids that their owners do not leave outdoors in winter. As in other Encephalartos, a young plant whose caudex is still underground benefits from the thermal inertia of the soil, whereas an adult with an aerial trunk does not: any tolerance seen in seedlings cannot be extended to large plants.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No dated cold report has been published from these countries, and no botanical source gives a USDA zone for the species; the zone ratings found on some websites cite no measurement. The only reference points are those of the habitat: about five frost days a year on average according to Swart and colleagues (2019), citing Rebelo and colleagues, and no frost at all according to PlantZAfrica, at low and middle altitude. Under a temperate climate, the prudent course is a pot kept frost-free and dry in winter, in a cool, bright glasshouse in Britain; outdoors, only largely frost-free gardens with dry winters, such as coastal southern California or the milder parts of Australia, offer conditions close to those of the habitat.

Toxicity and precautions

No study has dealt with the toxicity of Encephalartos latifrons, but it should not be regarded as harmless. The toxicity of Encephalartos was demonstrated for Encephalartos horridus as early as 1944 (Van der Walt), and Encephalartos contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; these substances affect the liver, the digestive tract and the nervous system, and the seeds are the most dangerous part. The ASPCA reports vomiting, diarrhoea, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses that have eaten cycads; dogs that chew the seeds are the most exposed.

Wear gloves to clean seeds, keep plants and seeds out of reach of children and pets, and if any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use specific to Encephalartos latifrons is described. SANBI (2015) only mentions limited bark harvesting from wild plants, without saying what it is for; at the level of the genus, cycad bark is sold for South African traditional medicine (muthi), according to the Global Initiative (2021).

Frequently asked questions

How many Encephalartos latifrons are left in the wild?

A few dozen adults. SANBI counted about 45 wild plants in 2015, in two main localities, plus 14 replanted confiscated plants; PlantZAfrica gives 60 to 100. A population discovered in 2013, with 97 plants, most of them seedlings, regenerates naturally (Swart and colleagues, 2019).

Why is Encephalartos latifrons so endangered?

Because small populations were first destroyed by farming, then plundered by collectors. Pineapple farming took part of the habitat in the twentieth century, and the removal of plants, suckers, cones and seed continues. The species cones rarely and wild populations are dominated by males, which limits regeneration.

What is the difference between Encephalartos latifrons and Encephalartos horridus?

Mainly habit and colour. Encephalartos horridus stays small and has very glaucous, blue-grey leaves; Encephalartos latifrons forms a trunk and has dark green, glossy leaves with broader leaflets. Both have lobed, spiny leaflets, hence the old confusions.

Can Encephalartos latifrons be confused with Encephalartos arenarius?

Yes, especially when young. Both have broad, lobed leaflets. Encephalartos arenarius grows in the dunes of Alexandria and never becomes tree-like; Encephalartos latifrons grows on quartzite rocks and forms a trunk up to about 3 m. A seedling without a reliable origin can remain hard to identify for years.

Is Encephalartos latifrons frost-hardy?

Nobody knows, and it is unlikely. No trial has been published, its habitat has few or no frost days, and Kirstenbosch recommends a cool but frost-free winter. Grow it in a pot in a very free-draining mix and keep it frost-free and dry in winter.

How fast does Encephalartos latifrons grow?

Very slowly. Over 25 years, the Kirstenbosch plants produced only eight to fourteen flushes of leaves each, although a seedling can make a fine specimen in about fifteen years with good care.

Can I buy Encephalartos latifrons legally?

Yes, but only a plant propagated in cultivation, with its documents. The species is in CITES Appendix I and in Annex A in the European Union, and the EU opinion on imports from South Africa, even of artificially propagated plants, is negative. Ask for the certificates and check the current rules before buying.
