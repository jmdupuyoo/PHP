Zamia oligodonta is a cycad of the Colombian Andes, endemic to the department of Risaralda on the Western Cordillera, where it grows in premontane rainforest between 1,500 and 1,800 m. This species of the genus Zamia, described in 2003 from a few cultivated plants, has a stem that is almost always underground, one to three large leaves and broad pleated leaflets with deep grooves between the veins. Placed in the synonymy of Zamia montana in 2009, it was reinstated in 2015; the IUCN lists it as Critically Endangered.

  • Natural range: Colombia, department of Risaralda (Western Cordillera), 1,500 to 1,800 m

How to recognise Zamia oligodonta

It is recognised by its broad, elliptic leaflets, pleated and almost wavy, whose veins form grooves on the upper surface and stand out strongly beneath, ending in a long acuminate to tail-like point, carried by only one to three leaves on a stem that is usually buried. The petiole bears a few scattered prickles.

Two descriptions exist. The original description (Calderón-Sáenz and Stevenson, 2003), read in full, is based on a few cultivated plants: an underground stem 6 to 12 cm in diameter, leaves 55 to 125 cm long carrying (8) 10 to 15 (17) leaflets 20 to 40 cm long and 7 to 13 cm wide, pleated, with grooves 2 to 3 mm wide between the veins, one to three large irregular teeth up to 5 mm long near the tip, and a slightly curved point. The young leaves are described there as golden. The revision by Calonje and colleagues (2015), based on wild plants, showed that these reach far larger sizes:

  • Stem: usually underground, rarely tree-like, up to 40 cm long and 11 cm across, upright or reclining, usually solitary but sometimes branched.
  • Leaves: 1 to 3 at a time, straight to slightly arching, up to 2.90 m long; petiole up to 1.62 m, with a few prickles up to 4 mm long; rachis up to 1.31 m, carrying 2 to 26 opposite or nearly opposite leaflets.
  • Leaflets: narrowly elliptic to elliptic, straight or barely sickle-shaped, with an acuminate to tail-like tip, thin to somewhat leathery; the median ones measure 20 to 50 cm by 6.6 to 14 cm. New leaves emerge covered in white down, soon turn pale yellow-green, then bronze, before becoming a glossy bright green.
  • Male cones: 1 to 3 per stem, 7 to 11 cm by 2 to 2.7 cm, yellow-green turning beige-yellow.
  • Female cones: one per stem, upright, ellipsoid, up to 17 cm by 11 cm, red-brown when they emerge, then brown and light tan when ripe.
  • Seeds: ovoid-pyramidal, 3.5 to 4.2 cm by 2.2 to 2.8 cm, with a sarcotesta (fleshy seed coat) that turns from pink to orange.

The name of the species comes from these few teeth near the tip of the leaflets, which the original description presented as the distinguishing character. The 2015 revision qualifies this: the margin is mostly entire, more or less wavy at the end, sometimes so much that it looks broadly toothed (as on the holotype), but the character is very variable and also occurs on some leaflets of Zamia montana; on its own it is not enough to identify the species.

Hybrids

No hybrid, natural or horticultural, has been documented.

Possible confusion

The most direct confusion is with Zamia montana, from Antioquia, with which it was united from 2009 to 2015. Zamia montana has a tree-like trunk, up to ten leaves and obovate leaflets with veins only slightly raised beneath; Zamia oligodonta has a stem that is almost always underground, no more than three leaves, and elliptic, wavy, long-acuminate leaflets with veins strongly raised beneath (Calonje and colleagues, 2015).

The original description also compared it with other Zamia with pleated or acuminate leaflets:

  • Zamia amplifolia, from the Western Cordillera, has even thicker, more symmetrical and more elliptic leaflets, with neither teeth nor an acuminate tip, and grooves twice as wide;
  • Zamia skinneri, from Panama, also has pleated, acuminate leaflets, toothed at the end, but with a point that is not curved, more teeth, and a much sturdier habit, with a tree-like stem up to 4 m tall;
  • Zamia cunaria, from Panama, and Zamia amazonum lack these few large teeth below the tip, and their leaflets are not pleated;
  • Zamia purpurea, from Mexico, also has an underground stem and acuminate leaflets, but no more than five pairs of leaflets, purple when they emerge and regularly toothed in their upper third.

The authors then counted eight Zamia with pleated leaves: Zamia oligodonta, Zamia wallisii, Zamia montana, Zamia roezlii, Zamia skinneri, Zamia amplifolia, Zamia dressleri and Zamia neurophyllidia.

Taxonomy

The World List of Cycads and GBIF accept the name Zamia oligodonta; POWO, the site’s nomenclatural authority, could not be consulted directly. The species was described in 2003 by Eduardo Calderón-Sáenz, of the Alexander von Humboldt Institute in Bogotá, and Dennis W. Stevenson, of the New York Botanical Garden, in the Revista de la Academia Colombiana de Ciencias Exactas, Físicas y Naturales (27(105): 485–490). The authors, who could not name a plant from Risaralda with the key of the Flora de Colombia (Stevenson, 2001), also published a revised key to the Zamia of Colombia in the same paper.

The holotype (Calderón-Sáenz 174) is a male plant bearing a cone, grown by Eduardo Calderón-Sáenz and collected in October 1999; it came from a plant found in Risaralda, in sub-Andean forest. It is kept in the herbarium of the Humboldt Institute (FMB), at Villa de Leyva. The exact locality was deliberately kept secret, to prevent plundering by unscrupulous traders. The “El Refugio-Torremolinos reserve” sometimes given as the place where the type was grown does not appear in the original description.

In 2009 A.J. Lindström placed the species in the synonymy of Zamia montana (Taxon 58(1): 265–270; article not consulted, so his arguments are not given here). In 2014 Michael Calonje, Gustavo Morales, Cristina López-Gallego and Francisco Javier Roldán studied both species in the wild; their revision, published in Phytotaxa in 2015, separated them again. The World List of Cycads follows this revision, but Species+, the CITES database, still treated Zamia oligodonta as a synonym of Zamia montana in 2026.

Etymology and history

The epithet comes from the Greek roots oligo-, “few”, and odonto-, “tooth”, alluding to the few teeth near the tip of each leaflet (original description; Haynes, 2022, as given by The World List of Cycads). As seen above, the 2015 revision considers this character inconsistent.

When the species was described, several sterile plants had been seen at the type locality, but only two cultivated plants had become fertile, one with a male cone and the other with a female cone. The fieldwork of 2014 revealed wild plants much larger than these cultivated ones.

Common names

No vernacular name is attested, in English or in Spanish.

In the wild

According to the 2015 revision, the species is endemic to the department of Risaralda, where it grows in premontane rainforest on the Western Cordillera, between 1,500 and 1,800 m (one specimen collected in 2014 came from between 1,750 and 1,800 m). The climate, estimated by modelling (WorldClim), brings 2,600 to 2,700 mm of rain a year, with two peaks (October–November and April–May) and drier months from January to March and in July–August; the mean annual temperature is 18.8 °C (65.8 °F), with values between 13.6 and 24.3 °C (56.5 and 75.7 °F).

At the end of March 2014 developing female cones and young male cones were seen; ripe seeds have been reported in October. The male cones were completely rotten by early July, which places pollen release around April–May. No herbivore or pollinator has yet been observed on the species.

Conservation status and regulation

Zamia oligodonta is listed as Critically Endangered (CR) by the IUCN, under criterion B1ab(iii), in the assessment by Cristina López-Gallego and Michael Calonje published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads); GBIF gives the same category (the Red List itself could not be consulted).

The 2015 revision painted a slightly less gloomy picture than for Zamia montana: five subpopulations, about 1,000 adults, an extent of occurrence of about 30 km² and an estimated area of occupancy of 5 km². Regeneration seemed good, with plants of all ages, and fertile female cones were seen in two populations; one population lies in a protected area of Risaralda, and the region still keeps good forest cover. Cleared areas of suitable habitat nevertheless suggest that the species is declining. The main threat is habitat destruction for pasture and crops, above all coffee and sugar cane. Rated Data Deficient in the Colombian red list of cycads (Galeano and colleagues, 2005, as cited by the revision), it should, according to the authors, have been moved to Endangered (EN), criteria B1ab(i-v)+2ab(i-v); C1. The 2022 global assessment is more severe (CR). The exact localities were not published, to limit illegal collecting.

Like all species of the genus except Zamia restrepoi, Zamia oligodonta falls under CITES Appendix II, where Zamia have been listed since 4 February 1977. International trade in the plants is therefore allowed, but requires an export permit from the country of origin. The listing carries annotation #4: seeds, pollen, seedlings and tissue cultures obtained in vitro and transported in sterile containers are not covered by CITES. In the European Union the species falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, whose current list is set by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026.

Species+ covers the species under the name Zamia montana, which it treats as a synonym; the status is the same. Growing Zamia oligodonta

No growing guide or grower’s report has been published. All that is known is that the species was described from plants grown in Colombia by one of its authors, plants that stayed much smaller than wild ones.

The French specialist nursery Cycadales.eu (2023) places Zamia oligodonta among the cloud-forest Zamia, describing it as “rare and difficult to grow”. For this group, for lack of data, it suggests as a starting point a compost that always drains well, neutral to acid, water that is also neutral to acid, and temperatures between 20 and 30 °C (68 and 86 °F), avoiding extremes. These are indications for a group of species, not a trial on this one.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

No outdoor cultivation has been reported anywhere outside its native range. Its habitat suggests that only a cool, humid, frost-free tropical highland climate could suit it; this is a deduction, not a result.

Container cultivation

In the UK and other temperate climates it could only be attempted under glass, in a humid house kept between about 20 and 30 °C (68 and 86 °F), as Cycadales.eu suggests for the group, in an acid, free-draining compost. The large leaves of wild plants, up to 2.90 m long, call for a large container and plenty of headroom; no grower has reported such an attempt.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In short: no record of the species in British, American or Australian gardens.

  • United Kingdom, United States and Australia: no record in BGCI, in the online catalogue of Montgomery Botanical Center (no match on 24 October 2026) or in the online catalogues of British, American and Australian gardens (Fairchild, Edinburgh, Cambridge, Sydney, Melbourne and others) checked on 19 October 2026.
  • Elsewhere: the only declaration to BGCI comes from the Jardín Botánico de Bogotá José Celestino Mutis, in Colombia (consulted on 24 October 2026).

A BGCI declaration shows that a garden held the plant at some point, not that it is alive today.

Propagation

Like all Zamia, the species is dioecious: a male and a female plant are needed, and hand pollination in cultivation. The seeds, ovoid-pyramidal and 3.5 to 4.2 cm long, have been known since 2015, as have the first leaves of the seedlings, which carry two narrowly elliptic leaflets; no germination data have been published.

Pests and diseases

No pest or disease is documented, in the wild or in cultivation. For the Zamia of wet habitats as a whole, Cycadales.eu warns of root rot in a compost that is too wet in cool weather.

Cold hardiness

There are no cold-hardiness data for Zamia oligodonta. Its habitat is cool (mean annual temperature of 18.8 °C, or 65.8 °F, with modelled values between 13.6 and 24.3 °C, or 56.5 and 75.7 °F), but no source reports frost in its range or any cold trial: any hardiness figure would be invented.

Mediterranean and European experience

No in-ground cultivation has been reported in France, and no dated frost episode concerns it. No cultivation is documented in France or Italy.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No growing report is documented in any of these countries, nor anywhere else, in the horticultural literature.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity study specific to Zamia oligodonta has been published. The risk is, however, documented in the genus and in other cycads: Zamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, and for Zamia furfuracea the ASPCA cites cycasin and BMAA, a neurotoxic amino acid, with vomiting, bloody diarrhoea and liver damage in dogs, cats and horses; the seeds are the most dangerous part. In the absence of evidence to the contrary, treat the plant as poisonous: keep seeds and leaves out of reach of children and animals, and wear gloves to clean seeds. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet straight away.

Traditional uses

No use is documented for this species.

Frequently asked questions

Where does Zamia oligodonta grow?

Only in the department of Risaralda, in Colombia, on the Western Cordillera, in premontane rainforest between 1,500 and 1,800 m.

What does “oligodonta” mean?

“With few teeth”, after the few large teeth near the tip of the leaflets. The 2015 revision showed that this character is variable and not enough to identify the species.

Is it the same plant as Zamia montana?

No. The two were united from 2009 to 2015, but Zamia montana has a tree-like trunk and up to ten leaves, whereas Zamia oligodonta has a stem that is almost always underground and no more than three leaves. CITES nevertheless still treats them as a single species.

Is it endangered?

Yes. The IUCN lists it as Critically Endangered (2022); in 2015 about 1,000 adults were counted in five subpopulations, threatened by pasture, coffee and sugar cane.

Can I grow it in the UK?

Nothing is documented. A specialist nursery describes it as rare and difficult, to be kept between 20 and 30 °C (68 and 86 °F) in a free-draining, acid compost, which in the UK means a heated, humid glasshouse.

Is it poisonous?

It must be assumed so. No analysis exists for the species, but the cycads studied contain serious toxins, especially in their seeds.
