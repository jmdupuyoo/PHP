Macrozamia lucida is a cycad with no visible trunk from south-eastern Queensland, in eastern Australia, found from the Brisbane area north to Nambour and in the far north-east of New South Wales, where it grows in the understorey of coastal wet sclerophyll forest. It belongs to the genus Macrozamia and takes its name from its dark green, highly glossy leaves. Modest in the garden, it is famous among biologists: it is the species on which pollination of a cycad by thrips, tiny insects that live in its male cones, was demonstrated and measured. In Queensland it was long mislabelled Macrozamia spiralis, a New South Wales species.

Key facts about Macrozamia lucida

  • Accepted name: Macrozamia lucida L.A.S.Johnson (1959), published in the Proceedings of the Linnean Society of New South Wales, ser. 2, 84(1): 102, accepted by The World List of Cycads and the Australian Plant Census
  • Former names and synonyms: no synonym; the name Macrozamia spiralis was often misapplied to this plant in Queensland (Johnson, 1959; PACSOA)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: “Pineapple Zamia” (Flora of Australia, Australian Plant Census)
  • Natural range: Australia, south-eastern Queensland (Brisbane to Nambour) and the far north-east of New South Wales (Border Ranges)
  • IUCN status: Least Concern (LC), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated August 2020 and published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union; no listing under Australian threatened-species law (Atlas of Living Australia)
  • Documented hardiness: no measured value and no cultivation report with a temperature; ranked among the least hardy Macrozamia by an Australian specialist, according to a forum post (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, the understorey of coastal wet sclerophyll forest; in cultivation, a pot on a patio or a rockery (PACSOA), free-draining soil
  • Habit and adult size: underground stem 8 to 20 cm across; 2 to 15 leaves (up to 40), 70 to 120 cm long; a clump about 1.5 m tall and 1.5 to 2 m wide (PACSOA)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: grown at the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney since before 1959 (Johnson) and at Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria (29 plants); declared to BGCI by 22 institutions, including RHS Garden Wisley and Montgomery Botanical Center
  • Toxicity: leaves toxic to cattle (nervous disorders documented in 1968); seeds toxic as throughout the genus (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Macrozamia lucida

It is recognised by its broad, dark green, very glossy leaves on a long spine-free petiole, and by its broad, non-pungent leaflets with a large white callus at the base (Flora of Australia, Hill, 1998). Unless stated otherwise, the measurements below come from that flora.

  • Stem: underground, 8 to 20 cm across; the crown stays at ground level.
  • Leaves: 2 to 15 per crown, exceptionally up to 40, 70 to 120 cm long, slightly keeled to flat in section, dark green and highly glossy. The rachis is not to moderately twisted; the PACSOA stresses that it is almost straight in the adult leaf.
  • Petiole: 25 to 50 cm long, without spines; the lowest leaflets are not reduced to spines.
  • Leaflets: 50 to 120 per leaf, simple, 15 to 35 cm long and 7 to 11 mm wide, much paler beneath, with a blunt tip; the flora of New South Wales notes 5 to 11 prominent veins on the underside.
  • Male cones: spindle-shaped, 12 to 15 cm long and 3.5 to 4 cm across; the PACSOA has seen five to eight on a single plant.
  • Female cones: ovoid, 15 to 20 cm long and 7.5 to 9 cm across, with an apical spine of 6 to 50 mm; usually two per plant, “about the diameter of a closed fist”, with about sixty seeds (PACSOA).
  • Seeds: ovoid, 22 to 25 mm long and 18 to 20 mm wide, with red flesh; the PACSOA describes them as about 3 cm long and dark orange when ripe.

In the original description, Johnson (1959) summed up the species as having long slender petioles, a rachis not or scarcely twisted, and curved, very glossy leaflets with sharply demarcated whitish bases. A grower on the Italian Tropicamente forum noted in 2013 that, at first sight, its arching, shiny leaves recall a Ceratozamia.

Hybrids

No hybrid has been demonstrated. Johnson (1959) mentioned a probable hybrid population derived from Macrozamia lucida and the very different Macrozamia moorei, to be discussed in the Flora of New South Wales then in preparation. Len Butt, author of the PACSOA profile, likewise reports that “a prominent New South Wales botanist” states that hybrids exist between the two species, without a reference or a locality. The Atlas of Living Australia also lists a name “Macrozamia lucida x Macrozamia macleayi”, not examined by taxonomists. These records remain to be confirmed.

Possible confusion

The main confusion is historical: in Queensland, the name Macrozamia spiralis was often applied to Macrozamia lucida.

  • Macrozamia spiralis: a New South Wales species whose name refers to a spirally twisted rachis. Johnson (1959) noted that the error was made in Queensland but not in New South Wales; according to the PACSOA, the name does not fit Macrozamia lucida, whose adult rachis is almost straight. The very glossy leaves and the Queensland origin of Macrozamia lucida settle the matter.
  • Macrozamia johnsonii: old New South Wales collections attributed to Macrozamia lucida were in fact young Macrozamia johnsonii (Flora of Australia). Johnson's only New South Wales record in 1959 came from three miles east of Dalmorton, the home of Macrozamia johnsonii. The leaves of that species have their lowest leaflets reduced to spines, which is never the case in Macrozamia lucida.

Taxonomy

Macrozamia lucida L.A.S.Johnson is accepted by The World List of Cycads and the Australian Plant Census. Lawrence A.S. Johnson described it in 1959 in his revision of the Australian cycads, “The families of cycads and the Zamiaceae of Australia” (Proceedings of the Linnean Society of New South Wales, ser. 2, 84(1): 102–103). He collected the type himself on 13 June 1951 on the southern side of Mount Ngungun, in the Glass House Mountains, at about 400 ft (about 120 m) (holotype NSW 40668, in the herbarium of the Royal Botanic Gardens Sydney). As no good material with cones was available, he chose a vegetative specimen that he had seen living in the field, and described the cones, which he assigned to the species only as probable, following F.M. Bailey (1902).

The name has not changed since. The PACSOA places the species in section Parazamia, which groups the species without an aerial trunk or spiny lower leaflets. The phylogenomic study by Habib et al. (2022), based on 4,740 nuclear genes from 39 species of Macrozamia, concludes however that this traditional division into sections does not reflect the evolutionary history of the genus.

Etymology and history

The epithet comes from the Latin lucidus, “shining”, after the very glossy leaflets (Johnson, 1959; Haynes, 2022, as given by The World List of Cycads). According to the PACSOA, it is one of the “thicket” species observed by Professor Chamberlain early in the twentieth century. Johnson recorded in 1959 that it had been grown in the Sydney Botanic Gardens for many years and kept its distinctive appearance there.

Since the mid-2000s, Macrozamia lucida has become a model species for the study of cycad pollination, through the work of Irene Terry (University of Utah) and her colleagues at the University of Queensland (see Pollination by thrips).

Common names

The Flora of Australia, the Australian Plant Census and the Atlas of Living Australia give “Pineapple Zamia”; according to the PACSOA, it is the name used by local farmers. In Queensland, “zamia” is a common name for the native cycads, not to be confused with the American genus Zamia. No common name is attested in other languages.

In the wild

Macrozamia lucida grows in south-eastern Queensland, from around Brisbane north to Nambour, and in the far north-east of New South Wales (Flora of Australia). It is scattered and sporadic in coastal wet sclerophyll forest. Collections cited by the flora come from Mount Glorious, the Glass House Mountains, Enoggera and the Jimna–Kenilworth road; in New South Wales, the Flora of Australia cites a recent collection from the Border Ranges and another from Pottsville, and PlantNET places it in closed moist forest in the Border Ranges. Johnson (1959) gave its range as the southern coastal region of Queensland from Wide Bay to the Moreton district, “widely spread in hilly country” but never forming dense stands.

The PACSOA, in an older text by Len Butt, extends the range from Hervey Bay west to Carnarvon Gorge, then south to the New South Wales border. This much larger range is not followed by the Flora of Australia, and Carnarvon in particular is not confirmed. The same author notes that the sites he knows are hard for cattle to reach.

Pollination by thrips

Macrozamia lucida depends on insects to reproduce: its pollinators are thrips, Cycadothrips chadwicki, which feed and breed only in the male cones.

In 2005, Terry, Walter, Donaldson, Snow, Forster and Machin confirmed and measured this role in the American Journal of Botany, comparing Macrozamia lucida and its thrips with Macrozamia machinii and its Tranes weevils. Their field and laboratory tests showed that:

  • no seed forms without fertilisation when both wind and insects are kept away from female cones, whereas excluding wind alone does not prevent seed set, because the insects still reach the cone;
  • in enclosure tests, each thrips pollinates on average 2.4 ovules per cone, against 26.2 for a weevil;
  • fluorescent powder carried by the thrips is concentrated around the micropyle of each ovule visited, where the pollination drop is released and where pollen must be deposited, whereas the weevils scatter it over the whole ovule: chance plays a greater part in weevil pollination;
  • a weevil and a group of 25 thrips deliver 6.2 and 5.2 pollen grains respectively to each ovule visited: the pollination potential of 25 thrips roughly equals that of one weevil.

The cones play an active part in this partnership. Every day, for about two weeks, the male cones of Macrozamia lucida and of Macrozamia macleayi warm up around midday, reaching 12 °C (21.6 °F) above the surrounding air in the middle of their active period (Roemer et al., 2008). The heating is weaker and earlier in female cones (Roemer et al., 2005). It goes with scent emissions, more than 90% of them β-myrcene, driven by the rise in respiration (Terry et al., 2016), and its timing is controlled by a circadian clock (Roemer et al., 2022). In 2007, Terry and colleagues described in Science the “push-pull” mechanism of an Australian Macrozamia and its thrips: during the warm phase, scent emissions, in which β-myrcene and ocimene vary, become so strong that pollen-laden thrips leave the male cones en masse; as the heating declines, emissions fall and the cones attract the thrips, which then pollinate the female cones. The abstract of that paper does not name the species.

Finally, Brookes et al. (2015) showed that Cycadothrips chadwicki is in fact a complex of cryptic species, each lineage specialising on one to three Macrozamia species. For the grower the consequence is simple: outside Australia, without these thrips, pollination must be done by hand.

Conservation status and regulation

Macrozamia lucida is not threatened. The IUCN Red List classes it as Least Concern (LC), in the assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated 27 August 2020 and published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, GBIF and Crossref, checked on 26 October 2026). The Atlas of Living Australia, which compiles the national and state threatened-species lists, shows no listing for it.

For international trade, like other Macrozamia, it falls under CITES Appendix II, with annotation #4, which exempts seeds among other items (listing in force on 5 March 2026), and under Annex B in the European Union (Species+, checked on 26 October 2026). Plants sold must come from seed or legal cultivation; check the regulations in force before any purchase or import.

Growing Macrozamia lucida

Macrozamia lucida is easy to grow in a pot but must be protected from cold: no data support growing it in the ground where frosts occur. According to the PACSOA, it “adapts well to cultivation” and suits a pot on a patio as well as a rockery of Australian plants.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Light: not documented for cultivation. In the wild, the species lives in the understorey of wet forest: filtered light or partial shade seem closest to its natural conditions.
  • Soil and water: free-draining. At the Jardin zoologique tropical in La Londe-les-Maures, in the Var (southern France), experience with the genus Macrozamia shows that watering once a week in summer, when there is no rain, is a minimum and twice a week ideal. This Mediterranean experience, gained with other species of the genus, is useful for gardens with a similar climate, such as coastal California or southern Australia.
  • Growth rhythm: a grower in the Hérault and the Ain (France) observed on the Fous de palmiers forum in 2012 that his Macrozamia lucida produces its leaves in flushes, like most cycads, and not continuously like the large Macrozamia of section Macrozamia.
  • Coning in cultivation: a grower in Barcelona (Catalonia, Spain) reported on the same forum in July 2013 that one of his female plants had produced a cone while the male had not, and was looking for pollen; another member grows plants raised from his seed.

Container cultivation

Container growing is the safest course outside the subtropics: a pot on a patio is one of the uses the PACSOA recommends. Use a free-draining mix, water regularly in summer and keep the plant frost-free and on the dry side in winter.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

  • Australia: Johnson (1959) recorded the species as cultivated in the Sydney Botanic Gardens for many years; the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney's Garden Explorer now lists seven plants, from accessions made in 1992 and 1993, most of them of wild origin, in beds 102i and 65i (updated 1 October 2026). The census of Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria, downloaded on 19 October 2026, records 29 plants: 24 at Melbourne, in the cycad collection and in several beds such as the Aloe Bed and the Eastern Lodge, many of wild origin and planted between 2007 and 2014, and 5 at Cranbourne (planted in 2012 and 2024). Brisbane Botanic Gardens Mount Coot-tha, the Australian Botanic Garden Mount Annan, the Australian National Botanic Gardens and the Botanic Garden of South Australia also declare it to BGCI.
  • United States: Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida) holds two accessions in its online catalogue. The Huntington, Lotusland, San Diego Botanic Garden, SeaWorld San Diego, the University of California Botanical Garden at Berkeley and the University of Hawaii at Hilo declare it to BGCI.
  • United Kingdom: RHS Garden Wisley is the only British garden that declares it to BGCI.

Twenty-two institutions in all declare the species to BGCI (checked on 26 October 2026), the others in China, the Czech Republic, Germany, France, the Netherlands, New Zealand and Singapore. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not necessarily a living plant on display today. No English-language grower's report with a date, a place and a temperature has been found.

Propagation

Macrozamia lucida is propagated by seed. According to the PACSOA, seedlings are easily obtained, as the seed is viable if collected fresh. In cultivation, a male and a female plant coning at the same time are needed, with hand pollination; the Barcelona case of 2013 shows that this timing is not guaranteed. Clean the seeds wearing gloves, as the flesh is toxic.

Pests and diseases

No pest or disease specific to the species is documented. In Macrozamia, growers' forums mention scale insects above all, and waterlogged soil encourages root rot.

Cold hardiness

Macrozamia lucida is probably one of the most cold-sensitive Macrozamia, but no value has been measured: there is neither published data nor a cultivation report with a temperature. No source gives a USDA zone for it.

Mediterranean and European experience

No trial in the ground is documented in Europe. In 2012, a grower in the Hérault and the Ain (France) who owns the species judged it “unlikely to stand the cold”. In 2014, a member of the Fous de palmiers forum relayed the ranking of an Australian nurseryman specialising in cycads, who placed Macrozamia lucida among the “not very hardy” species of the Queensland coast, without giving a temperature. A post on the Italian Tropicamente forum (2013) claims that the species “stands low temperatures well”, but gives no place, date or value. Without data, a pot kept frost-free in winter is the safe course.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No result with a temperature has been published. In Britain and in frost-prone parts of the United States, it is a plant for a pot brought under glass in winter.

Toxicity and precautions

Yes, Macrozamia lucida is toxic. Hall and McGavin (1968) described, in the journal Pathologia Veterinaria, the clinical signs and nerve lesions of cattle that had eaten the leaves of Macrozamia lucida or Bowenia serrulata. The PACSOA notes, however, that references to its toxicity to stock are few, as its sites are hard for cattle to reach. Macrozamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a powerful toxin of the liver (Healy, 1967). Keep the plant and its seeds out of reach of children, dogs and cats, wear gloves to clean seeds and, in case of ingestion, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No use specific to Macrozamia lucida has been documented. Asmussen's study (2011) of the consumption of Macrozamia seeds by Aboriginal people does not mention this species.

Frequently asked questions

Why is it called Macrozamia lucida?

Because its leaves shine. The epithet comes from the Latin lucidus, “shining”, and refers to the dark green, very glossy leaflets that set the species apart from other Macrozamia.

What pollinates Macrozamia lucida?

Thrips. Cycadothrips chadwicki lives in the male cones and carries the pollen to the female cones; without it, no seed forms in the wild. In 2005, Terry and colleagues calculated that one thrips pollinates on average 2.4 ovules per cone, with great precision.

Do the cones really heat up?

Yes. The male cones warm up every day around midday, to as much as 12 °C (21.6 °F) above the surrounding air, for about two weeks. The heating releases scents that first drive the thrips out of the male cones, then draw them to the female cones as it declines.

Is Macrozamia lucida frost-hardy?

Probably very little, but nobody has measured it. No report with a temperature exists, and an Australian specialist ranks it among the least hardy Macrozamia. Grow it in a pot and keep it frost-free in winter.

Is it the same plant as Macrozamia spiralis?

No. The name Macrozamia spiralis was often misapplied to Macrozamia lucida in Queensland, but it belongs to another species, from New South Wales, with a twisted rachis. The rachis of Macrozamia lucida is almost straight and its leaves are very glossy.

Is Macrozamia lucida poisonous?

Yes. Its leaves have caused nervous disorders in cattle, and its seeds are toxic like those of all Macrozamia. Keep it out of reach of children and pets.
