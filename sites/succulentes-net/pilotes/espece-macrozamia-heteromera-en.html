Macrozamia heteromera is a small cycad with no visible trunk from inland New South Wales, in eastern Australia, where it grows mainly in the Warrumbungle Range and to the south-west of it, in dry eucalypt woodland. This species of the genus Macrozamia is recognised by a feature unusual among cycads: its leaflets are usually forked, divided once or twice. Described as early as 1884, it long included plants now placed in other species, among them Macrozamia glaucophylla, the former variety Macrozamia heteromera var. glauca. It is almost absent from cultivation outside Australia, and the IUCN lists it as Least Concern.

Key facts about Macrozamia heteromera

  • Accepted name: Macrozamia heteromera C.Moore (1884), published in the Journal and Proceedings of the Royal Society of New South Wales 17: 122, accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and the Australian Plant Census
  • Former names and synonyms: Macrozamia heteromera f. heteromera (Schuster, 1932); two other names by Schuster (f. harmsii, var. dicranophylloides) are doubtful names; Moore's varieties glauca and tenuifolia now correspond to Macrozamia glaucophylla and Macrozamia stenomera
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: no English common name is attested
  • Natural range: Australia, inland New South Wales: Warrumbungle Range and the country to the south-west; also recorded at Warialda and Howell (PlantNET)
  • IUCN status: Least Concern (LC), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated September 2020 and published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union; no listing under Australian threatened-species law (Atlas of Living Australia)
  • Documented hardiness: no measured value and no cultivation report; ranked among the hardiest Macrozamia by an Australian specialist, according to a forum post (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, dry woodland under eucalypts, on poor siliceous, sandy or stony soils over acid volcanic rocks; in cultivation, not documented
  • Habit and adult size: underground stem 8 to 25 cm across, depending on the source; 2 to 10 leaves 45 to 90 cm long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: one plant planted in 2012 at Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria (Cranbourne); declared to BGCI by Mount Annan and by Lotusland (California); no British garden lists it
  • Toxicity: seeds containing macrozamin (Ladd et al., 1993, cited by Asmussen); toxic throughout the genus (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Macrozamia heteromera

It is a small plant with a few mid-green leaves whose narrow leaflets are almost all forked. According to the Flora of Australia (Hill, 1998), it differs from its relatives in its mid-green, narrow, usually divided leaflets, and in the divided leaflets of its seedlings.

  • Stem: underground, 15 to 25 cm across according to the Flora of Australia, 8 to 15 cm according to the flora of New South Wales (PlantNET).
  • Leaves: 2 to 8 in the crown according to the Flora of Australia, up to 10 according to PlantNET and the Palm and Cycad Societies of Australia (PACSOA), most often 4 to 6; 45 to 90 cm long, moderately keeled, mid-green, dull to semi-glossy, erect at first then spreading.
  • Petiole: 15 to 20 cm (5 to 12 cm according to PlantNET), without spines: the lowest leaflets are not reduced to spines.
  • Leaflets: 60 to 100 per leaf (80 to 130 according to PlantNET), 10 to 21 cm long, divided once or twice, rarely undivided; the segments are 1.5 to 4 mm wide, with stomata on both faces. According to the PACSOA, the most common division gives two segments, but a leaflet may fork close to the axis and give three, four and very rarely five segments; the last leaflets, towards the tip, often stay undivided. They rise from the axis in a V, with a creamy-yellow callus at the base.
  • Male cones: spindle-shaped, 14 to 21 cm long and 4.5 to 5.5 cm wide; up to four per plant (PACSOA).
  • Female cones: ovoid, 12 to 17 cm long and 7 to 9 cm wide (10 to 25 cm according to PlantNET); usually solitary, sometimes two (PACSOA).
  • Seeds: ovoid and flattened, 26 to 31 mm long and 20 to 25 mm wide, with red flesh (scarlet according to PlantNET).

The differences between the Flora of Australia and PlantNET may come from the two floras having examined different populations; the sources do not explain them. L.A.S. Johnson (1959) separated the species from Macrozamia stenomera, which also has forked leaflets, by the constant presence of stomata on the upper surface of the leaflets, and noted that the degree of division varies a great deal from plant to plant and from place to place.

Hybrids

Johnson (1959) reported apparent hybrids with Macrozamia secunda and Macrozamia stenomera where their ranges meet, probable hybrids with Macrozamia plurinervia (then a subspecies of Macrozamia pauli-guilielmi) near Warialda, and possible hybridism with Macrozamia diplomera. No recent study has described them. The PACSOA also reports a large population of hybrids between two related species of the same complex, Macrozamia diplomera and Macrozamia polymorpha, in the southern Pilliga forest; before 1998, these plants were lumped with Macrozamia heteromera as its “green form”.

Possible confusion

Macrozamia heteromera belongs to a group of small species of inland New South Wales with often forked leaflets, easy to confuse with one another.

  • Macrozamia glaucophylla: larger, with longer leaves that are always blue or grey, and a “bushy” look (Flora of Australia, PACSOA).
  • Macrozamia stenomera: leaflets divided several times, which makes it easy to recognise according to the PACSOA.
  • Macrozamia polymorpha: similar size and number of leaves, but leaflets usually undivided.
  • Macrozamia diplomera: a related species, sometimes confused with it in the history of livestock poisoning (see Toxicity and precautions).

Taxonomy

Macrozamia heteromera C.Moore was published in 1884 in the Journal and Proceedings of the Royal Society of New South Wales (volume 17, page 122). It is accepted by The World List of Cycads and the Australian Plant Census and belongs to the family Zamiaceae.

Moore gave the plant as growing “among the Warrenbungle ranges and on the Castlereagh River country”, “discovered in 1858” and since collected near Rocky Glen, between Coonabarabran and Gunnedah (as quoted by Johnson, 1959). No original cone material survives. The only old sheet, labelled “Warrumbungle Ranges” without a collector's name, is kept in the Sydney herbarium (NSW 40720); Johnson thought it probably collected by W. Carron or by Moore himself, while The World List of Cycads dates it to about 1845. This sheet carries two pieces of leaf belonging to two different species, Macrozamia heteromera and Macrozamia diplomera; in 1959 Johnson chose the part matching Macrozamia heteromera as lectotype, so that both names could be kept in their usual sense.

In the same paper, Moore described two varieties. The first, Macrozamia heteromera var. glauca, is now a synonym of Macrozamia glaucophylla D.L.Jones (1998); the second, Macrozamia heteromera var. tenuifolia, a synonym of Macrozamia stenomera L.A.S.Johnson (1959), according to The World List of Cycads. In 1932, Julius Schuster multiplied forms and varieties; two of his names, f. harmsii and var. dicranophylloides, are now regarded as doubtful. According to the PACSOA, David Jones's revision of the complex in 1998 separated Macrozamia glaucophylla and Macrozamia polymorpha from Macrozamia heteromera. In practice, a plant labelled Macrozamia heteromera before 1998, especially a blue one, may belong to one of these species.

The Flora of Australia defines the complex by leaves with stomata on both faces, nearly the same colour above and below, and often divided leaflets. The PACSOA places Macrozamia heteromera in section Parazamia, that of the small species without spiny lowest leaflets. The phylogenomic study by Habib et al. (2022), covering 39 species, concludes that this traditional division into sections does not reflect the evolutionary history of the genus.

Etymology and history

The epithet joins the Greek hetero-, “different”, and -merus, “part”, referring to the sometimes divided and variously forked leaflets (Haynes, 2022, as given by The World List of Cycads). According to the PACSOA, Moore had noted their “different” and “variable” character. Johnson (1959) found the epithet particularly apt, alongside that of Macrozamia diplomera, “two-parted”. The genus name joins the Greek makros, “large”, to the genus name Zamia.

Before the 1998 revision, enthusiasts distinguished a “blue form” and a “green form” of Macrozamia heteromera. According to the PACSOA, the first corresponds to Macrozamia glaucophylla; the second covered true Macrozamia heteromera, Macrozamia polymorpha and the hybrids of the southern Pilliga.

Common names

No common name is attested in English or in any other language: the scientific name is used. The word “burrawang”, applied to several Macrozamia of New South Wales, is not specific to this species.

In the wild

Macrozamia heteromera lives mainly in the Warrumbungle Range and to the south-west of it (Flora of Australia); PlantNET places it chiefly in the Pilliga and Warrumbungle area, with outliers at Warialda and Howell. Among the collections cited by the Flora of Australia are Warrumbungle National Park, Mendooran and Gilgandra. Johnson (1959) gave a range in the eastern and southern Pilliga, from Narrabri to Coonabarabran and the foothills of the Warrumbungles. It grows scattered in dry eucalypt woodland, on poor siliceous soils over acid volcanic rocks. According to the PACSOA, it occupies undulating foothills and stony hillsides, as single plants, sometimes in small local stands, but never in large dense stands; coning is very irregular, as in most inland Macrozamia.

For the climate, the PACSOA quotes Coonabarabran, at 509 m: 735 mm of rain a year over 80 days, a third of it in summer; winter lows of −3.6 °C (25.5 °F) and summer highs of 35.9 °C (96.6 °F), reached at least once a week in July and January; and 76 frost days a year.

Conservation status and regulation

Macrozamia heteromera is not threatened. The IUCN Red List classes it as Least Concern (LC), in the assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated 11 September 2020 and published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, GBIF and Crossref, checked on 26 October 2026). The Atlas of Living Australia, which compiles the national and state threatened-species lists, shows no listing for it (checked on 26 October 2026).

For international trade, like other Macrozamia, it falls under CITES Appendix II, with annotation #4, which exempts seeds among other items (listing in force on 5 March 2026), and under Annex B in the European Union (Species+, checked on 26 October 2026). Ask for a seed-raised plant and check the regulations in force before any purchase or import.

Growing Macrozamia heteromera

No published source describes the cultivation of Macrozamia heteromera, and no grower has published an account of it. The pointers below rest on its habitat and on experience with the genus.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Soil: very free-draining, poor, sandy or stony, like the siliceous soils of its range.
  • Light: in the wild, under the light canopy of eucalypt woodland.
  • Water: at the Jardin zoologique tropical in La Londe-les-Maures, in the Var (southern France), experience with the genus Macrozamia shows that watering once a week in summer, when there is no rain, is a minimum, and that frost on waterlogged soil is more dangerous than dry frost. This Mediterranean experience is useful for gardens with a similar climate, such as coastal California or southern Australia; it does not concern this species in particular.
  • Growth: very slow. According to a specialist nurseryman on the Fous de palmiers forum (2014), it takes at least 15 to 20 years to obtain an adult plant of these small Macrozamia.

Container cultivation

No container-growing report has been published. A deep pot, a sandy, very free-draining mix and a dry winter are the safest precautions; the small size of the plant makes container growing easy.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

  • Australia: the census of Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria, downloaded on 19 October 2026, records one plant at Cranbourne (Area 12), planted on 19 December 2012. The Australian Botanic Garden Mount Annan declares it to BGCI. The species does not appear in the Garden Explorer of the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney.
  • United States: Lotusland (Santa Barbara, California) declares it to BGCI. The Montgomery Botanical Center does not list it in its online catalogue.
  • United Kingdom: no garden declares it to BGCI, and no other source mentions it there.

Five institutions in all declare it to BGCI, the other three in China, the Czech Republic and South Africa (Lowveld National Botanical Garden). A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not necessarily a living plant on display today. No English-language grower's report has been found.

Propagation

Propagation is by seed. A male plant, a female plant and, outside Australia, hand pollination are needed; cones are rare and irregular. The seedlings already have divided leaflets, which helps to identify them (Flora of Australia). Clean the seeds wearing gloves.

Pests and diseases

No pest or disease specific to the species is documented. In Macrozamia, waterlogged soil encourages root rot, and scale insects are the pest most often mentioned on growers' forums.

Cold hardiness

The cold hardiness of Macrozamia heteromera is unknown: no value has been measured and no cultivation report is documented. No source gives a USDA zone for it.

Mediterranean and European experience

No trial is documented in Europe. In 2014, a member of the French Fous de palmiers forum relayed the ranking of an Australian nurseryman specialising in cycads, who placed Macrozamia heteromera among the “hardiest” Macrozamia, those of the Coonabarabran area and the Pilliga; this ranking gives no temperature. Another member, in Roussillon (Pyrénées-Orientales, France), added that within this group his Macrozamia diplomera had come through −7 °C (19.4 °F) without damage in an unheated glasshouse, but that is another species.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No result has been published. The climate of Coonabarabran (76 frost days a year, weekly lows of −3.6 °C (25.5 °F) in July) gives an idea of conditions in the range, without saying what the plant can withstand.

Toxicity and precautions

Yes, Macrozamia heteromera is toxic. According to Asmussen (2011), the analyses of Ladd et al. (1993) found macrozamin in the kernels of six Macrozamia, Macrozamia heteromera among them, at lower levels than in Macrozamia miquelii and Macrozamia moorei. Macrozamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a powerful toxin of the liver, and their leaves cause nervous damage in cattle (Healy, 1967), irreversible according to McKenzie (2006).

The PACSOA corrects a widespread error: the mass death of sheep in the Coonabarabran district in 1929 was caused not by Macrozamia heteromera but, “without any doubt”, by Macrozamia diplomera, as shown by a veterinary report published in June 1930 in the Agricultural Gazette of New South Wales, with a photograph of one of the plants involved. Keep the plant and its seeds out of reach of children, dogs and cats, wear gloves to clean seeds and, in case of ingestion, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No use specific to Macrozamia heteromera is documented. Asmussen's study (2011) of the consumption of Macrozamia seeds by Aboriginal people mentions the species only for its macrozamin content.

Frequently asked questions

Why are the leaflets of Macrozamia heteromera forked?

It is a character of the species and of a few related species of inland New South Wales. Its leaflets divide once or twice, hence its name, which means “with different parts”. No published source explains what this does for the plant.

Is it the same plant as Macrozamia glaucophylla?

No. Macrozamia glaucophylla, the former “blue form” or var. glauca of Macrozamia heteromera, has been a separate species since 1998: larger, with longer leaves that are always blue or grey. Macrozamia heteromera has mid-green leaves.

Did Macrozamia heteromera poison sheep in 1929?

No. According to the PACSOA, the 1929 poisoning near Coonabarabran was caused by Macrozamia diplomera, as a 1930 veterinary report established. Macrozamia heteromera is nevertheless toxic.

Is it frost-hardy?

Nobody knows. It lives in a region with frequent frosts, and an Australian specialist ranks it among the hardiest Macrozamia, but no value or trial has been published. Grow it in a pot first, sheltered from wet frosts.

Is the species endangered?

No. The IUCN classes it as Least Concern (2022). Like all cycads, it is nevertheless covered by CITES.

Can it be grown in Britain or the United States?

Possibly, but nobody has documented it. The plant is very rare in the trade and grows very slowly. A deep pot, a sandy, very free-draining mix and a dry winter are the safest precautions.
