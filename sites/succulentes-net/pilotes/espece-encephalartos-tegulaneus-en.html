Encephalartos tegulaneus, the Kenyan giant cycad, is a large tree-like cycad endemic to Kenya, where it grows on rocky slopes and in dry forest in the mountains of the north, between about 1,400 and 2,300 m above sea level. This species of the genus Encephalartos, collected in 1954 by Joy Adamson, the author of Born Free, owes its name to the scales of its male cones, which overlap like roof tiles. Two subspecies are recognised: the typical subspecies, plentiful in the Mathews and Ndoto ranges, and subsp. powysii, known from a single hill near Mount Kenya. The species as a whole is assessed as Least Concern, but subsp. powysii is Critically Endangered. In cultivation it is a vigorous plant for frost-free climates.

Key facts about Encephalartos tegulaneus

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos tegulaneus Melville (1957), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15), IPNI, Species+ and GBIF
  • Subspecies: Encephalartos tegulaneus subsp. tegulaneus and Encephalartos tegulaneus subsp. powysii Miringu & Beentje
  • Synonyms: none
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Kenyan giant cycad (Species+); lpision in Samburu (Faden and Beentje, 1989)
  • Natural range: Kenya; Mathews Range, Ndoto Mountains and Mount Lololokwe (typical subspecies), former Meru District (subsp. powysii)
  • IUCN status: Least Concern (LC) for the species and for subsp. tegulaneus, Critically Endangered (CR) for subsp. powysii, assessments dated March 2020 and published in 2024 (GBIF, Crossref); The World List of Cycads still cites the 2010 assessment of the species (LC)
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I since 1977, like the whole genus; Annex A in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: no measured value; horticultural sources advise a frost-free position (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, steep slopes, rock outcrops and dry forest; in cultivation, sun or partial shade, a well-drained soil and regular watering in dry spells
  • Habit and adult size: trunk up to 7 m tall and 30 to 50 cm in diameter; leaves 120 to 180 cm long with 90 to 95 pairs of leaflets; male cones about 42 cm, female cones about 40 cm long (original description, Melville 1957); LLIFLE reports exceptional trunks 10 m long and leaves up to 3 m in cultivation
  • In the UK, US and Australia: no British garden declares it; in the United States, the Montgomery Botanical Center (14 accessions), Fairchild, the Huntington, the San Diego Botanic Garden, UC Berkeley and others; in Australia, Royal Botanic Garden Sydney and Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria (outdoor bed in Melbourne)
  • Toxicity: not documented for the species; seeds toxic, as in all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos tegulaneus

Encephalartos tegulaneus is recognised by its tall, palm-like stature, its usually single trunk and its long yellowish cones, whose male scales overlap like tiles. Seen from a distance, it can be taken for a palm.

Ronald Melville's original description (1957), based on Joy Adamson's collection, gives the following characters (figures checked against the printed pages):

  • Trunk: cylindrical, up to 7 m tall and 30 to 50 cm in diameter (33 to 50 cm in the Latin diagnosis), covered with alternating bands of leaf scars (8 to 9 cm wide) and scale-leaf scars; scale leaves (cataphylls) lanceolate-acuminate to linear, 8 to 18 cm long, covered outside with a dense brownish-fawn wool;
  • Leaves: linear-oblanceolate, 120 to 180 cm long and 30 to 40 cm wide, with about 90 to 95 pairs of leaflets; rachis distinctly grooved between the leaflets; swollen base of the petiole covered with brownish-buff wool;
  • Leaflets: the median ones oblong-lanceolate, rigid and leathery, with turned-back margins, 16 to 28 mm wide and 16 to 32 cm long according to the Latin diagnosis; the English text of the same article prints “16-22 cm. long, 16-28 cm. wide”, an evident misprint for the width, which the Latin gives in millimetres; 28 to 46 veins beneath; front margin without teeth or with one to three short spiny teeth near the base, back margin with one; pungent tip; towards the base, more spiny lanceolate leaflets (three to six spines on the front margin, two or three on the back), then three-forked, and a few simple spines just above the swollen petiole base;
  • Male cones: subcylindrical, about 42 cm long and 13 cm in diameter, on a stalk 20 cm long, fawn-woolly in the lower part; median scales bent downwards, 45 to 50 mm by 24 to 28 mm, overlapping like tiles;
  • Female cones: cylindrical, rounded at the top, about 40 cm long and 19 cm in diameter, on a stalk 9 cm long; median scales with an outer face 5 to 6 cm wide;
  • Seeds: oblong, truncate, more or less three-angled, scarlet, 30 to 37 mm long and 18 to 23 mm in diameter.

The online encyclopaedia LLIFLE, which cites the Flora of Tropical East Africa, the IUCN assessment and Kew, gives wider ranges, drawn partly from later or cultivated plants; some depart from the original description (number of veins, shape and size of the female cones):

  • a very large trunk, usually unbranched, upright or sometimes reclining then rising, up to 7 m tall and 50 cm in diameter (sometimes 10 m long and 60 cm to 1 m in diameter);
  • many leaves 1 to 1.8 m long (up to 3 m in cultivation), light green to bluish green, semi-glossy, flat in section, forming an upturned crown;
  • lanceolate, stiff, leathery leaflets 16 to 22 cm (up to 31 cm) long and 2 to 3 cm wide, with 26 to 40 veins, turned-back margins and one to three teeth near the base;
  • three to six male cones, green or yellow, narrowly ovoid to subcylindrical, 40 to 52 cm long, on a stalk up to 20 cm;
  • ovoid female cones, green or yellow, 40 to 68 cm long and 19 to 25 cm (up to 30 cm) in diameter;
  • seeds with an orange-red fleshy coat, 3 to 3.7 cm (up to 4 cm) long.

Faden and Beentje (1989), who examined later collections, note that the longest median leaflets reach 28.5 cm by 2.2 to 2.5 cm, that the front margin carries one or two teeth towards the base, as in the original description, but that the back margin may carry up to two. They count about four female cones and three to six male cones per plant. Jungle Music, a nursery in southern California, describes a grey-green plant with stiff, leathery, sharply armed leaflets, yellow male cones and orange female cones; its page also gives trunks “up to 25 feet” and “over ten meters tall”, about one metre in diameter, figures that it does not source and that exceed all the published descriptions.

The characters that separate subsp. powysii from the typical subspecies are set out in its description (Miringu and Beentje, 1999), together with its habitat, population and conservation status.

Hybrids

No natural or garden hybrid of Encephalartos tegulaneus is documented.

Possible confusion

In Kenya, the possible confusions concern the large green Encephalartos of East Africa. Faden and Beentje (1989) distinguish Encephalartos tegulaneus from Encephalartos kisambo by its smaller, more widely spaced leaflets. The oldest known collection of the species, made in 1911, was first identified as Encephalartos hildebrandtii, a species of Kenya and Tanzania.

In Melville's keys (1957), the species closest to it is Encephalartos bubalinus: both have woolly petiole bases and scale leaves, but in Encephalartos tegulaneus the scale leaves are lanceolate to linear with a soft brownish-buff wool and the scales of the male cone are bent downwards, whereas in Encephalartos bubalinus the scale leaves are triangular to ovate, thick and keeled, covered with a close buff felt, and the male cone scales spread more or less horizontally.

When they described Encephalartos sclavoi from Tanzania, Stevenson, Moretti and De Luca placed it in a group of species that includes Encephalartos tegulaneus, together with Encephalartos bubalinus, Encephalartos gratus, Encephalartos hildebrandtii and Encephalartos voiensis, now included in Encephalartos kisambo. The cones with overlapping scales and the mountain origin in northern Kenya remain the best clues.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos tegulaneus is an accepted name with no synonyms. Ronald Melville described it in 1957 in the Kew Bulletin (12(2): 249, fig. 3), in an article on the Encephalartos of central Africa written for the Flora of Tropical East Africa, from a single collection: Joy Adamson's, made on 7 May 1954 on Mount Lololokwe, which the Samburu call Sabatchi, in the Mathews Range, about 35 miles (56 km) north of Isiolo; the type label gives an altitude of 2,300 m. The holotype is at Kew (K), with isotypes in Berlin (B), Meise (BR) and Nairobi (EA). The World List of Cycads dates the collection 5 May 1954 and gives 7,000 feet, whereas the original description gives 7 May and 2,300 m.

In 1999 Beatrice W. Miringu and Henk Beentje described subsp. powysii in the Journal of East African Natural History (88: 35–39), from the former Meru District in central Kenya (holotype B.W. Miringu 1, 23 November 1995, EA; IPNI and The World List of Cycads date its actual publication to 2000). This publication automatically created the name of the typical subspecies, Encephalartos tegulaneus subsp. tegulaneus, which covers the populations of the Mathews Range, the Ndoto Mountains and Mount Lololokwe.

Etymology and history

The epithet comes from the Latin tegula, “tile”, and refers to the scales of the male cones, which overlap like the tiles on a roof; Melville gives this explanation himself (Haynes, 2022, cited by The World List of Cycads, says the same). Subsp. powysii honours Gilfrid Powys, a former district officer of Meru District and keen plantsman, who introduced the second author, Henk Beentje, to this population.

Joy Adamson was long thought to have been the first to collect the species, in 1954. Melville thanks her for “excellent and complete” specimens, accompanied by photographs of the plants in their natural setting and a watercolour sketch, and quotes her notes: the mountain rises abruptly from about 3,000 to about 7,000 feet (about 900 to 2,100 m), with heavy “cedar” forest on top, date palms and a few huge candelabra euphorbias, and the cycad grows on bare rock, in the thickest forest or near springs. In 1989, however, Faden and Beentje found an older collection in the United States National Herbarium, made on 1 September 1911 by Edmund Heller during the Smithsonian-Rainey expedition to East Africa. Its label, which places it on Mount Warges in the Mathews Range, between 4,000 and 5,000 feet, describes it as plentiful at the edge and in the forest of the western slope up to 6,000 feet, as a “tree 8–12 ft.” The plant had been identified as Encephalartos hildebrandtii. The same authors showed that the species, known from the original description and the Flora of Tropical East Africa (1958) only from Mount Lololokwe, also grows in the Ndoto Mountains and in the Mathews Range proper.

Common names

In English, “Kenyan giant cycad” is used by Species+ and already appeared in the Virtual Cycad Encyclopedia of the Palm and Cycad Societies of Florida (1998–2000). In Samburu, the language of the herders of the Mathews Range, the plant is called “lpision” (Faden and Beentje, 1989). Jungle Music calls it the “Giant Kenyan African cycad”, a commercial description rather than an established name.

In the wild

Encephalartos tegulaneus is endemic to Kenya. The typical subspecies lives in remote, hard-to-reach parts of the Mathews Range, especially on the Ndoto, Warges and Lololokwe hills and near Barsaloi, between 1,400 and 2,300 m above sea level, according to the IUCN assessment as quoted by LLIFLE. It grows on steep slopes and rock outcrops, in bushland or dense dry forest.

Faden and Beentje (1989) add that it can be one of the dominant species in the understorey of the dry upland forests which, in the Mathews Range, at about 1,600 to 2,150 m, are dominated by Podocarpus latifolius and the juniper Juniperus procera. It is less common in the moister forests with Croton megalocarpus and Strychnos, and in the dry bushland with Euphorbia candelabrum at lower altitudes, down to 1,400 m. Subsp. powysii is known only from a single hill near Mount Kenya.

Reproduction is probably very slow: many seeds are eaten by baboons or wild pigs, and the generation length is estimated at about 70 years (IUCN assessment, as quoted by LLIFLE).

Conservation status and regulation

The species is assessed as Least Concern (LC) on the IUCN Red List. Its two subspecies, however, do not share the same fate, and each now has its own assessment. According to Crossref and GBIF, the current assessments, by the Eastern Arc Mountains and Coastal Forests CEPF Plant Assessment Project and J.D. Bösenberg, are dated 11 March 2020 for the species and the typical subspecies (both LC) and 9 March 2020 for subsp. powysii (CR), and were published in 2024. The World List of Cycads still cites the previous assessment of the species, by J.S. Donaldson (2010), also LC, and lists the two subspecies as “Not Evaluated”.

For the typical subspecies, the assessment quoted by LLIFLE counts six localities and at least 5,000 to 10,000 mature individuals, with stable populations; the IUCN action plan of 2003 (Donaldson, 2003) gave the same order of magnitude and cited traditional use as a minor threat. For subsp. powysii, the same action plan counted 200 to 400 plants, in moderate decline because of collecting, and already classed it as Critically Endangered.

Regulation, according to Species+ (consulted through its public API on 26 October 2026):

  • CITES: Appendix I since 4 February 1977, like the whole genus;
  • European Union: Annex A, current list in Regulation (EU) 2026/1383 (in force since 29 June 2026); for artificially propagated plants imported from South Africa (source codes A and D), the EU Scientific Review Group gave a positive opinion for caudices up to 7 cm in diameter (20 June 2011) and a negative opinion above that size (7 February 2013), both still current.

Seeds are covered in the same way as plants. Any plant offered for sale must come from cultivation and carry the CITES documents required by the countries concerned (and, in the European Union, the EU documents). These notes do not replace the official texts: check Species+ and the national authorities before any purchase or import.

Growing Encephalartos tegulaneus

Encephalartos tegulaneus is considered an easy, vigorous cycad in frost-free climates, provided it is given room. The cultivation data come from horticultural sources (LLIFLE, Jungle Music), not from a botanic garden sheet.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

LLIFLE, which compiles horticultural sources, describes it as adaptable, to be grown in sun or partial shade in a well-drained soil, with regular watering in the dry months. Young plants grow slowly in their first years, especially in pots too small for their taproot, then speed up: a four- or five-year-old plant can carry leaves a metre long. In full sun the leaves stay shorter and less exposed to wind; in shade they lengthen and may break. The plant is said to stand transplanting well as an adult, preferably after its leaves have been removed.

In southern California, Jungle Music recommends it as a good garden plant, in partial sun on the coast and inland, and in filtered light or shade in desert areas, in a loamy soil. It notes that the species tolerates water but does not want to be excessively wet. Its size means it should be planted well back in the garden, away from paths.

Container cultivation

A pot is only a stage for this tall species. LLIFLE stresses that young plants need deep pots for their taproot, and that growth is slowed by containers that are too small. In cool climates, a large container moved under glass for the winter is the only realistic way to grow it, until the plant outgrows it.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

BGCI PlantSearch lists 22 institutions holding the species. In the United States, the Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida) lists 14 accessions in its online catalogue, two older ones, one of whose numbers points to 1964, and twelve from 2006; BGCI also lists Fairchild, the Atlanta Botanical Garden, the Huntington, the San Diego Botanic Garden, the San Diego Zoo, UC Berkeley, the New York Botanical Garden, the United States Botanic Garden in Washington and two Hawaiian gardens. In Australia, Royal Botanic Garden Sydney declares it, and Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria grows it in an outdoor bed at Melbourne Gardens according to its living collection census. No British garden declares it. Subsp. powysii is declared only by the Nairobi Physic Garden in Kenya. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not a living plant on display today. In Italy, the Messina botanic garden listed the species in 2009. Jungle Music describes it as very rare but offers it in several sizes, from small plants to boxed specimens of coning size.

Propagation

Sowing is the usual method; the seeds, with their orange-red fleshy coat, are sown fresh, after cleaning with gloves (see the article on growing cycads from seed). LLIFLE stresses that seedlings need deep pots for their taproot. The trunk is usually single and offsets are uncommon; Jungle Music notes that basal suckers do occur, but not freely. Seeds are covered by CITES Appendix I.

Pests and diseases

No disease or pest specific to Encephalartos tegulaneus is documented in cultivation. In the wild, baboons and wild pigs eat a large share of the seeds. As with all Encephalartos, rot of the roots and caudex in a cold, waterlogged medium is the main risk in cultivation, which matches Jungle Music's warning against excessive wetness.

Cold hardiness

No cold-tolerance value, measured or reported by a grower, is documented for Encephalartos tegulaneus; the horticultural sources advise a frost-free position. As with all Encephalartos, survivals in cold areas often concern young plants whose caudex is still small, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil; a large species with an aerial trunk like this one loses that protection once adult.

Mediterranean and European experience

  • Southern Italy: on the Tropicamente forum, a collector in Salento (Apulia) listed the species in December 2008 among those that would have no chance of surviving a frost, an opinion not backed by a dated episode.
  • France: no cultivation result is documented.

No measured result is therefore available for Mediterranean climates; a large container overwintered frost-free under glass is the prudent choice.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

  • United States: LLIFLE and Jungle Music present it as well suited to subtropical climates, including that of southern California, without giving temperatures.
  • Australia: a plant grows in an outdoor bed in Melbourne (Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria census), without published winter data.
  • United Kingdom: no data; only a heated glasshouse with plenty of headroom would suit an adult plant.

No USDA hardiness zone is documented for Encephalartos tegulaneus in a reliable source.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data have been published for Encephalartos tegulaneus. Like other cycads, Encephalartos contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a toxin affecting the liver, the digestive tract and the nervous system; the seeds are the most dangerous part. Treat the plant as toxic, wear gloves to clean the seeds, and keep plants and seeds out of reach of children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

According to the IUCN assessment as quoted by LLIFLE, trunks of the typical subspecies were traditionally hollowed out to serve as watering troughs for livestock. No food or medicinal use is documented.

Frequently asked questions

Why is it called tegulaneus?

Because of its male cones. Their scales overlap like the tiles on a roof, tegula in Latin; Melville gives this explanation himself in the original description.

Did Joy Adamson really discover it?

Partly. Her 1954 collection served to describe the species in 1957, but Edmund Heller had already collected it in 1911 without the plant being recognised: it had been taken for Encephalartos hildebrandtii.

What is the difference between the two subspecies?

First of all their range and status. The typical subspecies is plentiful in the mountains of northern Kenya and assessed as Least Concern; subsp. powysii is known only from one hill near Mount Kenya, suffers from collecting and is assessed as Critically Endangered. The morphological characters that separate them are set out in the 1999 description of subsp. powysii.

Is it endangered?

Not as a whole: the species is assessed as Least Concern. Subsp. powysii, however, is Critically Endangered.

How tall does it grow?

It can become very tall. The original description gives a trunk up to 7 m tall and 50 cm in diameter, with leaves 1.2 to 1.8 m long; LLIFLE reports trunks 10 m long and longer leaves in cultivation. Growth speeds up after the first few years.

Is it frost-hardy?

No, according to the horticultural sources, which recommend a frost-free position. No measurement has been published; in cool climates, grow it in a large container overwintered frost-free.

Is it poisonous?

It should be treated as such. No data exist for the species, but Encephalartos contain toxins dangerous to people and animals.
