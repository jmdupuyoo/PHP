Encephalartos mackenziei is a multi-stemmed cycad from South Sudan, known only from the Didinga Hills, in Eastern Equatoria State. This species of the genus Encephalartos was described in 2002 by the botanist Leonard E. Newton, from a plant collected in 1995 by Paul Mackenzie behind the village of Chukudum. With Encephalartos septentrionalis, it is one of the northernmost species of the genus. The IUCN lists it as Critically Endangered. Its stems, up to ten per plant, reach 3.5 m in length and end up lying on the ground.

Key facts about Encephalartos mackenziei

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos mackenziei L.E.Newton (2002), according to The World List of Cycads
  • Former names and synonyms: none; provisional name “Encephalartos sp. Sudan”, under which Heibloem (1999) illustrated it before its description
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: none attested, in English or in any other language
  • Natural range: South Sudan, Didinga Hills (Eastern Equatoria State)
  • IUCN status: Critically Endangered (CR), assessment dated 4 March 2020 and published in the IUCN Red List 2024-1; the previous assessment (October 2009, published in 2010) had already listed it as Critically Endangered under criterion B1ab(i,ii,iii)
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I since 1977, like the whole genus; Annex A in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: no measured value; considered cold-sensitive by a Californian nursery (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, rocky hills; in cultivation, a free-draining soil and partial sun or filtered light, according to a nursery
  • Habit and adult size: up to ten stems per plant, up to 3.5 m long and 35 cm in diameter, erect for 1.5 m and then lying down; leaves up to 1.5 m long (Newton, 2002)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: no botanic garden declares it to BGCI; offered occasionally by a nursery in southern California
  • Toxicity: not studied for this species; a documented risk for the genus (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos mackenziei

What sets the species apart, according to its original description, published by Newton in 2002 in the Botanical Journal of the Linnean Society, is its branching at the base, which produces up to ten stems, its broad green leaflets and certain details of its cone scales. Newton describes the plant as follows:

  • Stems: up to 3.5 m long and 35 cm in diameter, erect for 1.5 m, then leaning and finally lying on the ground; the plant branches at the base, and the largest have up to ten stems;
  • Leaves: elongate-elliptic, up to 150 cm long and 32 cm wide, light green and downy when emerging, later darker green and almost hairless; rachis without a groove, at most 15 mm wide in the middle of the leaf;
  • Leaflets: the median ones ovate to slightly curved, 15 to 17 cm long and 27 to 35 mm wide, green, with short, sparse white down on both faces; three to five spines near the base of the upper margin, none or one on the lower margin; a short, sharp point directed towards the tip of the leaf; inserted at 60° on the rachis and angled at 45°, those of the two sides forming a V; 23 to 28 mm apart; towards the base they decrease and pass into simple spines 8 to 10 mm long;
  • Male cones: up to ten at a time, ovoid-cylindrical, up to 22 cm long and 9 cm wide, green, then olive brown to yellow when mature; stalk up to 30 cm, densely downy; median scales hairless, 30 mm long;
  • Female cones: up to four at a time, ovoid, up to 40 cm long and 16 to 18 cm in diameter, green, then olive brown to yellow when mature; stalk 10 to 15 cm; median scales hairless, with an outer face 55 to 60 mm wide;
  • Seeds: oblong, 35 mm long and 25 mm in diameter, with an orange fleshy coat (sarcotesta).

Hybrids

No natural or cultivated hybrid of Encephalartos mackenziei is documented.

Possible confusion

The most likely confusion is with Encephalartos septentrionalis, the other South Sudanese species, also found in Uganda, the Central African Republic and the Democratic Republic of the Congo. A Californian nursery notes that young plants of the two species look alike, but that Encephalartos mackenziei differs in its adult size and in the length and width of its leaflets. Newton (2002) gives the following differences: in Encephalartos septentrionalis the stems are no more than 2 m long and 30 cm wide and only two or three per clump, the rachis is clearly hollowed between the leaflets, the leaves are greyish blue-green (green in Encephalartos mackenziei), the leaflets carry one to eight spines on both margins, the male cone scales are finely downy and only 12 to 22 mm long, and the seeds are dark red (orange in Encephalartos mackenziei).

Further south, in Uganda, Encephalartos macrostrobilus grows on a granite plateau near the South Sudanese border. According to Newton, it differs mainly in its usually single stem (sometimes with offsets at the base), its narrower leaflets (at most 25 mm wide) and its much larger female cones (up to 80 cm).

Taxonomy

Encephalartos mackenziei L.E.Newton is accepted by The World List of Cycads. Leonard E. Newton described it in 2002 under the title “A new species of Encephalartos (Zamiaceae) in Sudan”, in the Botanical Journal of the Linnean Society (140(2): 187–192, figs 1–6). The type (holotype), collected without number by P.C. Mackenzie on 11 April 1995 in the Didinga Hills, behind the village of Chukudum (then Equatoria Province, Sudan), is kept at the East African Herbarium in Nairobi (EA). The original description gives only “Equatoria Province”: Newton deliberately withheld the locality to protect the plants from illegal collecting; the precise locality comes from The World List of Cycads. The species has no synonym.

Newton could not visit the site because of the civil war; he worked on fresh leaves and cones brought back by Mackenzie, then on ten living plants established in Mackenzie's garden in Nairobi. He chose the rank of species, rather than subspecies of Encephalartos septentrionalis, because the two taxa differ in their vegetative parts, in their cones and in their geographical isolation.

Before its description, it was illustrated in colour under the provisional name “Encephalartos sp. Sudan” by Heibloem (1999), according to Newton. An editorial note in the journal Encephalartos (1997), citing a manuscript by Osborne, already mentions under that name a seed collection made around 1995 in a population in the Didinga Hills. Turner, Sclavo and Malaisse (2006) cite Newton's description among recent work on the central African Encephalartos and echo his call for biosystematic studies to clarify the rank of several taxa. No published genetic study clarifies the relationships of the species.

Etymology and history

The epithet honours Paul Mackenzie, owner of the Rosslyn River Garden Centre in Nairobi, Kenya, who collected the type specimen (Haynes, 2022, followed by The World List of Cycads). Newton presents him as a traveller who had explored tropical Africa in search of cycads in the wild, and whose living collection in Nairobi is open to botanists. The material was collected in 1995 by Paul Mackenzie and Peter Heibloem; the description followed seven years later. According to the Californian nursery already mentioned, the area is seldom visited by enthusiasts or botanists, and no later expedition is documented.

Common names

No common name is attested, in English or in any other language: Species+ gives none, and no name in the Didinga language is recorded.

In the wild

Encephalartos mackenziei is known only from the Didinga Hills, in South Sudan, in Eastern Equatoria State (The World List of Cycads). Newton (2002) shows a plant growing on rocks and describes, from Heibloem and Mackenzie, a “large and widespread” population around the type locality; the altitude is not given in the original description. The stems there are blackened by frequent fires, which usually burn the previous season's leaves; a new crown of leaves emerges after the fire. The cones mature around September. Many seedlings are destroyed by fire, but some survive and the species regenerates. The area is much disturbed by villagers, but the rocky terrain does not lend itself to intensive farming, and Newton then saw no reason to fear for the survival of the population. The IUCN assessments of 2010 and 2024 nevertheless conclude that the species is critically at risk (see below); the published sources do not explain the difference, which may reflect the very small range of the species. A Californian nursery describes the habitat as rocky hills and woodland, with a hot, dry climate and rain during the summer wet season.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos mackenziei is listed as Critically Endangered (CR) on the IUCN Red List. The current assessment, by J.D. Bösenberg, is dated 4 March 2020 and was published in the Red List 2024-1, with no change of category (GBIF and Crossref, consulted on 26 October 2026; these sources do not give its criteria). The previous assessment, by J.S. Donaldson, dated October 2009 and published in 2010, is the one still cited by The World List of Cycads: it listed the species as Critically Endangered under criterion B1ab(i,ii,iii), that is a very small extent of occurrence, a single location or a severely fragmented population, and a continuing decline in extent of occurrence, area of occupancy and habitat quality. Described in 2002, the species did not appear in the IUCN action plan of 2003. A nursery mentions reports of poaching, which no source confirms.

Like every species of the genus, it has been listed in CITES Appendix I since 4 February 1977, and in Annex A in the European Union (Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026). For imports from South Africa of artificially propagated plants (source codes A and D), the EU Scientific Review Group issued a positive opinion for caudices no more than 7 cm in diameter (2011) and a negative opinion above that size (2013). Species+ consulted on 26 October 2026: check the regulations in force.

Growing Encephalartos mackenziei

Encephalartos mackenziei is very rare in collections, in Europe as elsewhere. On the French Fous de palmiers forum, in May 2015, a collector whose location is not given showed a plant, and a grower in Belley (Ain) and Montpellier (Hérault), France, noted that the species is rarely seen in cultivation; the first replied that it is hard to obtain, especially in Europe.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

A specialist nursery in Encinitas, in southern California, describes moderate to slow growth and advises partial sun or filtered light in coastal and inland gardens, a soil that drains well, and some protection in winter; it considers the species difficult in desert areas, where it would need shade or filtered light under protection. In Nairobi, ten plants from the 1995 collection were well established in Paul Mackenzie's garden, where Newton studied them a few years later.

Container cultivation

No container-growing report has been published; the general rules for the genus apply, with a deep pot and a free-draining mix.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

  • United States: no botanic garden declares the species to BGCI, and the Montgomery Botanical Center catalogue has no accession; the Encinitas nursery offers plants occasionally and notes that seeds have not been available for a while.
  • United Kingdom and Australia: no garden declares it to BGCI, and no other source mentions it there.

Only two institutions in the world declare the species to BGCI, in China and in Spain. At the Nong Nooch Tropical Botanical Garden in Thailand, which grows every Encephalartos species but one, the curator notes in 2022 that tropical species thrive there, without naming this one.

Propagation

Propagation is by fresh, fertile seed, very rarely available according to the Californian nursery, or by separating the offsets that the species forms at the base, a technique not documented for it. Seeds, like plants, fall under CITES Appendix I.

Pests and diseases

No disease or pest specific to Encephalartos mackenziei is documented. As with other Encephalartos, root and stem rot threaten plants kept cold and wet.

Cold hardiness

No cold limit has been measured for Encephalartos mackenziei. The Encinitas nursery considers it sensitive to cold, like other central African species with thin leaflet tissue: hardier than some, but less hardy than the more robust central African species and much less so than the South African species. It advises winter protection in a subtropical climate. As with other Encephalartos, survivals in cold areas often concern young plants whose caudex is still underground, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil; a species with aerial stems like this one does not benefit from it as an adult.

Mediterranean and European experience

No outdoor result is documented in France or Italy, and no plant is reported in an Italian botanic garden. Readers in Mediterranean climates, or in comparable climates such as coastal California or southern Australia, should keep it frost-free in winter until an outdoor trial is documented.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

In coastal southern California, the species is grown with winter protection, according to the Encinitas nursery. No other data is documented, and no USDA hardiness zone is given for the species in any source.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data specific to Encephalartos mackenziei is documented, but the risk is documented for the genus: Encephalartos are toxic, and their seeds particularly so. The toxicity of one species of the genus, Encephalartos horridus, was demonstrated as early as 1944 by Van der Walt. Cycads contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a compound that affects the liver, the digestive tract and the nervous system. For another cycad, the sago palm (Cycas revoluta), the ASPCA reports vomiting, black stools, jaundice, bleeding gastroenteritis, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses.

Wear gloves to handle the seeds and their fleshy coat, pick up fallen seeds and keep plants and seeds out of reach of children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use of Encephalartos mackenziei is documented.

Frequently asked questions

Where does Encephalartos mackenziei grow?

Only in South Sudan, in the Didinga Hills, in Eastern Equatoria State. The type was collected in 1995 behind the village of Chukudum. It is one of the northernmost species of the genus.

Who was Mackenzie?

Paul Mackenzie, owner of a garden centre in Nairobi, Kenya, the Rosslyn River Garden Centre. He collected in 1995 the specimen used to describe the species, and Newton named it after him in 2002.

How can it be told apart from Encephalartos septentrionalis?

By its many stems and its broad green leaflets. According to the original description, Encephalartos mackenziei branches at the base and forms up to ten stems 3.5 m long; its leaflets reach 35 mm in width and its leaves are green, not blue-green. Its seeds are orange, dark red in Encephalartos septentrionalis. Young plants of the two species look alike.

Is it threatened?

Yes. The IUCN has listed it as Critically Endangered since 2010, a category kept in the assessment published in 2024, because it occupies a tiny area whose habitat is declining. The 2002 description, however, spoke of a large population that was regenerating.

Is it hardy?

No. No cold value has been measured, but a Californian nursery considers it sensitive to cold and advises winter protection. In climates with frost, it should be overwintered frost-free.

Is it poisonous?

Yes, it should be treated as poisonous. No study deals with this species, but toxicity is demonstrated in the genus and cycad seeds contain cycasin.
