Encephalartos nubimontanus is a blue-grey South African cycad that once grew on cliffs in the Wolkberg region of Limpopo province and is now extinct in the wild. This species of the genus Encephalartos was described in 1995, then described again in 1996 as Encephalartos venetus, a name now treated as a synonym. Collectors emptied its only known site within about a decade: 66 plants were counted in the 1990s and none in 2010–2011. The species survives only in cultivation, where it is fairly widespread thanks to nurseries, and it is valued in gardens as a fast-growing blue cycad with a trunk up to 2.5 m long.

Key facts about Encephalartos nubimontanus

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos nubimontanus P.J.H.Hurter (1995), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15), GBIF and Species+
  • Former names and synonyms: Encephalartos venetus Vorster (1996)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: blue cycad (SANBI, PlantZAfrica, Species+), cloud mountain cycad (PlantZAfrica); bloubroodboom in Afrikaans (PlantZAfrica)
  • Natural range: South Africa, Limpopo province (Wolkberg region, mountains north and east of Penge), at about 1,000 m; extinct in the wild
  • IUCN status: Extinct in the Wild (EW), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg published in 2022; already listed as EW in 2006
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I since 1977, like the whole genus; Annex A in the European Union, with a negative EU opinion on artificially propagated plants from South Africa; specially protected in Limpopo
  • Documented hardiness: no measured value; according to PlantZAfrica, frost may burn the leaves but will not kill established plants (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, cliff faces in full sun in low, open deciduous woodland; in cultivation, full sun or semi-shade and a well-drained soil (PlantZAfrica)
  • Habit and adult size: trunk often reclining, up to 2.5 m long and 35 to 40 cm in diameter; glaucous leaves 1.1 to 2 m long (original description)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Fairchild, the Huntington, Lotusland, the San Diego Botanic Garden, the San Diego Zoo, the Atlanta Botanical Garden and three other American institutions; Royal Botanic Garden Sydney, Brisbane and James Cook University in Australia (BGCI); no British garden declares it
  • Toxicity: not studied for the species; documented risk in the genus (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos nubimontanus

It is recognised by its tree-like, often reclining trunk and its dense crown of stiff, glaucous leaves, recurved at the tip, whose leaflets are usually curved like a sickle and often toothed. Hurter's original description (1995) gives the following characters:

  • Habit: suckering at the base; trunk often decumbent, up to 2.5 m long and 35 to 40 cm in diameter, keeping the old leaf bases; cataphylls (the small scales that protect the bud) velvety white, becoming almost hairless with age
  • Leaves: 1.1 to 2 m long (1.4 m on average); petiole about 23 cm, swollen at the base, slightly tomentose, with a reddish-brown collar when mature; rachis hairless, round in section, turning yellow with age; opposite leaflets set at about 70° to each other, giving the leaf a V-shaped profile, and pointing towards the leaf tip
  • Leaflets: those of the middle of the leaf 18 to 25 cm long and 15 to 25 mm wide, curved, pungent, entire or toothed; the basal leaflets reduced to a series of prickles
  • Male cones: hairless, one to five per trunk, subconical, 25 to 40 cm long and 5 to 9 cm in diameter; the middle scales tilt slightly downwards and end in a drooping, often slightly crenate lip
  • Female cones: hairless, one to three per trunk, ovoid, 36 to 40 cm long and 18 to 20 cm in diameter; the sterile scales at the top are drawn out into upright lips
  • Seeds: about 200 per female cone, with an orange-red fleshy coat; the seed without its coat measures 35 to 38 mm by 23 to 30 mm

The sources differ on some points. The original description calls the scale faces light green, whereas SANBI and PlantZAfrica describe bluish-green male cones and light blue-green female cones. PlantZAfrica, in a fact sheet by Winfred Ngwenya (Lowveld National Botanical Garden, August 2020), describes a single-stemmed plant sometimes forming clusters of offsets, with leaves about 1.2 m long, blue-grey, slightly twisted on some leaves, arranged in a rounded, arching crown, two to five male cones and seeds 25 to 38 mm long. SANBI adds that the plant is most often single-stemmed, with occasional basal suckers, and that it would produce on average two cones at a time every five years, according to an unpublished thesis (Konings, 2016). Jungle Music, a nursery in southern California, finds mature plants almost always with basal suckers, with a leaf colour varying from blue-green to powdery white, leaflets and petioles mildly armed to almost unarmed, and male and female cones yellow-green with a hint of blue.

Hybrids

No hybrid of Encephalartos nubimontanus is described. Since the species survives only in collections, where several blue species are often grown side by side and pollinated by hand, seed of unknown origin may be hybrid; female cones must be isolated and the pollen source controlled to obtain pure seed.

Possible confusion

The commonest confusion is with Encephalartos cupidus, its closest relative according to SANBI, which also has stiff, pungent, glaucous leaves. Hurter (1995) gives the differences: Encephalartos cupidus is a dwarf species with a largely underground stem and oblong, uncurved median leaflets; its female cones lack the drawn-out sterile scales at the top, and the scales of its male cones are spreading, without a drooping lip. PlantZAfrica sums it up in terms of habit: Encephalartos cupidus is a small plant, up to 1 m tall, with several underground stems.

This resemblance has practical consequences. SANBI reports that owners of Encephalartos nubimontanus plants hold permits issued for Encephalartos cupidus and claim not to have known that they were a different species. Conversely, a grower in Nardò (Apulia, Italy) reports on the Tropicamente forum, in February 2013, that a plant bought as nubimontanus turned out to be an Encephalartos cupidus with a caudex of about 20 cm. The same grower had pointed out in 2008 that nubimontanus is entirely blue, questioning the identity of a photographed plant.

Other blue species of the same group can also be confusing. On the French forum Fous de palmiers, about a plant offered as Encephalartos middelburgensis, a member wrote in 2013 that it looked neither like Encephalartos cupidus nor really like Encephalartos nubimontanus, “but there are many wild variants”, and leaned towards Encephalartos dyerianus. Finally, sales lists reproduced on that forum in 2009 offered commercial forms called “nubimontanus robust”; such names have no botanical standing and are not recognised by The World List of Cycads.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos nubimontanus is accepted by The World List of Cycads, GBIF and Species+. It was described by P.J.H. Hurter, of the Lowveld National Botanical Garden in Nelspruit, in the journal Phytologia (78(6): 409–410, fig. 1), in a paper that also described Encephalartos whitelockii. The issue is dated June 1995; IPNI gives 29 November 1995 as the effective date of publication. The type (holotype) is a leaf and a male cone collected by Hurter on 27 March 1995 in the Northern Province, at 1,000 m (Hurter 95R/1), kept at the National Herbarium in Pretoria (PRE). Seven other collections, including a female cone, accompany the description; to protect the plants from poachers, the author gave their position only at the scale of a 1:250,000 map (sheet 2430, Pilgrim's Rest).

In 1996 Piet Vorster described the same plant as Encephalartos venetus, in the South African Journal of Botany (62(2): 71). The World List of Cycads, IPNI and Species+ treat this name as a synonym. Under the principle of priority of the botanical code, the name published first, nubimontanus, applies; the detailed reasons for the synonymy are not documented. For the reader, a label reading “Encephalartos venetus” therefore refers to this species. SANBI places the species in the Encephalartos eugene-maraisii complex, a group of blue-leaved species from northern South Africa.

Etymology and history

The epithet refers to the “cloud mountains”: according to Haynes (2022), quoted by The World List of Cycads, it is formed from the Latin nubilis (“cloud”) and montanus (“of the mountains”), in reference to the Wolkberg region, whose Afrikaans name means “cloud mountain”. PlantZAfrica gives the same meaning, “growing on the cloud mountains”. Jungle Music's reading of the epithet as “blue-grey” is not supported by these sources. The meaning of the name venetus is not documented.

The history of the species is short. Hurter described it in 1995 from a single small area, where the plants grew scattered on cliffs. According to the SANBI document underpinning South Africa's trade finding (2023, updated 2025), 66 plants were seen in the 1990s, eight in 2000 or 2001 depending on the passage of the document, only a few stems in 2003–2004, and none in 2010–2011. PlantZAfrica estimates that the population once numbered up to 100 mature plants. The last stems were removed by the conservation authorities for safekeeping in a collection. Helicopter surveys over the last decade have found no plants, although a ground check is still needed.

SANBI considers it very likely that the large plants in collections were taken illegally from the wild and then legalised in other provinces: one collector alone held 50 to 60, suckers included. Wild plants, perhaps females, may still be present in private collections; a census was under way when the document was written. It is one of the five Encephalartos classed as Extinct in the Wild by The World List of Cycads, with Encephalartos woodii, Encephalartos heenanii, Encephalartos brevifoliolatus and Encephalartos relictus.

Common names

SANBI and Species+ use the English name blue cycad; PlantZAfrica adds cloud mountain cycad, together with bloubroodboom in Afrikaans, isigciki-somkhovu in Zulu and gebeleweni in Swati. “Blue cycad” is also used loosely for blue-leaved cycads in general, which makes it ambiguous; “cloud mountain cycad” is a translation of the epithet.

In the wild

Encephalartos nubimontanus no longer exists in the wild. Hurter (1995) describes it as known from a single small area of the Northern Province (now Limpopo), at 1,000 m, where the plants grew scattered in mixed deciduous woodland, mainly on cliffs. SANBI classes it as a savanna species: the plants lived in low, open, deciduous woodland, on rock faces, in full sun. The only known population was on communal land, outside any protected area. PlantZAfrica places it in the Limpopo Drakensberg, along the mountain range north and east of Penge, at about 1,000 m.

PlantZAfrica recalls that the fleshy seed coat of cycads is eaten and the seeds dispersed by animals, mainly birds, rodents, small mammals and fruit-eating bats, and that insects, beetles and bees, are the pollinators in the wild; SANBI notes that dispersal in cycads is poorly known and generally limited. The climate of the site, the nature of the rock and soils and the actual pollinators of this species are not documented.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos nubimontanus is Extinct in the Wild (EW) on the IUCN Red List, according to the assessment by J.D. Bösenberg (2022) cited by The World List of Cycads and SANBI; GBIF gives the same category, and SANBI states that the species has been in this category since 2006. The cause is poaching for horticulture and ornament; SANBI also reports collection for traditional medicine, according to the 2006 assessment (Rogers and Pillay).

In South Africa, all Encephalartos species are specially protected in Limpopo (Limpopo Environmental Management Act of 2003), and the collection of wild cycads has been banned nationally since 2007, then under Government Notice 371 of 2012. No collecting permit is reported ever to have been issued for this species. Between 1995 and 2020, 4,092 specimens (plants and seeds) were legally exported from South Africa; SANBI considers it likely that this trade encouraged the plundering of wild plants to build up parent stock. Eight South African nurseries are registered with the CITES Secretariat for this species, and 52 artificially propagated parent plants were recorded in the national nursery audit. Since the 2023 finding, only these registered nurseries may export seedlings, under conditions.

Regulation, according to Species+ (consulted through its public API on 26 October 2026):

  • CITES: Appendix I since 4 February 1977, like the whole genus;
  • European Union: Annex A, current list in Regulation (EU) 2026/1383; negative opinion of the EU Scientific Review Group on artificially propagated plants from South Africa (source codes A and D), current since 7 February 2013; a new consultation covering the whole genus has been open since 16 September 2026.

Artificially propagated plants can be traded under conditions, with the required permits. These notes do not replace the official texts: check Species+ and the national authorities before any purchase or import, and ask for proof that a plant was propagated in cultivation; be wary of large plants, which SANBI considers often of wild origin.

Growing Encephalartos nubimontanus

Encephalartos nubimontanus is one of the easier blue cycads to grow, and it is well represented in collections. SANBI calls it common in cultivation, with many artificially propagated parent plants in nurseries. PlantZAfrica describes it as hardy, drought-resistant and fast-growing, doing well in full sun or semi-shade, in a well-drained soil with sufficient moisture and frost-free conditions, and suited to subtropical and warm temperate regions. It prefers a sheltered position, as wind damages the leaves. For planting, PlantZAfrica advises keeping the soil at its original level around the base of the plant, mixing in a little slow-release balanced fertiliser and bone meal, soaking the plant once after planting and then letting the soil dry out between waterings, since too much water causes rot; dead leaves should be removed only once completely dry. The roots are not aggressive, so the plant can be placed almost anywhere in the garden.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

In southern California, Jungle Music recommends full sun, or partial sun, on the coast and inland, partial sun or filtered light in desert areas, good drainage and mound planting in gardens with regular irrigation; it describes the species as a good but not fast grower, often holding three sets of leaves at a time, and suggests placing it towards the front of a planting with 1 to 1.5 m of space. In Mediterranean and comparable climates, the same rules apply: a sunny, sheltered position, a raised bed or slope, and a dry winter.

Container cultivation

PlantZAfrica considers it a decorative container plant. In Europe, the only documented results concern pots: a grower in Ravenna (Emilia-Romagna, Italy) reports on the Tropicamente forum that a young seedling grown in a pot, which had produced two leaves in 2011, produced none in the summer of 2012, then a new one in 2013; he keeps his cycads in pots, sheltered from rain in winter. A deep pot with a gritty, free-draining mix, full sun in summer and a cool, dry, frost-free winter suits this approach.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

BGCI PlantSearch lists 16 institutions holding the species. In the United States they are Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden (Miami), the Huntington (San Marino), Ganna Walska Lotusland (Santa Barbara), the San Diego Botanic Garden (Encinitas), the San Diego Zoo, the Atlanta Botanical Garden, the University of Connecticut's plant conservatory (Storrs), the National Tropical Botanical Garden and the University of Hawaii at Hilo. In Australia, the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney lists it in its online catalogue, and Brisbane Botanic Gardens Mount Coot-tha and James Cook University (Townsville) declare it to BGCI. No British garden declares the species, and the Montgomery Botanical Center's catalogue lists no accession. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not a living plant on display today. Jungle Music only occasionally has plants for sale, seedlings or, more rarely, larger plants from private collections, and considers it always expensive and, after Encephalartos dolomiticus, one of the hardest of the Transvaal species to acquire.

Propagation

PlantZAfrica notes that the species is easily propagated from seed. Seeds are collected when the coat turns red-orange and cleaned with a table knife or after soaking in water for two to three days; fertile seeds sink in a bucket of water, infertile ones float. They are sown fresh, at any time of year, level with the surface of clean, coarse sand, with bottom heat of 25 to 28 °C (77 to 82 °F) in a mist house. An alternative is to sow them under a tree, in light shade, under fallen leaves, watering twice a week: germination begins after about two months, and seedlings can be potted after ten months into 4-litre bags of compost and river sand, then planted out in summer. Offsets are removed in early spring with a sharp, clean tool; the cuts are treated with fungicide, rooted offsets are potted at once and unrooted ones are left to callus in a cool, dry place for a few weeks. SANBI points out that seedlings for export must come from legally acquired parent plants.

Pests and diseases

PlantZAfrica lists white scale, woolly aphids and termites as pests. Like other cycads, the species is most at risk from rot of the roots and trunk in a waterlogged substrate, which PlantZAfrica attributes to excess water.

Cold hardiness

No cold-hardiness value has been published for Encephalartos nubimontanus. PlantZAfrica states that frost may burn the leaves but will not kill established plants, and recommends frost-free conditions; no temperature or frost duration is given. The natural site lay at about 1,000 m in north-eastern South Africa, but its winter temperatures are not documented. As with other Encephalartos, survivals in cold areas often concern young plants with an underground caudex, protected by the thermal inertia of the soil and sometimes by snow; an adult plant with an aerial trunk, as this species develops, does not benefit from this protection.

Mediterranean and European experience

No result of planting out has been published in France or Italy. On the Italian forum Tropicamente, growers in Ravenna and Apulia listed nubimontanus among the species that “should resist” cold, without any dated trial or measured value; these opinions are not reliable evidence. For readers in Mediterranean or comparable climates, such as coastal California or southern Australia, outdoor planting should be treated as a trial in the mildest, driest sites.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No measured value has been published for these countries, and the gardens that hold the species do not give any. No USDA zone is documented for the species in a reliable source.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data have been published for Encephalartos nubimontanus, but the risk is documented in the genus: the toxicity of Encephalartos horridus was demonstrated as early as 1944 (Van der Walt), and Encephalartos contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; these substances affect the liver, the digestive tract and the nervous system, and the seeds are the most dangerous part. For another cycad, the sago palm (Cycas revoluta), the ASPCA reports vomiting, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses. Wear gloves to handle seeds and their fleshy coat, pick up fallen seeds and keep plants and seeds out of reach of children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

SANBI reports that the species was collected for traditional medicine, according to the 2006 Red List assessment, and points out that in South Africa collectors and healers do not distinguish between cycad species. The nature of this use is not detailed. PlantZAfrica adds that in South Africa cycads in general are traditionally grown around the homestead as a charm against lightning and evil spirits.

Frequently asked questions

Does Encephalartos nubimontanus still exist in the wild?

No. No plant has been found since 2010–2011, and the species is assessed as Extinct in the Wild. It survives only in collections and nurseries, and SANBI does not rule out a future reintroduction.

Are Encephalartos nubimontanus and Encephalartos venetus the same plant?

Yes. Encephalartos venetus, described in 1996, is a synonym; the 1995 name, nubimontanus, has priority.

How can it be told from Encephalartos cupidus?

By its habit and cones. Encephalartos nubimontanus has an aerial trunk up to 2.5 m long and curved, often toothed leaflets; Encephalartos cupidus is a dwarf with a mainly underground stem. The female cones of nubimontanus have drawn-out sterile scales at the top.

What does nubimontanus mean?

“Of the cloud mountains”. The name translates that of the Wolkberg, the Limpopo range where the species grew.

Is it frost-hardy?

Only partly. PlantZAfrica says frost may burn the leaves but will not kill established plants, and recommends frost-free conditions; no measured value has been published. In frost-prone climates, keep it in a pot, dry and frost-free in winter.

Is it easy to grow?

Yes, for a blue cycad. PlantZAfrica describes it as hardy, drought-resistant and fast-growing in full sun or semi-shade and a well-drained soil, provided it is not overwatered.

Can it be bought legally?

Yes, under conditions. Only artificially propagated plants may be traded, with CITES permits; the European Union maintains a negative opinion on imports from South Africa. Be wary of large plants, often of wild origin according to SANBI.
