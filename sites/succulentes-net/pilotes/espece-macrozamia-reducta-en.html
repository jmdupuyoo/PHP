Macrozamia reducta is a cycad from New South Wales, in eastern Australia, that forms dense stands in dry eucalypt woodland from the western suburbs of Newcastle west to near Mudgee and south to Glen Davis. A species of the genus Macrozamia, it looks like a scaled-down Macrozamia communis: the same habit, the same straight leaves and spiny leaflets, but smaller leaves, cones and seeds. Collectors knew it as the “dwarf communis” or “Cessnock dwarf” before it was described in 1998. It is not threatened.

Key facts about Macrozamia reducta

  • Accepted name: Macrozamia reducta K.D.Hill & D.L.Jones (1998), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and the Australian Plant Census
  • Former names and synonyms: no synonym in current use; plants formerly included in Macrozamia communis, called “Cessnock dwarf” by collectors; the Australian Plant Census lists the old name Macrozamia tridentata f. wallsendensis as a synonym
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales; section Macrozamia, Macrozamia communis group
  • Common names: no established English name; “dwarf communis” and “Cessnock dwarf” are collectors' names, still used on the labels of the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney
  • Natural range: endemic to New South Wales, from the western suburbs of Newcastle west almost to Mudgee and south to Glen Davis
  • IUCN status: Least Concern (LC), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated September 2020 and published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union; no listing under Australian threatened-species law (Atlas of Living Australia)
  • Documented hardiness: no cultivation data; about 28 frosty days a year in its habitat at Cessnock (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, dry eucalypt woodland on sandy soils over sandstone ridges; light shade recommended in cultivation (PACSOA)
  • Habit and adult size: stem usually underground, rarely up to 0.4 m tall; 12 to 40 leaves 70 to 150 cm long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: grown at the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney, the Australian Botanic Garden Mount Annan and Montgomery Botanical Center (accessions of 2023); declared to BGCI by 10 institutions
  • Toxicity: not documented for the species; seeds and leaves toxic throughout the genus (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Macrozamia reducta

It is recognised by its straight, flat leaves, not twisted, whose lowest leaflets shrink into spines, as in Macrozamia communis, but on a smaller scale. The Flora of Australia (Hill, 1998), online through the ABRS, and the flora of New South Wales (PlantNET) describe it as follows.

  • Stem: underground, rarely aerial, up to 0.4 m tall and 20 to 40 cm across.
  • Leaves: 12 to 40 per crown, 70 to 150 cm long, flat in section, the axis not twisted; petiole 13 to 30 cm long.
  • Leaflets: 75 to 120 per leaf, pale to mid-green, semi-glossy, much paler beneath, simple, 16 to 32 cm long and 5 to 9 mm wide, with flat margins and a pungent tip; the lowest ones shrink into spines.
  • Male cones: spindle-shaped, 25 to 33 cm long and 6.5 to 7.5 cm across, the scales ending in a spine 2 to 25 mm long.
  • Female cones: ovoid, 16 to 23 cm long and 9 to 12 cm across, the scales ending in a spine 5 to 70 mm long.
  • Seeds: ovoid, 24 to 30 mm long and 20 to 26 mm wide, with red flesh.

Observers for the Palm and Cycad Societies of Australia (PACSOA) add a few field details: leaves mid-green at first and dark green later, erect at first and then spreading; leaflets angled forward at about 45° to the axis and lying in one plane; and a conspicuous creamy-white to yellowish callus where they join the axis, fading with age. Females usually carry a single cone, sometimes two; males one to four.

Hybrids

Yes: according to the PACSOA, Macrozamia reducta hybridises in the wild with two species. In the Cessnock area, where large stands of both occur, it crosses with Macrozamia flexuosa, a species of section Parazamia with twisted leaves; the PACSOA illustrates a leaf of Macrozamia reducta showing characters of Macrozamia flexuosa. In the Mudgee area, hybrid plants are found where Macrozamia reducta and Macrozamia secunda grow close together. Seeds collected in these contact zones may therefore give hybrid plants.

A twisted leaf axis on its own does not prove hybridisation, however. Stephen Bell (2019), in his review of Macrozamia flexuosa, notes that young plants of Macrozamia reducta and Macrozamia communis occasionally show a twisted, flexuous habit, which may explain a few doubtful records of Macrozamia flexuosa. Adult plants of pure Macrozamia reducta have a straight axis (Flora of Australia; PACSOA).

Possible confusion

The main confusion is with Macrozamia communis, from which Macrozamia reducta was split in 1998. According to the PACSOA, it is “in virtually every respect” a miniature Macrozamia communis, with shorter leaves and leaflets and smaller cones and seeds; the Flora of Australia likewise separates it by its smaller stature, shorter leaves and smaller cones and seeds. Outside its range and without a known origin, telling a young Macrozamia reducta from a young Macrozamia communis is very difficult: on the French Fous de palmiers forum, a grower pointed out as early as 2010 that many plants sold as Macrozamia communis before this revision are not necessarily that species.

The species of section Parazamia that live in the same region, such as Macrozamia flexuosa, are recognised by their twisted leaves and the absence of spiny leaflets.

Taxonomy

Macrozamia reducta is accepted by The World List of Cycads and the Australian Plant Census. Ken D. Hill and David L. Jones described it in 1998 in volume 48 of the Flora of Australia (p. 718). The type, collected on 25 February 1996 by K.D. Hill, L. Stanberg and K.L. Wilson (no. 4804) 5 km east of Kearsley, near Cessnock, is held at the National Herbarium of New South Wales in Sydney (NSW), with duplicates in New York (NY), Kew (K), Canberra (CANB), Brisbane (BRI) and Melbourne (MEL).

Before 1998, the plants were simply included in Macrozamia communis, itself described by L.A.S. Johnson in 1959. The species belongs to section Macrozamia and to the Macrozamia communis group, whose female cone scales have spines broad at the base (5 to 12 mm), according to Jones, Forster and Sharma (2001). For the reader, this means that an old “Macrozamia communis” label on a plant from the Newcastle, Cessnock or Mudgee areas may well refer to Macrozamia reducta.

Etymology and history

The name comes from the Latin reductus, “reduced”, after the smaller stature of the species compared with Macrozamia communis, with which it had been confused (Flora of Australia; Haynes, 2022, as given by The World List of Cycads). Before its description, cycad enthusiasts called it “dwarf communis” or “Macrozamia Cessnock dwarf” (PACSOA).

Common names

No established English name is attested. “Dwarf communis” and “Cessnock dwarf” are collectors' names that predate the description of the species; the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney and the Australian Botanic Garden Mount Annan still label it Macrozamia “Cessnock Dwarf”. No Aboriginal name specific to the species is attested.

In the wild

Macrozamia reducta is endemic to New South Wales. It is locally abundant in dry eucalypt woodland, on sandy soils over sandstone ridges, from the western suburbs of Newcastle west almost to Mudgee and south to Glen Davis (Flora of Australia; PlantNET). According to the PACSOA, it is the only cycad of New South Wales whose range runs from the coast and near-coastal areas to the inland slopes of the Great Dividing Range.

The PACSOA distinguishes two kinds of habitat. Around Cessnock, the plant forms several very dense stands on flat, poor, clay-based soil under eucalypts, with a thick understorey of Bursaria. Inland, it grows on undulating hillsides in poor, rocky soil; the plants there are smaller, with fewer leaves, and do not form dense stands. Cones appear regularly on the coast and near it, much less often inland.

At Cessnock, at 62 m, the average rainfall is 769 mm a year, on 102 days, over 60% of it in summer and autumn. The extreme temperatures reached at least once a week in July and January are −0.4 °C (31.3 °F) and 36.1 °C (97.0 °F), and frost occurs on 28 days a year on average (PACSOA).

Conservation status and regulation

Macrozamia reducta is not threatened. The IUCN Red List classes it as Least Concern (LC), in the assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated 11 September 2020 and published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, GBIF and Crossref, checked on 26 October 2026); an earlier assessment, by K.D. Hill, was published in 2010. Published sources describe it as locally abundant, and the Atlas of Living Australia shows no national or state listing for it.

Like other Macrozamia, it has been listed in CITES Appendix II since 4 February 1977, with annotation #4: seeds and pollen are exempt, but plants are not. In the European Union it falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026 (Species+, checked on 26 October 2026). Check the regulations in force before any purchase or import, and prefer seed-raised plants of known origin.

Growing Macrozamia reducta

Macrozamia reducta is little grown outside Australia, and the only cultivation advice published is that of the PACSOA: a lightly shaded, well-drained position; the species is said to be drought and frost tolerant, without figures. In the wild it grows under eucalypts, which points the same way: filtered light and a poor but free-draining soil.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Light: light shade, according to the PACSOA.
  • Soil: poor and free-draining, like the sands over sandstone of its ridges.
  • Water: no data specific to the species, whose habitat receives most of its rain in summer and autumn. At the Jardin zoologique tropical in La Londe-les-Maures, in the Var (southern France), experience with other species of the genus shows that watering once a week in summer, when there is no rain, is a minimum and twice a week ideal. This Mediterranean experience, gained with other Macrozamia, is useful for gardens with a similar climate, such as coastal California or southern Australia.

Container cultivation

No cultivation experience has been published in Europe. On the Fous de palmiers forum, a member reported in August 2026 having found seed of the species and wondered how long it would take to form a crown of leaves, with no result reported so far. Its smaller size than Macrozamia communis makes it, in theory, a better candidate for a large pot, but this remark rests on no published trial.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney's Garden Explorer lists two accessions of wild origin, from 1993 and 2022, and the Australian Botanic Garden Mount Annan two plants of 1993 in bed 14b and nine accessions of wild origin received in 2023 (updated 1 and 15 October 2026). The Australian National Botanic Gardens also declare it to BGCI.
  • United States: Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida) holds seven accessions in its online catalogue, all numbered 2023. Marie Selby Botanical Gardens (Sarasota), Naples Botanical Garden and San Diego Botanic Garden also declare it to BGCI.
  • United Kingdom: no British garden declares it to BGCI.

Ten institutions in all declare the species to BGCI (checked on 26 October 2026), the others in China, the Czech Republic and France. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not necessarily a living plant on display today. No English-language grower's report has been found.

Propagation

Macrozamia reducta is propagated by seed. Fresh, fertilised seed is needed, which means a male and a female plant, and hand pollination outside Australia. Seeds from areas where the species grows alongside Macrozamia flexuosa or Macrozamia secunda may give hybrids (see Hybrids). Wear gloves to clean off the flesh of the seeds, which is toxic.

Pests and diseases

No pest or disease specific to Macrozamia reducta is documented. As for other cycads, too much water in winter and scale insects are the risks most often mentioned for the genus.

Cold hardiness

The cold hardiness of Macrozamia reducta has not been documented by cultivation trials anywhere. The PACSOA calls it frost tolerant without a figure, and no source gives a USDA zone for it.

Mediterranean and European experience

No cultivation result is documented in Europe, in the ground or in a pot. The results obtained with Macrozamia communis, its close relative, cannot simply be transferred. In 2014, a member of the Fous de palmiers forum relayed the ranking of an Australian specialist, who placed Macrozamia reducta among the “relatively hardy” species of New South Wales, without giving a temperature. Without data, a pot overwintered under cover remains the prudent choice.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

In its habitat at Cessnock, the species experiences about 28 frosty days a year, with winter minima of around −0.4 °C (31.3 °F) reached every week in July (PACSOA); the inland populations, towards Mudgee, live in a more continental climate, but no figures are documented for them. These are climate data, not measurements of cold tolerance in cultivation.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data specific to Macrozamia reducta have been published, but the risk is well documented for the genus and for its close relative. Macrozamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a poison of the liver (Healy, 1967). Their leaves, above all the young shoots, cause irreversible hind-leg paralysis in cattle; in New South Wales, steers on the mid-north coast died in 2006 of acute liver damage after grazing cycad leaves, probably Macrozamia, the species not being identified (Glassop and Gill, 2007).

Treat the plant as toxic: wear gloves to handle the seeds, pick up those that fall and keep them out of reach of children, dogs and cats. In case of ingestion, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use specific to Macrozamia reducta is attested. Asmussen (2011) lists it only among the species whose use is possible, because they grow in regions where nineteenth-century accounts describe the eating of Macrozamia seeds: around Newcastle, in 1836, the seeds were roasted, pounded and then leached in water for two to three weeks (Backhouse, 1843). The species eaten is not identified. Never eat cycad seeds.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between Macrozamia reducta and Macrozamia communis?

Mainly size. Macrozamia reducta has shorter leaves (70 to 150 cm), shorter leaflets and smaller cones and seeds, but the same architecture: straight axis, lowest leaflets reduced to spines, red seeds. The two species were separated only in 1998.

Where does the name “Cessnock dwarf” come from?

From Australian collectors. Before its description, enthusiasts used that name, or “dwarf communis”, for this smaller Macrozamia from around Cessnock, near Newcastle (PACSOA).

Does Macrozamia reducta hybridise?

Yes. According to the PACSOA, it crosses with Macrozamia flexuosa near Cessnock and with Macrozamia secunda near Mudgee. A twisted axis on a young plant is not enough to prove it, though: young pure plants occasionally have one (Bell, 2019).

Is it frost-hardy?

Nobody knows precisely. No trial has been published; its habitat has moderate frosts, about thirty days a year at Cessnock, and the PACSOA calls it frost tolerant. Grow it in a pot and shelter it in winter until data are available.

Is it endangered?

No. The IUCN lists it as Least Concern (2022), and it remains locally abundant. Like all cycads, it is covered by CITES.

Is it poisonous?

Probably, like all cycads. No study covers this species, but the seeds and young leaves of Macrozamia are toxic to people and livestock.
