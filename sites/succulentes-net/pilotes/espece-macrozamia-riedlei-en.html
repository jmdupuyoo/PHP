Macrozamia riedlei is a cycad from the south-west of Western Australia, where it forms the understorey of the jarrah forests (Eucalyptus marginata) from Dwellingup to Albany. A species of the genus Macrozamia, it usually has no visible trunk and carries large, flat, glossy leaves; it was long known as Cycas riedlei and sometimes as Macrozamia fraseri, with which it was confused. Simply called “zamia” in Western Australia, it is famous on two counts: its seeds poisoned the first European sailors who tasted them and cause livestock poisoning, yet the Noongar ate their red flesh after a long treatment that made it edible.

Key facts about Macrozamia riedlei

  • Accepted name: Macrozamia riedlei (Fisch. ex Gaudich.) C.A.Gardner (1930), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and the Australian Plant Census
  • Former names and synonyms: Cycas riedlei Fisch. ex Gaudich. (1829), the basionym; Macrozamia preissii Lehm. (1844); Encephalartos preissii (Lehm.) F.Muell.; Encephalartos oldfieldii Miq. (1863); Macrozamia oldfieldii (Miq.) A.DC. (1868)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales; section Macrozamia
  • Common names: “Zamia” (FloraBase), “Zamia Palm” (Australian Plant Census); “by-yu” in Noongar for the seed with its flesh (Asmussen, 2011)
  • Natural range: endemic to south-western Western Australia, from Dwellingup to Albany and west to the coast
  • IUCN status: Least Concern (LC), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated August 2020 and published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II (annotation #4); Annex B in the European Union; “not threatened” according to the Western Australian Herbarium
  • Documented hardiness: no measured value; young plants spent the winter of 2008–2009 outdoors in Naples without damage, according to a grower, whereas a small plant tried outdoors near Paris died in the winter of 2011–2012 (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, the understorey of jarrah forest, on lateritic soils
  • Habit and adult size: usually without an aerial trunk (stem 0 to 0.3 m tall, 25 to 40 cm across); 12 to 30 leaves 1.2 to 2.2 m long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: grown at the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney (accession of 1979) and Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria; declared to BGCI by 12 institutions, including the Huntington, Descanso Gardens and the San Diego Zoo
  • Toxicity: seeds highly toxic to people and livestock; the red flesh is the part richest in macrozamin (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Macrozamia riedlei

It is recognised by its clump of large leaves, flat or barely keeled, mid- to dark green and often highly glossy, rising from a stem that is almost always underground. The Flora of Australia (Hill, 1998), online through the ABRS, describes it as follows.

  • Stem: underground, rarely aerial, 0 to 0.3 m tall and 25 to 40 cm across.
  • Leaves: 12 to 30 per crown, 1.21 to 2.2 m long, flat or slightly keeled, semi-glossy to highly glossy; petiole 18 to 50 cm long.
  • Leaflets: 92 to 150 per leaf, simple, 23 to 48 cm long and 7.5 to 11 mm wide, with flat margins and a pungent tip; the leaflets at the base of the leaf shrink into spines.
  • Male cones: spindle-shaped, 29 to 41 cm long and 11 to 15 cm across, the scales ending in a spine 8 to 75 mm long.
  • Female cones: ovoid, 25 to 35 cm long and 14 to 18 cm across.
  • Seeds: ovoid, 35 to 45 mm long and 25 to 35 mm wide, with red flesh (sarcotesta).

The Western Australian Herbarium (FloraBase) sums up the species in a few words: a cycad 0.5 to 3 m high, usually trunkless, with few, glossy, flat or openly keeled leaves, narrow leaflets and short cones. According to L.A.S. Johnson (1959), drawing on the observations of A. Baird, the aerial part of the trunk develops mainly on shallow soils, where the contractile roots cannot pull the stem down.

Hybrids

No natural or garden hybrid of Macrozamia riedlei has been reported. The species is geographically isolated from the Macrozamia of eastern Australia.

Possible confusion

In Western Australia, Macrozamia riedlei can be confused with the two other species of the state, Macrozamia fraseri and Macrozamia dyeri. According to the Flora of Australia, it is smaller than both, with fewer but glossier and flatter leaves, smaller cones and, only rarely, an aerial trunk. For much of the twentieth century these plants were grouped under the single name Macrozamia riedlei (see Taxonomy): older records of the species, notably on the Perth coastal plain or towards Esperance, may therefore concern one of the other two.

Outside Australia, the leaves of Macrozamia riedlei look very much like those of Macrozamia communis. Johnson (1959) noted that its leaflets are usually even more rigid and bear stomata on the upper surface, which separates them under the microscope. On the French Fous de palmiers forum, growers stress that a young potted plant whose leaves have been cut is very hard to identify: buy seed-raised plants of known origin.

Taxonomy

Macrozamia riedlei is accepted by The World List of Cycads and the Australian Plant Census. The species was published in 1829 by Charles Gaudichaud-Beaupré, as Cycas riedlei, in the botanical part of the account of the voyage of the Uranie, taking up a herbarium name of Fischer's. The type came from King George Sound, near Albany, and is held in Paris. Gaudichaud already suspected that the plant was closer to Zamia than to Cycas.

The botanist Charles A. Gardner moved it to Macrozamia in 1930, in his Enumeratio Plantarum Australiae Occidentalis, spelling it “reidlei”. That work, a simple census, contains no description: the combination appears as “M. Reidlei (Gaud.) Gardner, comb. nov.”, citing the author of the basionym, Gaudichaud, but not the basionym itself; The World List of Cycads explains that the combination is linked to it indirectly in this way. Gardner placed Macrozamia fraseri in synonymy under it, but kept Macrozamia dyeri as a distinct species. Another problem was settled recently: there was an older homonym, Cycas riedlei Dum.Cours. In 2022, Michael Calonje and James Clugston proposed conserving Gaudichaud's name (Taxon 71: 1113–1115); the Nomenclature Committee for Vascular Plants approved the conservation in 2024 by 16 votes to 1, then the General Committee by 25 votes to 2 (The World List of Cycads). For the reader, this means that the name Macrozamia riedlei and its authorship remain unchanged.

The history of the species is tied to that of the genus. According to Johnson (1959), Miquel's description of Macrozamia in 1842 rested almost entirely on plates by Ferdinand Bauer that in fact showed Macrozamia riedlei, published as Macrozamia spiralis; Miquel himself acknowledged this in 1863. Johnson concluded that the type species of the genus was, biologically, the plant of those plates. Johnson went further than Gardner and united all the Western Australian populations under Macrozamia riedlei, with Macrozamia fraseri and Encephalartos dyeri as synonyms, while expressing reservations for lack of field experience. The Flora of Australia (Hill, 1998) separated the three species again, and The World List of Cycads now accepts Macrozamia fraseri and Macrozamia dyeri as distinct species. Macrozamia riedlei belongs to section Macrozamia, the large species whose lowest leaflets are reduced to spines.

Etymology and history

The epithet honours Anselme Riedlé (1775–1801), head gardener at the Jardin des plantes in Paris, who sailed with Nicolas Baudin's expedition to Australia (1800–1804) and collected the type of the species, according to Haynes's etymological compendium (2022) as given by The World List of Cycads. The spelling of the name has varied a great deal: Gardner wrote “reidlei”, Miquel and Schuster “riedleyi”, Regel “riedeli”; Johnson (1959) pointed out that Gaudichaud had spelt it correctly, “riedlei”.

The species was among the first Australian plants known to Europeans, because of its toxicity. In January 1697, during Willem de Vlamingh's Dutch expedition, near present-day Perth, the expedition's surgeon and five men ate zamia kernels; about three hours later they were seized with vomiting so violent that, according to the ship's journal, there was hardly any difference between them and death (Grose and Panegyres, 2021).

Common names

In Western Australia, the state herbarium (FloraBase) simply gives “Zamia”, the Australian Plant Census “Zamia Palm”, and agricultural publications speak of “zamia palm”. These names also apply to other Australian cycads and are easily confused with the American genus Zamia. Among the Noongar, according to the nineteenth-century vocabularies brought together by Asmussen (2011), “by-yu” meant the zamia seed with its flesh, and February–March was the “by-yu” season. The Atlas of Living Australia also lists “Kwininy” as a traditional name, without stating its language or source.

In the wild

Macrozamia riedlei is endemic to south-western Western Australia. According to the Flora of Australia, it ranges from Dwellingup to Albany and west to the coast, where it is widespread and abundant in the understorey of jarrah forest. FloraBase records it in four bioregions (Esperance Plains, Jarrah Forest, Swan Coastal Plain and Warren) and in more than thirty local government areas, some of them north and east of Perth, which suggests a slightly wider range than that of the Flora of Australia; the two sources were not updated at the same time. The species grows on lateritic soils.

Its reproductive cycle was described by A. Baird in 1939, as tabulated by Asmussen (2011): pollination takes place in September, fertilisation around February, and the seeds fall in March and April. FloraBase gives cones in September and October. The seeds are actively eaten and dispersed by animals, which obliged gatherers to collect the cones quickly (Burbidge and Whelan, 1982, as cited by Asmussen). The pollinators of the species are not documented in the Flora of Australia or FloraBase.

Conservation status and regulation

Macrozamia riedlei is not threatened. The IUCN Red List classes it as Least Concern (LC), in the assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated 20 August 2020 and published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, GBIF and Crossref, checked on 26 October 2026). FloraBase lists it as “Not threatened” in Western Australia. The species is common enough for efforts to re-establish it after mining, the subject of a paper by Douglas, Korcynskyj and Koch (2011) cited by the Flora of Australia.

Like other Macrozamia, it has been listed in CITES Appendix II since 4 February 1977, with annotation #4: seeds and pollen are exempt, but plants are not. In the European Union it falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026 (Species+, checked on 26 October 2026). Check the regulations in force before any purchase or import, and prefer seed-raised plants.

Growing Macrozamia riedlei

Macrozamia riedlei is rare in cultivation and published data are thin: no reliable source gives cultivation advice specific to the species. The best guide is its habitat, the understorey of open forest on poor, well-drained lateritic soil, under a climate with winter rainfall.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Light: the understorey of open forest suggests sun to light shade; no recommendation specific to the species has been published.
  • Soil: poor and very free-draining.
  • Water: the species is grown at the Jardin zoologique tropical in La Londe-les-Maures, in the Var (southern France). The garden's experience with Macrozamia shows that watering once a week in summer, when there is no rain, is a minimum and twice a week ideal. This Mediterranean experience is useful for gardens with a similar climate, such as coastal California or southern Australia.
  • Winter: at the same garden, a dry frost proves less dangerous than the same frost after a spell of rain, when soil and plant are waterlogged.

Container cultivation

The few amateur reports concern young plants. A grower on the Italian Tropicamente forum noted in 2010 that, of two seedlings bought in Germany, one died within a month and the other was languishing; another member observed that germinating seeds rot easily. On the Fous de palmiers forum, a grower mentions small plants whose tuber was no bigger than a hazelnut. In practice, a very free-draining mix and a young plant protected from excess water seem essential.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

  • Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney's Garden Explorer lists one plant of wild origin, from an accession of 1979, in bed 102i, labelled “zamia palm” (updated 1 October 2026). The census of Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria, downloaded on 19 October 2026, records three plants at Cranbourne, planted in 2012, 2013 and 2014, one of them of wild origin. The University of Melbourne's System Garden also declares it to BGCI.
  • United States: the Huntington (San Marino), Descanso Gardens (La Cañada Flintridge), the Gardens of Golden Gate Park (San Francisco), the San Diego Zoo and Montgomery Botanical Center declare it to BGCI; the species does not appear in Montgomery's online catalogue.
  • United Kingdom: no British garden declares it to BGCI.

Twelve institutions in all declare the species to BGCI (checked on 26 October 2026), the others in the Czech Republic, France, Germany and Switzerland. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not necessarily a living plant on display today. No English-language grower's report that could be read in full has been found.

Propagation

Macrozamia riedlei is propagated by seed. Fresh, fertilised seed is needed; outside Australia, hand pollination is usually necessary. Germination seems tricky: a grower on Tropicamente obtained only 3 seedlings from 10 seeds in 2010, and another none from 10, with rot at germination. Wear gloves to remove the red flesh, which is highly toxic.

Pests and diseases

No pest or disease specific to Macrozamia riedlei is documented. Growers' reports mention above all rot of the roots and of germinating seeds when the mix stays wet.

Cold hardiness

The cold hardiness of Macrozamia riedlei is poorly known: no measured value has been published anywhere, and the published reports are few and incomplete: a winter without damage in Naples and a failure near Paris, without temperatures. No source gives a USDA zone for it.

Mediterranean and European experience

On the Italian Tropicamente forum, a grower in Naples reported in October 2009 that young seed-raised plants, three or four years old, had spent the previous winter outdoors without problems; he gave neither the minimum temperature nor whether they were in pots or protected. Further north, a grower in the suburbs of Paris (Île-de-France, France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum that his Macrozamia riedlei, a small plant a few centimetres across tried outdoors since the previous winter, died during the winter of 2011–2012, marked by the cold spell of February 2012; he gives neither the temperature nor the protection used. On the French Mediterranean coast, the species is grown at the Jardin zoologique tropical in La Londe-les-Maures, but the garden has published no cold data for it. Without data, a pot overwintered safe from frost and rain remains the prudent choice.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No result with a temperature has been published. Its native south-west of Western Australia has a Mediterranean-type climate with winter rain; in Britain and frost-prone parts of the United States, it is a plant for a pot under glass in winter.

Toxicity and precautions

Yes, Macrozamia riedlei is highly toxic, its seeds above all. The account of Vlamingh's expedition (1697) shows that the kernel causes violent vomiting in people. In Western Australia, according to an article in the state's agriculture journal (Wilkinson, 1960), the zamia palm is one of the longest-known poisonous plants of Australia; it is common in the jarrah and karri forests and on the coastal plains. As early as 1894, a Western Australian agricultural publication devoted an article to the cattle disease called “rickets” or “wobbles” (Edwards, 1894, as cited by Asmussen, 2011).

Macrozamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a poison of the liver. Healy (1967) distinguished two forms of livestock poisoning: damage to the liver and gut from the seeds, and a progressive, irreversible paralysis of the hind legs in cattle that eat the leaves, above all the young shoots.

In Macrozamia riedlei, the distribution of the toxin within the seed is unusual. According to Ladd et al. (1993), as cited by Asmussen (2011), the red flesh contains about 3.88% macrozamin (fresh weight), one of the highest levels measured in cycads, whereas the kernel contains only about 0.4%, the lowest of the Macrozamia species analysed. No data on dogs and cats have been published for the species, but poisoning of pets by cycads is well known. Wear gloves to handle the seeds, pick up those that fall and keep them out of reach of children, dogs and cats. In case of ingestion, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

Despite its toxicity, Macrozamia riedlei was a valued food of the Noongar of south-western Australia. Asmussen's review (2011), based on written accounts from the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, shows a practice unique in Australia: whereas in the east of the continent the kernel of Macrozamia seeds was eaten, in Western Australia the red flesh (sarcotesta) was eaten and the kernel discarded, although the flesh is the part richest in macrozamin.

Three detoxification methods are described. The first is long soaking, sometimes in seawater: seeds were found hung from a stake on the beach or immersed in a shallow stream, for a few days to two weeks (Edwards, 1894; Hammond, 1933). The second is ageing: the seeds were buried for about a month, according to Drummond (1862). The third, described by George Grey (1841), combines the two: women gathered the seeds in March, soaked them for a few days in a pool, then buried them for two weeks in pits called “mordak”, an arm deep, lined with rushes and covered with sand and tufts of grass tree (Xanthorrhoea). The flesh was then eaten raw or roasted.

Europeans who tasted it found it pleasant: Salvado found it much to his liking, Mrs Hassell compared it to dates with an olive flavour, Hammond to a tomato after soaking in seawater; only Drummond found it rancid, like whale oil. The flesh was traded: Mrs Hassell, who lived at Jerramungup from 1878 to 1886, reports that it was threaded on rushes and brought in from inland. Grey also notes that some families had the right to gather “by-yu” on their neighbours' land during the season. This knowledge is not a recipe: incomplete preparation causes severe poisoning. Never eat cycad seeds.

Frequently asked questions

Are Macrozamia riedlei and Macrozamia fraseri the same plant?

No. They are two species accepted by The World List of Cycads, long grouped under the name Macrozamia riedlei. According to the Flora of Australia, Macrozamia riedlei is smaller, with fewer but glossier and flatter leaves, and smaller cones.

Can the seeds of Macrozamia riedlei be eaten?

No. Raw seeds cause violent vomiting, as Vlamingh's crew found in 1697. The Noongar ate only the flesh, after weeks of soaking and burial, a technique that must never be improvised.

Is Macrozamia riedlei dangerous to livestock and pets?

Yes. In Australia, the leaves cause irreversible hind-leg paralysis in cattle, and the seeds damage the liver. Keep seeds and plants out of reach of dogs and other animals.

Is it frost-hardy?

Little is known. No measured value has been published; the species is grown at the Jardin zoologique tropical in La Londe-les-Maures, in southern France, young plants came through a winter outdoors in Naples, but a small plant tried outdoors near Paris died in the winter of 2011–2012. Grow it in a pot and overwinter it safe from frost and rain.

Why is it called “zamia”?

By local habit. In Western Australia, “zamia” or “zamia palm” is the usual name for this cycad, although it does not belong to the genus Zamia, which is American.

Why is the name sometimes spelt “reidlei”?

Because of a spelling mistake. Gardner published the combination in 1930 as “reidlei”, but the species honours the gardener Anselme Riedlé, and Gaudichaud had spelt it correctly, “riedlei”.
