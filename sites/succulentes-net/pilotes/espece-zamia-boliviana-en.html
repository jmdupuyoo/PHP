Zamia boliviana is a small cycad with an underground stem from the Cerrado, the great savanna of central South America, found in Bolivia and in the Brazilian state of Mato Grosso. This species of the genus Zamia was first described in 1846 as Ceratozamia boliviana, a name still found in some sources, before being moved to Zamia in 1868. Unlike most South American Zamia, which are forest plants, it grows in open country with a long dry season. It has narrow leaflets and a petiole without prickles. The IUCN lists it as Least Concern.

How to recognise Zamia boliviana

Three characters together identify it: an underground stem, a petiole without prickles and narrow leaflets, no more than 2 cm wide. The figures below come from the comparative table published in 2019 by Rosane Segalla and Michael Calonje when they described the closely related Zamia brasiliensis:

  • Leaves: 1 to 3 per plant, up to 119 cm long; petiole unarmed, that is, without prickles.
  • Leaflets: 16 to 40 per leaf on adult plants, 19 to 29 cm long and 1.1 to 2 cm wide (0.8 to 2 cm in the text of the same paper).
  • Male cones: covered in a uniform creamy-yellow down; their scales bear 17 to 22 pollen sacs, gathered in a single group or barely separated in the middle.
  • Female cone: at maturity uniformly cream-tan tinged with green, or creamy yellow to tan with the centre of the scales dark brown; 5 to 16 rows of megasporophylls arranged in 6 or 7 vertical series, their outer face 18.1 to 21.9 mm by 10.6 to 14.6 mm.
  • Seeds: sclerotesta (the hard shell) 11.1 to 15.3 mm long and 7.2 to 10.2 mm wide.

Hybrids

No hybrid, natural or horticultural, has been documented.

Possible confusion

The two most similar species grow not far away, without their ranges overlapping (Segalla and Calonje, 2019):

  • Zamia brasiliensis, from Mato Grosso and Rondônia, further east: its petiole is also unarmed, but its leaflets are much wider (2.4 to 3.5 cm), its male cones have a sterile tip covered in orange-brown down, and its female cones have more vertical series of megasporophylls (8 to 14). The authors regard it as the closest relative of Zamia boliviana.
  • Zamia ulei, an Amazonian species: petiole armed with stout prickles, much larger leaves (up to 4 m) and leaflets 3 to 7 cm wide.

According to the same authors, unarmed petioles are very rare among mainland Zamia: in South America they are known only in Zamia encephalartoides, Zamia boliviana and Zamia brasiliensis.

Taxonomy

Zamia boliviana is accepted by The World List of Cycads and by GBIF; the World Checklist of Vascular Plants (WCVP), on which POWO is based, also accepts it (consulted through GBIF on 31 October 2026). The French botanist Adolphe Brongniart described it in 1846 as Ceratozamia boliviana, in the Annales des Sciences Naturelles (series 3, 5: 9), that is, in a genus now restricted to the cycads of Mexico and Central America (see the genus Ceratozamia). Alphonse de Candolle transferred it to Zamia in 1868, in the Prodromus (16(2): 540), under the combination accepted today. In 1891 Otto Kuntze renamed it Palmifolium bolivianum, part of an attempt, since abandoned, to replace the generic name Zamia. For the reader, the upshot is simple: Ceratozamia boliviana and Zamia boliviana are the same plant, and the correct name is the second.

Flora e Funga do Brasil, the official checklist of the Brazilian flora, also treats Zamia brongniartii Wedd. as a synonym of Zamia boliviana (consulted through GBIF); The World List of Cycads does not list that name.

For the type, The World List of Cycads refers to that of Brongniart’s basionym. Segalla and Calonje (2019) regard Zamia boliviana as the closest relative of Zamia brasiliensis, because of the unarmed petioles, the geographical proximity and the similar cones. In 2023, Segalla, Pimenta and Calonje counted it among the ten accepted Zamia of the Amazon basin, which they describe as “the least understood assemblage of species” in the genus, together with Zamia amazonum.

Etymology and history

The epithet refers to Bolivia, the country from which the species was described (Haynes, 2022). In recent years the species has been studied in detail in Brazil by Rosane Segalla and her colleagues (phenology, pollination, sex ratio), and The World List of Cycads holds many photographs taken in the wild between 2015 and 2018.

Common names

There is no English name, and no verified vernacular name is attested in Spanish or Portuguese.

In the wild

The World List of Cycads places the species in Bolivia (departments of El Beni, Cochabamba and Santa Cruz) and in Brazil (Mato Grosso); according to the English Wikipedia summary, which cites Segalla and colleagues (2022), it also grows in the department of La Paz, between 130 and 450 m, in the Cerrado ecoregion. The Brazilian studies describe scattered populations in patches of savanna under a markedly seasonal climate; in Mato Grosso the species has been recorded at Chapada dos Guimarães, Cáceres and Glória do Oeste.

Its reproductive biology is among the best known of any South American cycad:

  • Pollination: according to Segalla, Pinheiro and Morellato (2021), a single beetle, Pharaxonotha cerradensis, carries the pollen: it lives and breeds in the male cones and visits the female cones, in an obligate mutualism known as brood-site pollination.
  • Phenology: according to Segalla and colleagues (2022), males and females produce their cones during the dry season; a male cone lasts about 50 days and a female cone about 330 days, until the seeds are shed, also in the dry season.
  • Sex ratio: a 2021 study (Segalla and colleagues, Plant Ecology) found an effective sex ratio biased towards males in several populations.

Like all cycads, it forms coralloid roots that host nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria.

Conservation status and regulation

Zamia boliviana is listed as Least Concern (LC) on the IUCN Red List. The assessment is by Cristina López-Gallego: according to Crossref it is dated 1 June 2020, was published in 2022 and was reissued with corrections, and GBIF relays the category; the status is taken from The World List of Cycads, as the Red List itself could not be consulted. The authors of the Brazilian studies nevertheless call it a threatened species in the titles of two of their papers.

Like every Zamia except Zamia restrepoi, Zamia boliviana is listed in CITES Appendix II, where the genus has appeared since 4 February 1977. The listing carries annotation #4 (the version adopted at CoP20, in force since 5 March 2026): seeds, pollen, seedlings and tissue cultures obtained in vitro and transported in sterile containers, as well as cut flowers of artificially propagated plants, are not covered by CITES; international trade in the plants themselves requires an export permit. In the European Union the species falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, whose current annexes are set by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026.

This summary does not replace the official texts: check the regulations in force before any purchase or exchange, ask the seller for proof of origin and choose nursery-raised plants grown from seed.

Growing Zamia boliviana

No documented cultivation experience and no verified growing guidance specific to the species have been published. The figures that circulate online (proportion of mineral compost, pH, growing temperatures, hardiness zone) have no identifiable source and are not repeated here. Its habitat only tells us that it is a savanna plant, used to open ground and to a long dry season during which it produces its cones.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

No planting outside its range has been documented. The botanic gardens that hold it are in Florida, coastal California and tropical Queensland (see below), but the sources do not say whether their plants grow outdoors or under glass.

Container cultivation

In cool climates, the only cautious option is a pot kept frost-free and dry in winter, under glass or indoors; this is inferred from the habitat, not from published trials.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In short: a few American and Australian gardens; no British record.

  • United States: Montgomery Botanical Center, in Coral Gables (Florida), lists one accession in its online catalogue, number 981682, from 1998 according to the garden’s numbering; Ganna Walska Lotusland, in Santa Barbara (California), also declares the species to BGCI (both consulted on 24 October 2026).
  • Australia: Cairns Botanic Gardens (Queensland) declares the species to BGCI.
  • United Kingdom: no record in BGCI or in the British catalogues checked in October 2026. The British nursery Trebrown has a species page with a USDA zone 9b rating, its own commercial classification; the page gives no information on the plant itself.

A BGCI declaration shows that a garden held the plant at some point, not that it is alive today. No report from a private grower is documented.

Propagation

No cultivation data. The species has separate male and female plants, both needed for seed; in the wild pollination depends on the beetle Pharaxonotha cerradensis, so in cultivation it would have to be done by hand. Seeds are exempt from CITES under annotation #4.

Pests and diseases

No pest or disease of the species in cultivation is documented. In the wild, a short note by Segalla and Morellato (2019, in the journal Cycads) reports butterflies of the genus Eumaeus, whose caterpillars are known to feed on cycads, associated with Zamia boliviana; the note is known only from its title and was not read.

Cold hardiness

There is no measured cold-hardiness data for Zamia boliviana. It lives in tropical savanna between 130 and 450 m; the hardiness zone sometimes suggested for it online (USDA 10) is an estimate without observations behind it and is not repeated here.

Mediterranean and European experience

No cultivation result has been published from southern France or Italy, outdoors or in a pot.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No dated cultivation report is documented. Trebrown’s zone 9b rating is a commercial classification, not a measured result.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity study specific to Zamia boliviana has been published, but the risk is well documented in cycads: Zamia, like other cycads, contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a substance toxic to the liver and nervous system; the seeds are the most dangerous part. In the genus, the ASPCA lists Zamia furfuracea as poisonous to dogs, cats and horses (vomiting, bloody diarrhoea, liver damage). In the absence of evidence to the contrary, treat the plant as poisonous: keep seeds and leaves out of reach of children and animals, and wear gloves to clean seeds. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet straight away.

Traditional uses

No traditional use specific to the species is documented.

Frequently asked questions

Are Ceratozamia boliviana and Zamia boliviana the same plant?

Yes. Brongniart described it in 1846 as Ceratozamia boliviana; Alphonse de Candolle moved it to Zamia in 1868. The accepted name is Zamia boliviana.

How can I tell it from Zamia brasiliensis?

By leaflet width: 0.8 to 2 cm in Zamia boliviana, 2.4 to 3.5 cm in Zamia brasiliensis. The male cones also help: uniformly creamy yellow in the first, with an orange-brown tip in the second.

Where does it grow?

In the Cerrado savannas of Bolivia (El Beni, Cochabamba, Santa Cruz, La Paz) and of Mato Grosso, Brazil, between about 130 and 450 m.

What pollinates it?

A small beetle, Pharaxonotha cerradensis, which lives in the male cones and carries pollen to the female cones.

Is it hardy in the UK?

Nothing suggests so, and no measured data exist. In Britain it should be kept frost-free, under glass or indoors.

Is it poisonous?

It should be assumed so. No analysis exists for this species, but the cycads studied contain serious toxins, especially in their seeds.
