The genus Hesperaloe Engelm. brings together clump-forming succulent perennials with long, narrow, fibrous leaves, native to Texas and northern Mexico, where they grow on rocky slopes and in the scrublands of the Chihuahuan Desert. It belongs to the family Asparagaceae, subfamily Agavoideae, the group of agavoids that also includes agaves and yuccas (the former family Agavaceae). The genus has eight species, all North American, but only one is widely grown: Hesperaloe parviflora, the red yucca or hummingbird yucca, long known as Yucca parviflora and as Hesperaloe yuccifolia. Spineless, very drought-tolerant and cold-hardy, it flowers for much of the summer on tall red to pink stalks, which has made it a staple of dry gardens in warm-temperate climates.

  • Accepted name (POWO): Hesperaloe Engelm., published in 1871 in the botanical report of the Fortieth Parallel survey (S. Watson, Botany: 497)

  • Former names: the first species were described in Aloe (Aloe yuccifolia A.Gray) and in Yucca (Yucca parviflora Torr., Yucca funifera K.Koch)
  • Family: Asparagaceae, subfamily Agavoideae (formerly Agavaceae)
  • Common names: red yucca, hummingbird yucca, redflower false yucca, samandoque (for Hesperaloe parviflora); false yucca (sometimes for the whole genus)
  • Natural range: Texas to northern Mexico (Coahuila, Nuevo León, Tamaulipas, San Luis Potosí, Sonora), according to POWO and regional floras

Characteristics of the genus Hesperaloe

A Hesperaloe is recognised by its clump of long, narrow, arching leaves, with no trunk and no terminal spine, from which rise tall, slender flower stalks bearing racemes or panicles. The leaves are leathery and grey-green, and horticultural descriptions of Hesperaloe parviflora mention white fibres that peel away and curl along the margins. The plant does not form a stem: the clump slowly thickens and divides into new rosettes.

The flowers, tubular or bell-shaped with six tepals, vary a great deal between species:

  • coral red to pink, sometimes yellow in some cultivated forms, in Hesperaloe parviflora, whose flowers are visited by hummingbirds;
  • whitish to greenish in Hesperaloe funifera, the most massive species of the genus;
  • pale and night-opening in Hesperaloe nocturna;
  • bell-shaped in Hesperaloe campanulata, whose epithet means precisely "bell-shaped".

Unlike most agaves, a Hesperaloe clump does not die after flowering: it blooms again year after year. Horticultural sources disagree, however, on the fate of each individual rosette, some stating that a rosette flowers only once before being replaced by its offsets. The fruit is a capsule that releases flat black seeds.

Taxonomy and classification

Hesperaloe is a genus accepted by POWO, in the family Asparagaceae. It was formerly placed in the Agavaceae, a family that the APG classification merged into the Asparagaceae, where it now forms the subfamily Agavoideae.

From Aloe and Yucca to Hesperaloe

The first species were described in other genera, which explains the old names still found in the trade and in older literature. According to Greg Starr's revision (1997, Madroño):

  • George Engelmann created the genus in 1871 for a plant described shortly before by Asa Gray as Aloe yuccifolia, which became Hesperaloe yuccifolia (A.Gray) Engelm.;
  • John Torrey had already described a Yucca parviflora in 1859 which, according to Coulter (1894), was the same plant: the accepted name is therefore Hesperaloe parviflora (Torr.) J.M.Coult.;
  • Karl Koch had described a Yucca funifera in 1862, which William Trelease transferred to Hesperaloe in 1902 (Hesperaloe funifera (K.Koch) Trel.).

Place within the Agavoideae

Molecular studies place Hesperaloe close to Hesperoyucca, the genus of Hesperoyucca whipplei, long known as Yucca whipplei. A phylogenetic analysis of relationships within Yucca by Ayala-Hernández and colleagues (2025, Phytotaxa) found Hesperoyucca and Hesperaloe more closely related to each other than either is to Yucca, confirming earlier work. Despite the name, Hesperaloe is not closely related to the true aloes, which belong to another family, the Asphodelaceae.

How many species?

Eight, according to the most recent lists consulted, after thirty years of additions. Until 1967 the genus held only two or three species; Howard Scott Gentry then described Hesperaloe nocturna from Sonora. In 1997 Greg Starr added two species (Hesperaloe campanulata and Hesperaloe tenuifolia) and a subspecies, Hesperaloe funifera subsp. chiangii, which Billie L. Turner raised to species rank in 2002 as Hesperaloe chiangii. Hesperaloe chiangii therefore comes from Hesperaloe funifera, not from Yucca. Hesperaloe malacophylla Hochstätter & Mart.-Aval., from Tamaulipas, is the most recent addition. The status of Hesperaloe engelmannii was long debated: Guillot and Van der Meer (2006) still treated it as a variety (Hesperaloe parviflora var. engelmannii), whereas POWO accepts it as a species.

Etymology and history of the genus

The name Hesperaloe combines the Greek hesperos, "western", with aloe: it is the "aloe of the West", a reference to the resemblance of these plants to aloes and to their North American origin. The type species is the plant Engelmann called Hesperaloe yuccifolia, now Hesperaloe parviflora.

The recent history of the genus is also agronomic. From 1993, the University of Arizona ran trials to domesticate Hesperaloe funifera as a fibre crop for arid regions: its long, fine fibres were studied for the manufacture of speciality papers.

Common names

In English, Hesperaloe parviflora is called "red yucca", "hummingbird yucca", "redflower false yucca" or "samandoque"; the last name also applies to Hesperaloe funifera. "False yucca" is sometimes used for the whole genus. These names are misleading: Hesperaloe is neither a Yucca nor an aloe. No English common name has been found for the rarer species, which are known only by their scientific names.

Biogeography and ecology

The genus occupies a compact range straddling the United States–Mexico border. It lives mainly in the scrublands and rocky slopes of the Chihuahuan Desert and its margins, on thin, often limestone soils.

  • Texas: according to Turner and Turner (2002), Hesperaloe parviflora grows there mainly in the northern Chihuahuan Desert, with Larrea tridentata and Agave lechuguilla, whereas Hesperaloe engelmannii grows rather under oaks on the Edwards Plateau; a natural occurrence of Hesperaloe funifera in Texas has been suggested recently.
  • North-eastern Mexico: Hesperaloe funifera (Coahuila, northern Nuevo León), Hesperaloe campanulata (central Nuevo León), Hesperaloe chiangii (San Luis Potosí), Hesperaloe malacophylla (Tamaulipas).
  • Sonora: Hesperaloe nocturna, described from a single locality, and Hesperaloe tenuifolia, very localised in the south of the state, in pine-oak forest at about 1,500 m according to the Ruth Bancroft Garden.

Pollination follows flower colour. A field study of Hesperaloe parviflora found that its red flowers were visited almost exclusively by the black-chinned hummingbird (Archilochus alexandri), with pollen deposition on the stigmas confirmed. Species with pale, night-opening flowers, such as Hesperaloe nocturna, are described as moth-pollinated.

Regulation and conservation

No international protection covers the genus. To our knowledge, no Hesperaloe species is listed in the CITES appendices (absence not checked directly on Species+), and no IUCN Red List assessment could be found. Several species are nevertheless naturally rare, being known from only a few localities (Hesperaloe nocturna, Hesperaloe tenuifolia).

Hesperaloe parviflora is a paradox: it is rare in the wild, yet so widely planted in Texas, even along roadsides, that botanists can no longer trace its original range, according to Turner and Turner (2002). Plants in the trade are nursery-grown. Statuses consulted in September 2026; they may change and should be checked on Species+ and the IUCN Red List.

Growing Hesperaloe

Hesperaloe is easy to grow in the ground wherever winters are not too wet, provided it gets sun and a soil that drains well. Almost all published experience concerns Hesperaloe parviflora; the other species remain collectors' plants with little documented cultivation data.

Outdoor / in-ground cultivation

  • Exposure: full sun; the species tolerates the reflected heat of urban plantings.
  • Soil: sand, loam, caliche or limestone, provided drainage is good; horticultural guides regard this as the essential condition.
  • Watering: very little once established.
  • Flowering: from late spring to midsummer, sometimes into autumn depending on the source; spent stalks are cut off.

Hardiness figures come mainly from nurseries and extension services, not from published measurements. North Carolina State University Extension and other US sources recommend Hesperaloe parviflora for USDA zones 5 to 10, zone 5 corresponding to average annual minimum temperatures of −29 °C to −23 °C. European nurseries are more cautious: Promesse de Fleurs rates it hardy to −15 °C in well-drained soil, more in dry soil, and other horticultural sources cite −18 °C. These values apply to established plants in the ground and say nothing about the duration of the cold or soil moisture; the gap between the American zone rating and the European figures has not been explained by any published trial.

In the Mediterranean basin, Guillot and Van der Meer (2006) recorded several taxa grown as ornamentals in the Iberian Peninsula and the Balearic Islands: Hesperaloe campanulata, Hesperaloe funifera, Hesperaloe parviflora, its variety engelmannii and a yellow-flowered form. No dated cultivation reports were found for other regions of the world (Atlantic Europe, southern Africa, Australasia). In wet-winter climates, drainage remains the point to watch.

Container cultivation

In a pot, use a free-draining mix and let it dry out between waterings. No published data specify the frost tolerance of container-grown plants, whose roots are more exposed to cold than in the ground.

Propagation and pests

Hesperaloe is propagated by dividing clumps or from seed. No pest specific to the genus is reported in the sources consulted. The agave snout weevil (Scyphophorus acupunctatus), established around the Mediterranean and in the south-western United States, attacks mainly large, fleshy, water-filled plants. The site's article on this pest notes that fine-leaved plants are far less attractive to it, and its original French version names Hesperaloe, with its narrow, hard, fibrous leaves, among the plants rarely targeted. No specific observation on Hesperaloe has been published by the Jardin zoologique tropical.

Toxicity and precautions

No reliable data document the toxicity of Hesperaloe to humans or pets, but none demonstrate that it is harmless either. The ASPCA database has no entry for the genus, and one consumer website lists it as toxic without citing any data. Saponins and calcium oxalate crystals, which irritate the skin, are documented in related genera of the same subfamily (agaves), but their presence in Hesperaloe has not been verified. In case of ingestion or skin reaction, contact a poison control centre, a doctor or a veterinarian.

Species of the genus Hesperaloe

The list follows POWO nomenclature and a practical geographical arrangement, from the north-east to the north-west of the range.

Texas and north-eastern Mexico

  • Hesperaloe parviflora (Torr.) J.M.Coult.: type species, the red yucca, with red to pink or yellow flowers; Texas and Coahuila; by far the most widely grown.
  • Hesperaloe engelmannii Krauskopf ex Baker: central and south-western Texas (POWO), under oaks on the Edwards Plateau.
  • Hesperaloe funifera (K.Koch) Trel.: the largest species, with whitish flowers; south-western Texas, Coahuila, northern Nuevo León; studied as a fibre crop.
  • Hesperaloe campanulata G.D.Starr: bell-shaped flowers; central Nuevo León; described in 1997.
  • Hesperaloe chiangii (G.D.Starr) B.L.Turner: San Luis Potosí; formerly a subspecies of Hesperaloe funifera.
  • Hesperaloe malacophylla Hochstätter & Mart.-Aval.: Tamaulipas; the most recently described species, very little known in cultivation.

Sonora

  • Hesperaloe nocturna Gentry: pale, night-opening flowers; described in 1967 from a single locality in Sonora.
  • Hesperaloe tenuifolia G.D.Starr: very fine leaves; southern Sonora, in pine-oak forest; described in 1997.

FAQ about Hesperaloe

Is Hesperaloe parviflora frost-hardy?

Yes, in well-drained soil. US extension services recommend it for USDA zones 5 to 10, while European nurseries quote −15 °C to −18 °C. No published measurement settles the difference; winter wet around the roots remains the main risk.

Does a Hesperaloe die after flowering?

No, not the plant. Unlike most agaves, the clump flowers again every year. Some sources state that an individual rosette flowers only once before its offsets take over, which goes unnoticed in a well-filled clump.

What is the difference between Hesperaloe and Yucca?

Mainly habit and flowers. Hesperaloe forms trunkless clumps of narrow, flexible leaves with no terminal spine, and bears tubular or bell-shaped flowers, often red or pink. Yuccas generally have stiffer, sharp-tipped leaves and white bell-shaped flowers. Both genera belong to the Agavoideae but are distinct.

Is Hesperaloe an aloe?

No. The name means "aloe of the West" because of a resemblance, but aloes belong to another family, the Asphodelaceae, and grow mainly in Africa and the Arabian Peninsula. Hesperaloe is an American member of the Asparagaceae, related to yuccas.

Is red yucca toxic to cats and dogs?

It is not known. No reliable source documents either toxicity or safety for this genus. As a precaution, keep pets from chewing the leaves and consult a veterinarian in case of ingestion.

Was Hesperaloe chiangii originally a Yucca?

No. Greg Starr described it in 1997 as a subspecies of Hesperaloe funifera, and Billie L. Turner raised it to species rank in 2002. The species once described in Yucca are Hesperaloe parviflora (Yucca parviflora) and Hesperaloe funifera (Yucca funifera).

How many Hesperaloe species are there?

Eight, according to the most recent lists consulted. Four were described from 1997 onwards after fieldwork in Mexico; only Hesperaloe parviflora is commonly sold.
