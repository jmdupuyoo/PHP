Macrozamia fraseri is a large cycad endemic to the south-west of Western Australia, where it grows in heath and shrubland on deep sand, from Eneabba in the north to Perth and Mandurah, including the urban bushland of Kings Park and Bold Park. This species of the genus Macrozamia forms a crown of 30 to 100 grey-green, keeled leaves on a trunk that may stay underground or rise to 3 m. Described in 1842 by the Dutch botanist Miquel, it was the first Macrozamia of Western Australia to be named; Miquel later moved it to Encephalartos as Encephalartos fraseri, and for several decades it was included in Macrozamia riedlei. Known as the sandplain zamia, it provided the Noongar people with seeds whose flesh was eaten after long preparation.

Key facts about Macrozamia fraseri

  • Accepted name: Macrozamia fraseri Miq. (1842), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15), the Australian Plant Census and Florabase
  • Former names and synonyms: Encephalartos fraseri (Miq.) Miq. (1863); included in Macrozamia riedlei by Gardner (1956) and Johnson (1959); Species+ adds names that The World List of Cycads places under Macrozamia riedlei (see Taxonomy)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: sandplain zamia (Florabase), zamia (Flora of Australia); djiridji or jeeriji, a Noongar name
  • Natural range: south-west Western Australia: from Eneabba and Jurien Bay to Perth and Mandurah (Swan Coastal Plain, Geraldton Sandplains, edge of the Jarrah Forest)
  • IUCN status: Least Concern (LC), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated August 2020 and published in 2022; “not threatened” in Western Australia (Florabase)
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II, like the whole genus; Annex B in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: no dated cultivation report; “quite cold tolerant” according to the PACSOA, without a figure (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, heath and open shrubland on deep sand; in cultivation, full sun and sandy, well-drained soil according to the PACSOA
  • Habit and adult size: trunk 0 to 3 m tall and 40 to 70 cm across; 30 to 100 leaves 1.4 to 2.7 m long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Kings Park and the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney among four Australian gardens; the Huntington and San Diego Zoo declare it to BGCI; in Britain, only the Millennium Seed Bank
  • Toxicity: seeds toxic to cattle according to an official analysis of 1938 (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Macrozamia fraseri

It is recognised by its robust habit, often with a visible trunk, its long keeled leaves with many leaflets, its very woolly crown and its large cones. These are the features that, according to the Flora of Australia (Hill, 1998), set it apart from the other two species of Western Australia. The Flora's measurements are as follows:

  • Trunk: underground or above ground, 0 to 3 m tall and 40 to 70 cm across;
  • Leaves: 30 to 100 in the crown, 137 to 270 cm long, moderately keeled, grey-green, dull to semi-glossy; petiole 18 to 55 cm;
  • Leaflets: 112 to 174 per leaf, undivided, 18.5 to 34 cm long and 7.5 to 14 mm wide, the same colour on both faces or slightly paler beneath, with flat margins and a spiny tip; the lowest leaflets are reduced to spines;
  • Male cones: spindle-shaped, 30 to 48 cm long and 10 to 14 cm wide, with terminal spines 15 to 90 mm long;
  • Female cones: ovoid, 35 to 45 cm long and 15 to 17 cm wide;
  • Seeds: ovoid, 40 to 50 mm long and 25 to 30 mm wide, with red flesh.

Florabase, the flora of the Western Australian Herbarium, describes it as a tree cycad with a variable trunk, dull, strongly keeled leaves, narrow to medium leaflets and large, broad cones. The account published by the Palm and Cycad Societies of Australia (PACSOA) explains the keel: opposite leaflets are inserted on the axis at an angle of 90 to 150°, which gives the leaf its V profile. According to Macintyre and Dobson, citing Barrett and Tay (2005), a female cone may hold up to 160 seeds.

Hybrids

No natural or garden hybrid has been reported.

Possible confusion

The main confusion is with Macrozamia riedlei, the other Macrozamia of the Perth region. According to the Flora of Australia, the three Western Australian species form a group of close relatives, with almost cylindrical petioles bearing a few leaflets reduced to spines; Macrozamia fraseri stands out by its robust, often tree-like habit, keeled leaves, densely woolly crown and large cones. Florabase adds the habitat: Macrozamia fraseri grows in low heath without jarrah (Eucalyptus marginata). In the field, the easiest character to see is therefore the shape of the leaf, V-shaped in Macrozamia fraseri.

Confusion with Macrozamia dyeri, from the south coast around Esperance, is rare, since the ranges are far apart. The Flora of Australia gives Macrozamia dyeri broader (12.5 to 17 mm) and fewer leaflets (70 to 100), and Florabase “long and narrow” cones, against “large, broad” cones in Macrozamia fraseri.

Taxonomy

Macrozamia fraseri Miq. was published in 1842 by Friedrich Anton Wilhelm Miquel in his Monographia Cycadearum (page 37), the work in which he created the genus Macrozamia. The description is very short: a trunk said to reach 30 feet, covered in scales, and cones with spirally arranged scales. Miquel moved it in 1863 to the genus Encephalartos, as Encephalartos fraseri; that name is now a synonym. The World List of Cycads accepts the species and indicates a probable holotype in Melbourne (MEL). It belongs to the family Zamiaceae.

For several decades the species was not recognised. Charles Gardner, in 1956, used the name Macrozamia riedlei in a broad sense, and L.A.S. Johnson, in his 1959 revision of the Australian Zamiaceae, listed Macrozamia fraseri among its synonyms, while noting that Miquel's description, brief as it is, was enough to make the name valid. It is treated as a distinct species in Ken Hill's account for the Flora of Australia (1998), followed by Florabase, the Australian Plant Census and The World List of Cycads.

The databases do not agree on its synonyms. Species+ attaches to it Macrozamia preissii, Encephalartos preissii, Macrozamia oldfieldii, Encephalartos oldfieldii, Encephalartos douglasii and Zamia cycadifolia, whereas The World List of Cycads places Macrozamia preissii, Encephalartos preissii and Macrozamia oldfieldii in the synonymy of Macrozamia riedlei. This page follows The World List of Cycads, the reference list of cycad specialists. For the reader, this means that an old plant or seed labelled “Macrozamia preissii”, or “Macrozamia riedlei” before 1998, may belong to either species.

According to Macintyre and Dobson, Barrett and Tay (2005, 2016) confirmed that the cycad of Kings Park and Bold Park is Macrozamia fraseri.

Etymology and history

The epithet honours a man named Fraser, whose identity has been confused. The World List of Cycads, following Haynes (2022), the online Flora of Australia and a dictionary of Western Australian plant names (Sharr, 1996, as quoted by the Friends of Queens Park Bushland) give Charles Fitzgerald Fraser (1883–1951), a Western Australian surveyor and pastoralist, as the collector of the type. That is impossible: the name dates from 1842, forty years before his birth. The World List of Cycads also calls him “first superintendent of the Sydney Botanical Gardens”, a post held by a different Charles Fraser.

Miquel's own text settles the question. He credits the first knowledge of the species to Fraser and quotes his account of the Swan River: on an island near the river mouth, Fraser “caught sight of a plant with an arborescent habit, which, on examination, proved to be a species of Zamia, with spiral fruit, differing only from Zamia spiralis in habit”, and further upstream this Zamia “was here observed to attain the height of thirty feet”. These lines belong to the journal of Charles Fraser (1788–1831), colonial botanist of New South Wales, who accompanied Captain James Stirling on his 1827 expedition to assess the Swan River for a settlement, as Macintyre and Dobson had already argued. Miquel also mentions a piece of leaf received from the Paris museum, gathered by French travellers at Geographe Bay, which in his view belonged either to Macrozamia spiralis or, more probably, to his new species.

The genus name joins the Greek makros, “large”, to the genus name Zamia. Among the reference specimens cited by the Flora of Australia are collections by Ken Hill at Melaleuca Park, north of Gingin, at the Hill River crossing and at Coomalloo Creek.

Common names

In English, Florabase and the Australian Plant Census give “sandplain zamia”, and the Flora of Australia “zamia”, the general name for Macrozamia in Western Australia. The Noongar name is written “djiridji” by the Flora of Australia and “jeeriji” by the Friends of Queens Park Bushland, in Perth. Early settlers also recorded the Noongar name of the fruit, that is the seed with its flesh: “by-yu” in Grey (1840), “baio” in Moore (1835) and “bayoo” in Bunbury (1836), according to Macintyre and Dobson; these names apply to the fruit of the Macrozamia of the region in general.

In the wild

Macrozamia fraseri is locally abundant but patchily distributed. According to the Flora of Australia, it ranges from Eneabba, in the north, to Perth, in shrubland and heath, usually on deep sand; Barrett and Tay, quoted by Macintyre and Dobson, give Jurien Bay to Mandurah. Florabase places it in three biogeographic regions (Geraldton Sandplains, Swan Coastal Plain and Jarrah Forest) and in nineteen local government areas, from Irwin in the north to Harvey in the south and inland to York and Brookton.

The PACSOA describes a strictly Mediterranean climate, with 650 to 750 mm of rain falling in the winter months. The plant is common in the bushland of Kings Park and Bold Park, in Perth. A member of the French Fous de palmiers forum also posted photographs in March 2016 of many plants in a regional park in south-western Australia, among Xanthorrhoea.

The species withstands fire: Macintyre and Dobson show plants and male cones after a summer fire at Toodyay in 2010. According to the Friends of Queens Park Bushland, emus eat the seeds and disperse them. Macintyre and Dobson noted in 2009 that female cones were far fewer at Bold Park than the year before, without knowing why.

Conservation status and regulation

Macrozamia fraseri is not threatened: the IUCN Red List classes it as Least Concern (LC), in the assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated 14 August 2020 and published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, GBIF and Crossref, checked on 26 October 2026), and Florabase gives it as “not threatened” in Western Australia. The Atlas of Living Australia shows no threatened-species listing (checked on 26 October 2026).

Like all species of the genus, it is listed in CITES Appendix II and, in the European Union, in Annex B of the wildlife trade regulations (Species+, checked on 26 October 2026). International trade in plants and seeds requires permits. The species does turn up in Europe: in 2014, a Belgian seller offered young plants with 50 cm leaves on the Fous de palmiers forum. Buy only plants raised in cultivation, with their documents, and check the regulations in force before any purchase or import.

Growing Macrozamia fraseri

Macrozamia fraseri is grown in full sun, in sandy, well-drained soil, with water given mainly in the cool season, as in its home climate. The PACSOA, which calls it very uncommon in cultivation, gives the only published advice.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Light: full sun preferred (PACSOA).
  • Soil: sandy, moist but well drained (PACSOA).
  • Water: the plant comes from a Mediterranean climate with winter rain (650 to 750 mm); in cultivation, this suggests watering mainly in the cool season and less in summer, without letting the root ball dry out completely. This regime is a deduction from the home climate, not a published cultivation result.
  • Cold: “quite cold tolerant” according to the PACSOA, without a figure.
  • Space: with leaves 1.4 to 2.7 m long and a trunk that may exceed 50 cm across, it is a plant for a large garden.
  • Growth: no growth data has been published.

No observation of the Jardin zoologique tropical in southern France has been published for this species. Its home climate, with dry summers and winter rain, is nevertheless close to that of Mediterranean gardens, coastal California or southern Australia.

Container cultivation

No container-growing report has been published. A young plant can be kept in a deep pot of sandy, very free-draining mix; the size of an adult calls for a very large tub.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

  • Australia: Kings Park and Botanic Garden, in Perth, within the natural range of the species, declares it to BGCI, as do Mount Annan and the Australian National Botanic Gardens in Canberra. The Royal Botanic Garden Sydney lists in its Garden Explorer (updated on 1 October 2026) a wild-collected plant accessioned in 1993 (S1993-2519), in bed 102i.
  • United States: the Huntington (San Marino, California) and San Diego Zoo Botanical Gardens declare it to BGCI. The Montgomery Botanical Center does not list it in its online catalogue.
  • United Kingdom: the only British institution that declares it to BGCI is the Millennium Seed Bank at Wakehurst (Sussex), a seed bank; no garden is known to grow it.

Twelve institutions in all declare it to BGCI, the others in China, the Czech Republic, France, Germany and the Netherlands. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not necessarily a living plant on display today. The species does not appear in the census of Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria. No English-language grower's report has been found.

Propagation

Propagation is by seed. The plants being male or female, both sexes and, in cultivation, hand pollination are needed to obtain fertile seed. The seeds ripen around March, in the southern autumn: that is when Noongar women gathered them, according to Grey (1841). No germination data specific to the species has been published, and no source mentions offsets.

Pests and diseases

No problem specific to the species is documented in reliable sources. In Macrozamia grown in Europe, growers' forums mainly mention scale insects and rot in cold, wet soil (see the genus page).

Cold hardiness

No dated cultivation report exists for Macrozamia fraseri, and no source gives a USDA zone for it.

Mediterranean and European experience

No planting in the ground is documented in Europe, and no recorded cold spell concerns the species. The posts that mention it on the Fous de palmiers forum are photographs taken in Australia, an offer for sale and unanswered questions.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

The PACSOA describes it as “quite cold tolerant”, without a figure or a dated observation. No other cultivation result has been published from Britain, North America or Australia. Any hardiness figure would be a guess, and this page gives none.

Toxicity and precautions

Yes, the seeds of Macrozamia fraseri are toxic. According to Macintyre and Dobson, the chemical laboratory of the Western Australian Mines Department analysed in 1938 the seeds and flesh of the local “zamia”, Macrozamia fraseri: it concluded that the seeds cause gastro-intestinal irritation in cattle, that only the seeds (the kernel) were toxic, and that the flesh contained 14% of an orange oil.

The question of the flesh remains open: according to Ladd et al. (1993), cited by Asmussen (2011), the flesh of the closely related Macrozamia riedlei contains a great deal of macrozamin. Macintyre and Dobson report that rats fed raw flesh of Macrozamia fraseri showed no ill effects, but this was an amateur trial, not published in a scientific journal.

Macrozamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a toxin of the liver; the young leaves cause irreversible paralysis of the hindquarters in cattle (McKenzie, 2006; see the genus page). Never eat cycad seeds, wear gloves to handle them, keep them out of reach of children, dogs and livestock, and contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay in case of ingestion.

Traditional uses

The Noongar of the Perth region ate the flesh of Macrozamia seeds after a treatment described by settlers from the 1830s. George Grey (1841), quoted by Macintyre and Dobson, relates that the women gathered the seeds in March, left them to soak for several days in a shallow pool, then buried them in holes dug in sandy ground, called “mor-dak”, an arm's length deep and lined with rushes. Other witnesses, such as Bunbury (1836) and Drummond, mention burial for about one moon. According to Drummond (1842), the kernel, freed of its red flesh, was not eaten.

From 2008 to 2010, Ken Macintyre and Barb Dobson repeated these methods with seeds of Macrozamia fraseri gathered at Bold Park, with the permission of the parks authority, and at Toodyay. After soaking or burial, the flesh became soft, oily and easy to remove. They conclude that the treatment mainly served to improve the taste, digestibility and keeping quality of the flesh rather than to detoxify it, which runs against Grey's interpretation and against the data of Ladd et al. (1993) on Macrozamia riedlei. This conclusion has not been published in a peer-reviewed journal. The season of consumption, April and May, matched the Noongar season that Moore called “geran”.

This knowledge is not a recipe: incomplete preparation can cause serious poisoning.

Frequently asked questions

How can it be told from Macrozamia riedlei?

By the leaf and the habit. Macrozamia fraseri has keeled, V-shaped leaves, a very woolly crown, large cones and often a trunk above ground; it grows in heath on sand, without jarrah.

Is it the cycad of Kings Park, in Perth?

Yes. According to Barrett and Tay, quoted by Macintyre and Dobson, the cycad common in the bushland of Kings Park and Bold Park is Macrozamia fraseri.

Who was Fraser?

Charles Fraser (1788–1831), colonial botanist of New South Wales, who visited the Swan River in 1827: Miquel quotes his account of the plant. Several databases name another Fraser, born in 1883, which is impossible for a name published in 1842.

Are its seeds edible?

No, never eat them. The Noongar ate the flesh of the seeds after weeks of soaking and burial, but the seeds are toxic and incomplete preparation is dangerous.

Is it cold-hardy?

Not known precisely. The PACSOA calls it quite cold tolerant, but no grower has published a measured result.

Should it be watered in summer?

Sparingly. It comes from a climate with dry summers and winter rain; no cultivation result sets a watering rhythm for this species.

Is it endangered?

No. The IUCN classes it as Least Concern (2022) and Western Australia considers it not threatened. It remains covered by CITES (Appendix II).
