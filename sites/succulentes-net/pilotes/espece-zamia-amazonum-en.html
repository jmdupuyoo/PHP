Zamia amazonum is a cycad of the lowland rainforest understorey of the upper Amazon basin, found from southern Venezuela and Brazilian Amazonia to Colombia, Ecuador and Peru. This species of the genus Zamia has no visible trunk: its stem stays underground, and only two to six leaves, sometimes more than two metres long, rise above the leaf litter. Described in 2001 by the American botanist Dennis Stevenson, it is almost unknown in cultivation. The IUCN lists it as Least Concern.

How to recognise Zamia amazonum

By its lack of an above-ground trunk, its few but long leaves, its lance-shaped leaflets 2 to 4 cm wide and finely toothed in their upper half, and the small, often branched prickles on its petiole. The measurements below come from the original description, published by Dennis Stevenson in the Flora de Colombia (2001):

  • Stem: underground, 3 to 8 cm in diameter; cataphylls 3 to 8 cm long, with a triangular base and a linear-lanceolate tip.
  • Leaves: 2 to 6, ovate to elliptic in outline, 0.5 to 2.5 m long. The petiole, 0.5 to 1 m long, bears small, rigid, branched prickles and usually a rust-coloured down at its base; the rachis, also 0.5 to 1 m long, is prickly in its lower third.
  • Leaflets: 10 to 30 pairs, almost opposite, not overlapping, papery in texture, lanceolate, 15 to 20 cm long and 2 to 4 cm wide, stalkless, without grooves, with a drawn-out (acuminate) tip and a finely toothed margin in the upper half.
  • Male cones: 2 to 6, cylindrical, brown, 6 to 10 cm long and 1 to 2 cm wide, on a stalk (peduncle) 8 to 15 cm long.
  • Female cone: usually single, reddish-brown, 10 to 15 cm long and 3 to 5 cm wide, on a peduncle 5 to 8 cm long.
  • Seeds: ovoid, about 1 cm long and 0.5 cm wide, with a red fleshy coat (sarcotesta).

Stevenson adds that the species is fairly variable.

Hybrids

No hybrid, natural or horticultural, has been documented.

Possible confusion

The Amazonian Zamia are a difficult group of understorey plants with underground or short stems and thin leaflets. In the original description, Stevenson places Zamia amazonum close to Zamia ulei, from which he separates it by its longer leaves (up to 2.5 m), its lanceolate leaflets and its small female cones. He also compares it with Zamia lecointei and Zamia muricata: Zamia amazonum differs from them in its rachis, densely hairy when young and armed with often branched prickles, and in its female cones, which do not exceed 15 cm including the peduncle.

Zamia hymenophyllidia and Zamia macrochiera also grow in this part of Amazonia. In the field, a reliable identification usually requires cones.

Taxonomy

Zamia amazonum is accepted by The World List of Cycads and by GBIF; POWO could not be consulted directly. It was described by Dennis William Stevenson in volume 21 of the Flora de Colombia, published in 2001 (page 33, figure 3). The type is the collection D.W. Stevenson 886, made in Brazil, in the state of Amazonas, at the confluence of the rio Tauri and the rio Içana; the holotype is kept at INPA in Manaus, with isotypes at COL, MO, NY and U. The name has never changed and has no synonyms.

Stevenson also mentioned three collections from Chocó, on the Pacific coast of Colombia, which might belong to the species while departing somewhat from typical material; whether they were the same species remained to be established. The World List of Cycads does not include Chocó in the range of Zamia amazonum.

The species belongs to a group that specialists consider poorly understood. In 2023, Segalla, Pimenta and Calonje wrote that the Zamia of the Amazon basin are currently “the least understood assemblage of species” in the genus, and listed ten accepted species there, Zamia amazonum among them, alongside Zamia boliviana and Zamia brasiliensis.

Etymology and history

The epithet amazonum refers to the species’ distribution in the upper Amazon basin, according to Haynes’s etymological compendium (2022) as quoted by The World List of Cycads; the original description links it more precisely to the Colombian department of Amazonas.

Before it was described, the plant had already been collected many times in Brazil (rio Negro, rio Vaupés, rio Juruá-Mirim, the Manaus to Caracaraí road), in Colombia, in Peru (Iquitos area) and in Venezuela (Maroa), as the specimens cited by Stevenson show. No history of introduction into cultivation is documented.

Common names

There is no English name. No traditional name has been confirmed in the botanical literature consulted, but Flora e Funga do Brasil, the official checklist of the Brazilian flora, records two Portuguese names for the species in the state of Acre, “batata-de-paca” and “palmeirinha-espinhosa” (consulted through GBIF; the checklist’s own page could not be opened). Their application is uncertain: The World List of Cycads does not list Zamia amazonum in Acre, and a Brazilian herbarium label gives “batata de paca” for Zamia ulei. These names should therefore be used with caution.

In the wild

Zamia amazonum is widespread in the Amazonian parts of Colombia, Venezuela, Brazil and Peru, and The World List of Cycads also records it in Ecuador. According to Stevenson, it is relatively common where it occurs. The Catalogue of the Plants and Lichens of Colombia gives an altitude range of 100 to 500 m. It is a plant of the understorey of lowland rainforest.

Like all cycads, it forms coralloid roots that host nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria. Its pollinator is not known, and no climatic data specific to the species have been published.

Conservation status and regulation

Zamia amazonum is listed as Least Concern (LC) on the IUCN Red List because of its wide range. The assessment is by Cristina López-Gallego and Michael Calonje: according to Crossref it is dated 24 June 2020, was published in 2022 and was reissued with corrections, and GBIF relays the category; the status is taken from The World List of Cycads, as the Red List itself could not be consulted. An earlier assessment by D.W. Stevenson appears in the 2010 Red List. No verified source gives the species a status on a national red list.

Like every Zamia except Zamia restrepoi, Zamia amazonum is listed in CITES Appendix II, where the genus has appeared since 4 February 1977. The listing carries annotation #4 (the version adopted at CoP20, in force since 5 March 2026): seeds, pollen, seedlings and tissue cultures obtained in vitro and transported in sterile containers, as well as cut flowers of artificially propagated plants, are not covered by CITES; international trade in the plants themselves requires an export permit. In the European Union the species falls under Annex B of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, whose current annexes are set by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026.

This summary does not replace the official texts: check the regulations in force before any purchase or exchange, ask the seller for proof of origin and choose nursery-raised plants grown from seed.

Growing Zamia amazonum

No cultivation experience is documented in the sources consulted, and no growing guidance specific to the species has been published. The only guide is its habitat: the understorey of the Amazonian rainforest, warm and humid all year round, in the shade of trees. Everything below is inferred from that habitat and has not been confirmed by published trials.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

Planting out can only be considered in a frost-free, humid tropical climate, in shade. No planted specimen outside its range has been documented.

Container cultivation

Elsewhere, it is a plant for a pot in a heated glasshouse or indoors, in filtered light, with steady warmth and humidity. No compost or watering regime has been tested for this species; the general cultivation of forest Zamia is described on the genus page.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In short: no record.

  • United States: Montgomery Botanical Center’s online catalogue returns no accession, and no American garden declares the species to BGCI (both consulted on 24 October 2026).
  • Australia and United Kingdom: no garden declares the species to BGCI, and it is absent from the British and Australian catalogues checked in October 2026. The British nursery Trebrown has a species page with a USDA zone 10b rating, its own commercial classification; the page gives no information on the plant itself.

No report from a private grower was found.

Propagation

No data specific to the species. Like all Zamia, it has separate male and female plants: both are needed to obtain seed, and in cultivation pollination by hand. Seeds are exempt from CITES under annotation #4.

Pests and diseases

No pest or disease specific to the species has been documented.

Cold hardiness

There is no data on the tolerance of Zamia amazonum to cold, heat or drought. It lives in the frost-free lowland rainforest of the upper Amazon basin, and nothing supports the idea that it tolerates frost.

Mediterranean and European experience

No cultivation result has been published from southern France or Italy, outdoors or in a pot.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No cold value and no dated cultivation report was found, from forums or from botanic gardens. Trebrown’s zone 10b rating is a commercial classification, not a measured result.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity study specific to Zamia amazonum has been found, but the risk is well documented in cycads: Zamia, like other cycads, contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a substance toxic to the liver and nervous system; the seeds are the most dangerous part. In the genus, the ASPCA lists Zamia furfuracea as poisonous to dogs, cats and horses (vomiting, bloody diarrhoea, liver damage). In the absence of evidence to the contrary, treat the plant as poisonous: keep seeds and leaves out of reach of children and animals, and wear gloves to clean seeds. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet straight away.

Traditional uses

Two uses are reported. According to a 2023 review of Zamia by El-Seadaway and colleagues, the Chayahuita people of north-eastern Peru use the crushed root, soaked overnight in water, to treat leishmaniasis; this review is known only from a summary and was not read directly. The Catalogue of the Plants and Lichens of Colombia, through GBIF, also includes the species in a list of unconventional food plants of Colombian Amazonia, for its root.

None of these sources describes a preparation: the plant remains poisonous and must not be eaten.

Frequently asked questions

Is it a palm?

No. Despite its appearance, Zamia amazonum is a cycad (Zamiaceae), a gymnosperm that reproduces by cones and is not closely related to palms.

Where does it grow?

In the understorey of lowland rainforest in the upper Amazon basin, in Brazil, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru and southern Venezuela, at roughly 100 to 500 m.

How can I tell it from Zamia ulei?

According to the original description, by its longer leaves (up to 2.5 m), its lanceolate leaflets and its smaller female cones, no more than 15 cm long including the peduncle. Without cones, the distinction remains difficult.

Is it hardy in the UK?

No data exist, but it comes from frost-free tropical forest: in Britain it is a plant for a heated glasshouse or indoors.

Is it endangered?

No, according to the IUCN, which lists it as Least Concern because of its wide range. International trade in the plants is still regulated by CITES.

Is it poisonous?

It should be assumed so. No analysis exists for this species, but the cycads studied contain serious toxins, especially in their seeds.
