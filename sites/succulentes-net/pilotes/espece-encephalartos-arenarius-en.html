Encephalartos arenarius is a South African cycad confined to a small area of the Eastern Cape around Alexandria, where it grows on old, stabilised sand dunes covered in coastal thicket and dune forest. This species of the genus Encephalartos forms clumps of short stems, often lying on the ground and half buried in sand, with leaves 0.9 to 1.25 m long and broad, lobed leaflets that may be green or bluish. Its English names are Alexandria cycad and dune cycad, and its Latin name means “of the sands”. Described in 1955 by Robert Allen Dyer, it is listed as Endangered on the IUCN Red List: its populations have more than halved in sixty years, mainly through collecting, which the loose sand makes easy.

Key facts about Encephalartos arenarius

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos arenarius R.A.Dyer (1955 according to IPNI, 1956 according to The World List of Cycads), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15)
  • Former names and synonyms: no synonym; plants formerly identified as Encephalartos latifrons
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: Alexandria cycad, dune cycad (Species+, SANBI) and Kwa Qaba cycad (PlantZAfrica); uMphanga wakwa Qaba in a Nguni language (PlantZAfrica)
  • Natural range: South Africa, Eastern Cape, Alexandria district; extent of occurrence about 258 km², area of occupancy about 20 km² (SANBI)
  • IUCN status: Endangered (EN), criteria A2acd; B1ab(i,ii,iii,iv,v)+2ab(i,ii,iii,iv,v); C1, assessment dated June 2020 and published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I (whole genus); Annex A in the European Union; collecting from the wild prohibited in South Africa
  • Documented hardiness: no published frost data for its coastal habitat; no quantified cold report from cultivation (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, the shade of thicket on deep sand of stabilised dunes, sometimes full sun where the thicket has been cleared; very free-draining sandy soil in cultivation
  • Habit and adult size: stems single or clumping, reclining or erect, up to 1 m above ground (1 to 2 m long in all according to SANBI) and 20 to 30 cm thick; leaves 0.9 to 1.25 m
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Kew and Glasgow (BGCI); Montgomery Botanical Center, Fairchild, the Huntington, Lotusland, San Diego Botanic Garden, San Diego Zoo, UC Berkeley and others; Royal Botanic Garden Sydney and an outdoor bed at the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria, Melbourne
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; treat it as poisonous like all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos arenarius

Look for fairly short leaves curving back at the tip and broad, hairless leaflets whose lower margin usually carries three spiny lobes while the upper margin is almost always entire. Dyer's original description (1955) gives these characters:

  • Stem: single or, more often, branched at the base into clumps; up to 1 m long and then usually reclining; in the field the lower part is often covered with sand and dead leaves
  • Leaves: 90 to 125 cm long, somewhat glaucous when young, usually recurved at the tip; petiole 15 to 20 cm, with a swollen base covered in brown wool
  • Leaflets: about thirty pairs, flat or slightly twisted, pungent, sometimes remaining slightly glaucous; middle leaflets 12 to 16 cm long and 2.5 to 4 cm wide; upper margin entire, very rarely with one tooth; lower margin with three triangular, spiny lobes, rarely four; seedling leaflets deeply three-lobed at the tip
  • Female cone: solitary, on a thick stalk 4 to 8 cm long, 35 to 45 cm long and about 20 cm wide, more or less glaucous; scales smooth, hairless, barely wrinkled, with an exposed face about 5 by 4 cm
  • Male cone: not seen whole by Dyer, apparently smaller; scales 3 to 3.5 cm by 2.25 to 2.5 cm
  • Seeds: coral red, up to about 5 cm long and 2 to 2.5 cm wide

Cone sizes vary between sources: the PlantZAfrica account of SANBI (2008) gives solitary, barrel-shaped female cones 50 to 60 cm long and 25 to 30 cm wide, light green when mature, and one to three male cones 30 to 50 cm long and 8 to 15 cm wide, also light green; it adds that the female cone scales open for five to ten days in April and May. Dyer's description, based on the population of the Kaba valley, remains the reference here. PlantZAfrica also distinguishes a green form and a rarer blue form found in certain localities; neither is recognised as a separate taxon.

Hybrids

Dyer considered the Kaba valley population uniform: among about a hundred plants examined, he saw no sign of hybridisation. On the other hand, on the right bank of the Bushmans River he described a very variable population with resemblances to Encephalartos altensteinii, Encephalartos lehmannii, Encephalartos horridus and Encephalartos arenarius, which he interpreted at least partly as the result of hybridisation. In his 1965 account of Encephalartos trispinosus, Dyer likewise described plants on the Bushmans and lower Great Fish rivers that seemed to combine Encephalartos trispinosus and Encephalartos altensteinii, and did not rule out a contribution from Encephalartos arenarius and Encephalartos latifrons, which grow a few miles away.

Hybrids are common in cultivation. On the French forum Fous de palmiers a collector warned in 2013 that some “blue arenarius” on sale are hybrids with Encephalartos horridus, and on the Italian forum Tropicamente a grower in Ravenna germinated in 2013 a seed sold as the hybrid Encephalartos arenarius × Encephalartos latifrons. The origin of such plants cannot be checked: buy a plant with documented provenance if you want the pure species.

Possible confusion

The classic confusion is with Encephalartos latifrons, its closest relative according to Dyer, to the point that a single leaf or leaflet cannot always separate them. With the whole plant the difference is clear: Encephalartos latifrons forms stout, erect trunks of two metres or more, with stiff, arching leaves, leaflets that are hairy when young, closely set and strongly veined, and large female cones with coarsely papillose scales. Encephalartos arenarius is less robust, its reclining stems rarely rise more than a metre above the ground, its leaflets are hairless and less crowded, and its smaller female cones have smooth, glaucous scales. PlantZAfrica also mentions confusion with the taller, glossy-leaved Encephalartos latifrons and, sometimes, with the broad-leaved Encephalartos ferox.

Blue-leaved plants are also confused with Encephalartos horridus and Encephalartos lehmannii. In his account of Encephalartos horridus, Dyer (1965) notes that Encephalartos arenarius has light to mid-green leaves, barely glaucous, and leaflets more than 2.5 cm wide with three or four lobes, whereas Encephalartos horridus has very glaucous foliage; Encephalartos lehmannii shares the blue foliage but has leaflets that are almost always entire. On Tropicamente, a grower in Ravenna concluded in 2012 that a plant bought in 2009 as Encephalartos horridus was a blue form of Encephalartos arenarius, an identification disputed by another member. Young plants of these species are hard to tell apart.

Taxonomy

Encephalartos arenarius is an accepted name with no synonyms. Robert Allen Dyer, of the National Herbarium in Pretoria, described it in the Journal of South African Botany (22(1): 1–4), after a paper read in July 1955 in Grahamstown. The issue bears the publication date of 31 December 1955, used by IPNI; The World List of Cycads uses 1956, the year printed on the cover and quoted by Dyer himself in later work.

The type, Dyer 5441, was collected in the Kaba valley, Alexandria division, about 4 miles (6.5 km) from Woody Cape, at about 500 ft (150 m); the holotype is in Pretoria (PRE), with isotypes in Grahamstown (GRA), at Kew (K) and at Kirstenbosch (NBG). In his 1965 study of the cycads of southern Africa, Dyer lists the species among those newly separated since 1945, noting that it was distinguished from Encephalartos latifrons.

Etymology and history

The epithet is the Latin arenarius, “of the sands”, a reference to its habitat on old dune deposits (Haynes, 2022, cited by The World List of Cycads; PlantZAfrica).

Its history is that of a plant long grown without a name. In 1852 the botanist Karl Zeyher recorded an “Encephalartos tridentatus” near the Bushmans River, and Dyer suspected he meant a related or identical plant. The account in Flora Capensis (1933) did not recognise it and identified a specimen as Encephalartos latifrons. In 1945 M. R. Henderson drew attention to a plant grown at Kirstenbosch. In 1953 E. E. A. Gledhill reported a colony of unidentified Encephalartos on the inland dunes of the Kaba valley; Dyer inspected it with her in 1954, which led to the description. The farm owners told him that the species was fairly widespread in the thicket, but that a large number of plants had recently been taken for cultivation.

Common names

Species+ gives the English names Alexandria cycad and dune cycad; dune cycad is also used by SANBI in its 2023 CITES assessment. The PlantZAfrica account of SANBI (2008) gives Alexandria cycad and Kwa Qaba cycad, and the Nguni name uMphanga wakwa Qaba.

In the wild

Encephalartos arenarius is endemic to the Albany thicket biome of the Eastern Cape and occupies only a small area around Alexandria: an extent of occurrence of about 258 km² and an area of occupancy of about 20 km², according to SANBI. It grows in dense coastal forest and thicket on moderate to steep slopes of stabilised dunes, in deep sand. Plants usually live in shade; where the thicket has been cleared for grazing, isolated individuals survive in full sun, even on farmland. Dyer found that this habitat recalled that of Encephalartos ferox on the coastal dunes of Mozambique and Zululand.

PlantZAfrica lists some of its companions in the coastal dune forest, among them Apodytes dimidiata, Carissa bispinosa, Euclea natalensis, Gasteria acinacifolia, Mimusops caffra, Olea europaea subsp. africana, Schotia afra, Sterculia alexandri and Zanthoxylum capense.

Growth is slow: SANBI estimates the age at first reproduction at 30 to 40 years in the wild and a generation time of about 70 years, and a plant may live for more than a century. Each stem is said to produce a cone on average every three or four years. The seeds are poorly dispersed (usually less than 20 m) and are thought to be carried mainly by baboons, since hornbills do not enter the dense thicket. The coralloid roots of the species host cyanobacteria, studied in 1989 by Joubert and colleagues together with those of Encephalartos transvenosus and Encephalartos woodii.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos arenarius is listed as Endangered (EN) on the IUCN Red List under criteria A2acd; B1ab(i,ii,iii,iv,v)+2ab(i,ii,iii,iv,v); C1. The assessment, by J. D. Bösenberg, is dated June 2020 and was published in 2022, according to The World List of Cycads. Some websites still call it Vulnerable: that is out of date.

According to SANBI, the population numbers 850 to 1,500 mature individuals, and repeat photography shows a decline of more than 50% in sixty years. Collecting dates back to the early 1950s, and in the past whole lorry-loads of plants were taken away. In 2009 a single plant remained at a site that once held 10 to 15 stems; several colonies have fewer than 50 individuals. The loose sand makes plants easy to dig out. Other threats are clearing for grazing, urban development, the building of the N2 road and, an emerging threat, sand mining.

All subpopulations were microchipped in 1996; only one lies in a state protected area, the others on private land. The Eastern Cape banned the collecting of wild plants in 2003, and the species was excluded from the 2004 amnesty for people holding cycads without permits. It is covered by the national Biodiversity Management Plan for threatened cycads, in force since 2017. Stolen plants were still being seized at roadblocks in 2022.

Like the whole genus, Encephalartos arenarius has been listed in CITES Appendix I since 4 February 1977, without annotation, so plants, seeds and pollen are all covered. Commercial international trade in wild-collected plants is prohibited; only artificially propagated plants can be exported, from registered nurseries and with the required permits. In the European Union the species is in Annex A of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383. Since 7 February 2013 the EU has held a negative opinion on imports of artificially propagated plants of any size from South Africa. These details come from Species+, the official CITES and EU database, consulted in October 2026; check the current rules before any purchase or import, and ask for proof that the plant was propagated in cultivation.

Growing Encephalartos arenarius

Encephalartos arenarius is one of the easier and faster South African Encephalartos in cultivation, provided it has very free-draining sandy soil and a climate with little or no frost. That at least is what nurseries and growers report, without any published study. PlantZAfrica describes it as growing well in semi-shade but tolerating full sun, easily grown from seed and easily transplanted.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Soil: sandy and very free-draining. A collector near Agen, in south-western France, adds 50% sand to a cactus compost, “especially for arenarius, which lives on dunes” (Fous de palmiers forum, 2009)
  • Exposure: the San Diego nursery Jungle Music recommends full sun on the coast, and even inland for the blue form; in the wild, however, the plant usually lives in the shade of thicket, which leaves room for part shade
  • Growth: Jungle Music describes it as a fairly good grower forming large, spreading crowns; on Tropicamente, a grower in the Canary Islands wrote in 2008 that Encephalartos arenarius and Encephalartos laurentianus “grow like grass” there, and other members find it one of the most regular species, without skipped years
  • Feeding: at Kirstenbosch, PlantZAfrica reports a spring feed with bone meal and a generous layer of compost during the year
  • Tropical heat: at Nong Nooch Tropical Botanical Garden, in Thailand, the species grows but rarely cones, like Encephalartos trispinosus and Encephalartos lehmannii, which the garden attributes to the lack of winter coolness (CYCADS bulletin, 2022)
  • Colour: growers distinguish green and highly prized blue plants. Dyer described young leaves as somewhat glaucous and the cones as more or less glaucous, and PlantZAfrica records a rare blue form in certain localities

Container cultivation

Young plants grow well in pots of very sandy, free-draining compost. French growers advise wintering young plants dry, in a bright, frost-free room. A grower in Belley (Ain) and Montpellier (Hérault), France, reports on the Fous de palmiers forum in 2012 an “impressive success” with unrooted suckers of the blue form, treated with fungicide and grown in a conservatory.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

United Kingdom: Kew and Glasgow Botanic Gardens declare the species to BGCI PlantSearch; in Britain such plants are grown under glass.

United States: Montgomery Botanical Center, in Coral Gables, Florida, lists it in its online catalogue (accession 20200147, from 2020 according to the number), and Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden lists it in its Garden Explorer. The Huntington, Lotusland, San Diego Botanic Garden, San Diego Zoo, the UC Berkeley Botanical Garden and some fourteen other American gardens declare it to BGCI, which shows that a garden has listed the species, not that the plant is alive today.

Australia: the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney lists it in its Garden Explorer, and the census of the Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria places it in an outdoor bed at Melbourne Gardens. The Adelaide Botanic Garden and one other Australian garden declare it to BGCI.

Propagation

Encephalartos arenarius is propagated from seed and from suckers, which it produces freely. Suckers root well, as the conservatory grower quoted above found. The coral-red seeds, about 5 cm long, must be sown fresh; seedlings have leaflets three-lobed at the tip, unlike those of adults. For hand-pollination, PlantZAfrica describes the Kirstenbosch method also used for other Encephalartos:

  • harvest the male cone when a light tap sheds pollen, and store the pollen at −15 °C (5 °F)
  • check the female cone scales: they stay open for between three days and two weeks depending on the plant
  • pollinate with pollen suspended in distilled water, applied with a syringe
  • leave harvested seed to mature for a year, then sow at the beginning of summer in river sand on a heated bench at 24 to 28 °C (75 to 82 °F); germination should start about three weeks after sowing, though some seeds take longer

Like all Appendix I cycads, seeds and suckers can only move internationally with CITES documents; no collecting from the wild has ever been authorised for this species.

Pests and diseases

No disease specific to Encephalartos arenarius is documented. PlantZAfrica notes that leaves and cones are prone to scale insects and mealybugs, that a gall midge attacks young leaves, and that cycad seeds in general are commonly attacked by the weevil Antliarhinus zamiae. In cultivation the main risk is root rot if the compost holds water, all the more so as the species comes from very fast-draining sand.

Cold hardiness

Encephalartos arenarius comes from a coastal region, and no reliable growing report establishes its cold tolerance. We know only that it grows well in very mild climates. No USDA zone is published for it by a botanical source.

Mediterranean and European experience

In France, the only plant followed in the open grows in southern Corsica. A grower there reports on the Fous de palmiers forum in August 2013 that it is the species that grows best in his garden: a main stem more than 30 cm across and 30 cm high, with several suckers; the plant produced its first cone, a male, in summer 2014. Its cold tolerance has never been tested there: the grower says the hardest frost in seventeen years was −0.5 °C (31 °F) for a quarter of an hour, and he does not say whether the plant is in the ground or in a large tub.

In Italy, a grower in Salento (Nardò, Lecce) reports on the Tropicamente forum in September 2010 that his plant, recently planted out, had already come through “substantial frosts without damage”, without giving temperatures; in December 2008 he had written that larger plants lose all their leaves after a frost but have a good chance of recovering the following season. A grower in Ravenna judged in December 2012 that the species is “not particularly hardy”. No observation of this species has been published by the Jardin zoologique tropical in the Var. In Naples, the botanic garden grew five plants of South African origin in 1993–1994 (Gaudio et al., 1997), and a 2019 account of its glasshouses still illustrates the species.

For gardeners in comparable climates, such as coastal California or southern Australia, these reports point to a plant for frost-free or almost frost-free sites, with very sharp drainage.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No dated, quantified cold report is documented from English-speaking countries. At the other extreme, the constant tropical heat of Nong Nooch does not kill it but limits coning. As for all Encephalartos, a young plant with a buried caudex benefits from the thermal inertia of the soil; an adult with stems above ground has no such protection. In Britain it can only be grown under glass; the outdoor plant at Melbourne and the gardens of southern Florida and coastal California give no published cold data.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data are published for Encephalartos arenarius itself, but the risk is documented in the genus: the toxicity of Encephalartos horridus was demonstrated as early as 1944 by Van der Walt, and Encephalartos, like other cycads, contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; the seeds are the most dangerous part. PlantZAfrica reminds growers that cycad seeds are toxic to humans and should be handled with gloves. The ASPCA reports vomiting, diarrhoea, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses that have eaten cycads.

Treat the plant as poisonous: wear gloves to clean seeds, pick up any that fall, and keep plants and seeds away from children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use specific to Encephalartos arenarius is documented. SANBI points out that all South African cycads are exposed to harvesting for traditional medicine and ritual use, since harvesters do not distinguish between species, but no data concern this one.

Frequently asked questions

Why is Encephalartos arenarius called “of the sands”?

Because it grows on old, stabilised sand dunes near Alexandria, in the Eastern Cape. The base of its stems is often buried in sand and dead leaves. That loose sand is also what makes it so easy for poachers to dig out.

How does it differ from Encephalartos latifrons?

In habit and in the cones. Encephalartos latifrons forms stout trunks of two metres or more and large cones with papillose scales; Encephalartos arenarius has shorter, reclining stems, hairless leaflets and smaller cones with smooth scales. A single leaflet is not always enough to tell them apart.

Is the blue form a separate species?

No. Bluish plants are a colour form prized by collectors and recorded by PlantZAfrica in certain localities, not a recognised taxon. Some “blue arenarius” on sale are said to be hybrids with Encephalartos horridus: ask for documented provenance.

Is Encephalartos arenarius frost-hardy?

Probably not very. It comes from a coastal region, no reliable trial has been published, and an Italian grower reports that larger plants lose their leaves after frost. Grow it in a pot and keep it dry and frost-free in winter, except in places where it hardly ever freezes.

Is it fast-growing?

Fairly fast for an Encephalartos, according to growers. Forum members describe it as one of the most regular species, and a grower in Corsica considers it the best grower in his garden. In the wild, by contrast, growth is slow: 30 to 40 years before the first cones.

Is it endangered?

Yes. The IUCN lists it as Endangered, with 850 to 1,500 mature individuals and a decline of more than half in sixty years. It is in CITES Appendix I and in Annex A in the European Union.
