Macrozamia diplomera is a cycad endemic to north-western New South Wales, in eastern Australia, where it grows in dry forest on the poor sands of the Coonabarabran district and the Pilliga, between the Warrumbungle Range and the Mooki River. This species of the genus Macrozamia forms a dense tuft of erect, pale green leaves from an underground trunk and is recognised by its leaflets, most of which fork into two near the base. Described in 1866 as a variety, Encephalartos spiralis var. diplomera, it was raised to species rank only in 1959. It is also held responsible for one of the worst cases of sheep poisoning by a cycad in Australia, near Coonabarabran in 1929.

Key facts about Macrozamia diplomera

  • Accepted name: Macrozamia diplomera (F.Muell.) L.A.S.Johnson (1959), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) and the Australian Plant Census
  • Former names and synonyms: Encephalartos spiralis var. diplomera F.Muell. (1866, basionym); Macrozamia tridentata f. diplomera (F.Muell.) J.Schust. (1932)
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: no English common name is attested
  • Natural range: Australia, north-western New South Wales (southern North-western Slopes, from the eastern Warrumbungle Range to the Mooki River; Pilliga, Coonabarabran, Baradine)
  • IUCN status: Least Concern (LC), assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated September 2020 and published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix II, like the whole genus; Annex B in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: −7 °C (19.4 °F) without damage for a young plant in an unheated greenhouse in southern France (date of the frost not given); 76 frost days a year on average at Coonabarabran (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, open dry sclerophyll forest on deep, poor sands; in cultivation, very free-draining soil
  • Habit and adult size: underground trunk 20 to 40 cm across; 12 to 50 leaves 60 to 120 cm long
  • In the UK, US and Australia: three accessions of 2023 at Montgomery (Florida); one wild-origin plant at Melbourne, planted in 2013; listed at the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney; declared by Kew
  • Toxicity: highly toxic; held responsible for the death of about 2,200 sheep near Coonabarabran in 1929 (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Macrozamia diplomera

It is the only Macrozamia that combines a full crown of flat, straight, erect leaves, lower leaflets reduced to spines and middle leaflets that are often forked. The Flora of Australia (Hill, 1998) characterises it by its flat, stiff, dull pale green leaves and its stiff, sharp leaflets, often divided into two. Its measurements are as follows:

  • Trunk: underground, 20 to 40 cm across;
  • Leaves: 12 to 50 in the crown, 60 to 120 cm long, flat (not keeled); petiole 10 to 20 cm; axis not twisted;
  • Leaflets: 70 to 120 per leaf, undivided or divided into two, 13 to 21 cm long and 5 to 8 mm wide (segments 2 to 5 mm), paler beneath, with a sharp tip; several of the lowest leaflets progressively reduced to spines;
  • Male cones: spindle-shaped, 20 to 29 cm long and 6 to 7 cm wide;
  • Female cones: ovoid, 15 to 18 cm long and 11 to 12 cm wide;
  • Seeds: ovoid, up to 32 mm long and 20 to 24 mm wide, with red flesh according to the Flora.

Lawrence Johnson's Latin description of 1959 adds that the leaflets carry stomata on both surfaces, and that the fork of a divided leaflet usually lies near its base, but towards the middle in the leaflets near the leaf tip. At that time the cones were not known with certainty.

Craig Thompson and Paul Kennedy, who followed the species for about ten years and wrote the account published by the Palm and Cycad Societies of Australia (PACSOA), describe a set of characters useful in the field:

  • the leaves, erect at first, pale green and dulling with age, usually number 10 to 20 per plant, sometimes 5, sometimes more than 30;
  • the leaflets point forwards at about 40° to the axis and lie in one plane;
  • the leaflets divide very close to the axis, usually within 1 cm; they normally give two segments, sometimes three or four, very rarely five; towards the leaf tip, the last few leaflets stay entire;
  • on the same plant, some leaves have mostly divided leaflets (the commonest case), others mostly entire leaflets, and others a mixture of both;
  • the callus at the leaflet base is pale yellow, sometimes orange or reddish;
  • the seed flesh is red or yellow.

David Jones, in Cycads of the World (quoted by the PACSOA), compares the crown of obliquely erect leaves to a shuttlecock.

Hybrids

According to the PACSOA, Macrozamia diplomera appears to hybridise naturally with Macrozamia polymorpha where the two grow close together. The authors suggest that emus, attracted by the coloured flesh of the seeds, could carry seeds from one population to another; they found cycad seeds stripped of their flesh in droppings, probably of emus, in the middle of a stand. This hypothesis has not been tested in a published study. Johnson (1959) had also mentioned an apparent hybrid with Macrozamia heteromera, once grown at the Sydney Botanic Gardens from seed collected in the Coonabarabran district. No garden hybrid has been reported since.

Possible confusion

The most frequent confusion is with Macrozamia reducta, very similar in size and appearance, which grows further south-east, around Newcastle and Cessnock. According to the PACSOA, a stand of Macrozamia diplomera first suggests Macrozamia reducta, but its foliage is much paler, and a look at the divided leaflets settles the question, since those of Macrozamia reducta are always entire. Plants with mostly entire leaflets remain the hardest to identify.

Other New South Wales species have divided leaflets: Macrozamia heteromera, Macrozamia glaucophylla, Macrozamia stenomera and Macrozamia polymorpha. The PACSOA separates Macrozamia diplomera by its size, its number of leaves, its leaves that are straight and erect at first, and its leaflets spread in a single plane. Johnson (1959) noted that most collectors and botanists before him had failed to distinguish it from Macrozamia heteromera, whose range partly coincides with its own and which also has divided leaflets with stomata on both surfaces, but which belongs to section Parazamia.

Taxonomy

Macrozamia diplomera (F.Muell.) L.A.S.Johnson belongs to the family Zamiaceae. Ferdinand von Mueller first described it in 1866, in his Fragmenta Phytographiae Australiae (vol. 5, p. 172), as a variety of a broader species, Encephalartos spiralis var. diplomera. At that time, several Australian Macrozamia were placed in the African genus Encephalartos. In 1932, J. Schuster placed it in Macrozamia tridentata, as forma diplomera.

In 1959, in his revision of the Australian Zamiaceae (Proceedings of the Linnean Society of New South Wales, ser. 2, 84: 98–99), Johnson raised it to species rank under its present name and gave it a new Latin description, Mueller's being very brief. Mueller's material, collected before 1866 by W. Carron, Charles Moore's collector, on the Castlereagh River in the Warrumbungle Range, is a mixed sheet in the National Herbarium of Victoria (MEL): two pieces of leaf belong to this species and one to Macrozamia heteromera. Johnson chose the first two as the lectotype, noting that Mueller's description and epithet fit them better. He separated the species from Macrozamia communis, whose smaller inland forms it otherwise resembles, by its divided leaflets and by its leaves bearing stomata on both surfaces. His revision recognised 14 species of Macrozamia and served as the basis for later floras (Jones, 1991). The name Encephalartos spiralis var. diplomera still appears in old books.

Johnson placed it in section Macrozamia, and the PACSOA notes that it is the only Macrozamia with divided leaflets in that section, the other New South Wales species with divided leaflets belonging to section Parazamia. The World List of Cycads does not use sections. The same account adds that its seedlings, small and with their leaves turned to one side, look more like those of section Parazamia.

Etymology and history

The epithet comes from the Greek diplo-, “double”, and -meros, “part”, in reference to the leaflets divided in two (Haynes, 2022, as given by The World List of Cycads); Johnson translated Mueller's epithet as “two-parted”. The genus name joins the Greek makros, “large”, to the genus name Zamia.

Johnson himself had seen the species in the field only once, in passing. Among the reference specimens of the Flora of Australia are collections by Johnson near Bugaldie (1961) and by A.N. Rodd and A. Blombery near Premer.

The best-known episode in its history is the sheep poisoning of 1929 near Coonabarabran (see Toxicity and precautions). The PACSOA account is a revised version of an article by one of its authors published in March 1992 in issue 55 of Principes minor.

Common names

No common name is attested for this species. In Australia, “burrawang” or “zamia” are used generally for Macrozamia, without being specific to Macrozamia diplomera.

In the wild

Macrozamia diplomera lives in a dry, frosty region of inland New South Wales. According to the Flora of Australia, it is scattered in dry sclerophyll forest on poor siliceous soils, in the south of the North-western Slopes, from the eastern Warrumbungle Range to the Mooki River, as Johnson already wrote in 1959. PlantNET places it mainly between Bugaldie and Premer, on sandy or stony soils. The PACSOA describes it in the Pilliga, around Coonabarabran, north towards Baradine and east towards Gunnedah.

It grows in deep sand, in flat, dry country, most often in extensive stands of scattered plants, sometimes in dense colonies, under a eucalypt canopy with, in places, Callitris (Australian cypress pines). Some plants develop beneath the trunk a taproot 1.2 to 1.5 m long, thin as a whipcord, which goes straight down into the sand, perhaps to reach moisture in the deeper layers (PACSOA). The trunk is usually unbranched; the authors did however see a plant damaged by a road grader, on a dirt-road bank, that carried three heads and two underground suckers.

At Coonabarabran, at 509 m, rainfall averages 735 mm a year over 80 days, a third of it in summer and the rest spread fairly evenly over the other seasons. The temperature falls at least once a week to −3.6 °C (25.5 °F) in July and rises to 35.9 °C (96.6 °F) in January; there are 76 frost days a year on average (PACSOA, from Bureau of Meteorology data).

Coning is very irregular: the PACSOA authors saw only one large coning event in ten years of observation. Cones are usually solitary, but plants with several male or female cones are not unusual.

Conservation status and regulation

Macrozamia diplomera is not threatened: the IUCN Red List classes it as Least Concern (LC), in the assessment by J.D. Bösenberg dated September 2020 and published in 2022 (The World List of Cycads, GBIF and Crossref, checked on 26 October 2026). The Atlas of Living Australia shows no listing for the species under national or New South Wales threatened-species legislation.

Like all species of the genus, it is listed in CITES Appendix II and, in the European Union, in Annex B of the wildlife trade regulations (Species+, checked on 26 October 2026). International trade in plants and seeds requires permits. In 2014, a grower on the French Fous de palmiers forum took the view that the small Macrozamia then sold in Europe often came from seed collected illegally in Australia; this opinion is unverified, but it is a reminder to buy cultivated plants with their documents. Check the regulations in force before any purchase or import.

Growing Macrozamia diplomera

Macrozamia diplomera is grown like the other small Macrozamia from dry climates: very free-draining soil, sun or bright light shade, moderate watering. Sources on its cultivation are scarce and come mostly from amateurs.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

  • Soil: in the wild, deep, poor sands; in cultivation, a very free-draining sandy or gritty mix is essential.
  • Light: the plant lives under a light canopy of eucalypts and Callitris, so a bright position suits it. No reliable source states how well it tolerates full sun outside Australia.
  • Water: about 735 mm of rain a year in its range, with a slightly wetter summer; water regularly during growth and let the soil dry between waterings. Avoid cold, waterlogged soil in winter.
  • Growth: extremely slow, according to the PACSOA. A grower in Roussillon, in the far south of France, reported in 2014 that a seed-raised plant grown for about 18 years had a trunk only 3.5 to 4 cm across; another forum member was surprised, noting that such a plant was still far from reproductive size.

No observation of the Jardin zoologique tropical in southern France has been published for this species; the garden's experience with Macrozamia communis is given in that species' profile.

Container cultivation

The long taproot seen in some wild plants argues for a deep pot, with a very free-draining mix. The plant in Roussillon mentioned above, grown in an unheated greenhouse, is the only documented case of protected cultivation.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

  • United Kingdom: the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, declares the species to BGCI; no other British garden lists it.
  • United States: the Montgomery Botanical Center, at Coral Gables in Florida, lists three accessions in its online catalogue, all from 2023 (20230182, 20230183, 20230189); the Huntington, Lotusland and the Los Angeles County Arboretum declare it to BGCI.
  • Australia: the census of Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria, downloaded on 19 October 2026, records one plant of wild origin in the Drive Bed at Melbourne Gardens, planted on 16 December 2013; the Royal Botanic Garden Sydney lists it in its Garden Explorer (updated on 1 October 2026); the Australian Botanic Garden Mount Annan, the Australian National Botanic Gardens and Adelaide declare it to BGCI.

Fifteen institutions in all declare it to BGCI. A BGCI listing records a declaration by the garden, not necessarily a living plant on display today, and does not say whether the plant grows outdoors or under glass. No English-language grower's report has been found.

Propagation

Propagation is by seed. The plants being male or female, both sexes and, in cultivation, hand pollination are needed to obtain fertile seed. Seedlings are small and grow very slowly (PACSOA). Offsets are exceptional: the only ones reported formed on a plant injured by machinery. No germination data specific to the species has been published.

Pests and diseases

No problem specific to the species is documented in reliable sources. In Macrozamia grown in Europe, growers' forums mainly mention scale insects and rot in cold, wet soil (see the genus page).

Cold hardiness

Only one cultivation report exists: a young plant came through −7 °C (19.4 °F) without any damage in an unheated greenhouse in southern France. In the wild, the species experiences about 76 days of frost a year.

Mediterranean and European experience

A grower in Roussillon (Pyrénées-Orientales, France) reports on the Fous de palmiers forum, in March 2014, that his seed-raised Macrozamia diplomera, grown for about 18 years, survived −7 °C (19 °F) without any damage in an unheated greenhouse, whereas at the same temperature his Macrozamia communis planted outdoors lost half its foliage. He gives neither the date of this frost nor whether the plant was in a pot or in the ground inside the greenhouse. He added that the Australian data needed confirmation, temperature swings being smaller in France than in Australia.

This report concerns a sheltered plant: the greenhouse protects it from wind, rain and radiative cooling at night, and the temperature recorded does not tell what a plant in the open would have endured. No planting in the open ground is documented in Europe.

In the same thread, another grower reported in 2014 the opinion of an Australian nurseryman specialising in cycads, who ranked Macrozamia diplomera among the hardiest species of the genus, those of the Coonabarabran and Pilliga area, without giving temperatures.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

No dated cultivation report has been found from Britain, North America or Australia, and no source gives a USDA zone for the species. The climate data for Coonabarabran (frequent frosts, a minimum of about −3.6 °C (25.5 °F) reached every week in July) show only that the species withstands repeated frosts in the wild, on an underground trunk protected by the soil.

Toxicity and precautions

Macrozamia diplomera is highly toxic, particularly its seeds and leaves. David Jones, in Cycads of the World (second edition, quoted by the PACSOA), attributes to this species, and not to Macrozamia heteromera as other writers had suggested, a poisoning near Coonabarabran in 1929 in which about 2,200 sheep died, reportedly after eating seeds from disintegrating cones. According to the same source, some observers now believe the sheep had instead browsed the leaves during a drought. The source does not cite the original veterinary report.

Healy (1967), a New South Wales veterinary research officer, cites for his part a case described by Seddon, Belschner and King (1931): 30% of a flock of 6,000 merino ewes died after eating the ripe seeds of a Macrozamia then called Macrozamia spiralis, with no locality given in his text. Nothing in these sources shows whether this is the same episode.

Macrozamia contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived, a toxin of the liver; the young leaves cause irreversible paralysis of the hindquarters in cattle (McKenzie, 2006; see the genus page). No chemical analysis specific to this species has been published. Wear gloves to handle seeds, keep them out of reach of children, dogs and livestock, and contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay in case of ingestion.

Traditional uses

No use specific to Macrozamia diplomera is documented. Brit Asmussen's 2011 review of Aboriginal consumption of Macrozamia seeds does not mention this species (see the genus page).

Frequently asked questions

Why are its leaflets forked?

It is a character of the species, which gave it its name (“in two parts”). Most leaflets divide into two near the axis, sometimes into three or four, but the same plant can carry leaves with entire leaflets.

Is this the species that poisoned sheep at Coonabarabran?

Yes, according to David Jones, quoted by the PACSOA: about 2,200 sheep died near Coonabarabran in 1929. Some writers had blamed Macrozamia heteromera, and the respective roles of seeds and leaves are still debated.

Is Macrozamia diplomera cold-hardy?

Fairly, on the little evidence available. A young plant came through −7 °C (19.4 °F) without damage in an unheated greenhouse in southern France, and the species endures 76 frost days a year in the wild. No trial in the open ground has been published.

How can it be told from Macrozamia reducta?

By its leaflets and colour. Macrozamia diplomera usually has divided leaflets and pale green foliage, whereas Macrozamia reducta, dark green, always has entire leaflets. Their ranges are separate.

Does it grow fast?

No, very slowly. A plant grown in France for about 18 years had a trunk only 3.5 to 4 cm across in 2014.

Does it need sandy soil?

Preferably. The species lives on deep, poor sands, where some plants develop a taproot more than a metre long. A very free-draining mix and a deep pot are advisable.

Is it protected?

Yes, by CITES (Appendix II) and, in the European Union, by Annex B of the wildlife trade regulations, like all Macrozamia. It is not threatened according to the IUCN (LC, 2022).
