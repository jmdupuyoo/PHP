Encephalartos barteri is the only cycad of West Africa: it grows in Benin, Ghana, Nigeria and Togo, on rocky hills in wooded savanna, far from every other species of the genus Encephalartos. It is a small plant with a short trunk, often partly underground, green leaves 1 to 1.65 m long and narrow leaflets. Described in 1868 from a plant collected ten years earlier in Nigeria by Charles Barter, it has two subspecies, one of them confined to the Jos Plateau, where its trunks reach 2.6 m. The IUCN lists it as Vulnerable, threatened by bush fires, habitat degradation and the collecting of seeds and young plants.

Key facts about Encephalartos barteri

  • Accepted name: Encephalartos barteri Carruth. ex Miq. (1868), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15) with two subspecies, subsp. barteri and subsp. allochrous L.E.Newton (1978); POWO could not be consulted directly
  • Former names and synonyms: Encephalartos heteropterus Miq. ex J.Schust., a name published only in synonymy (1932) and therefore not valid
  • Family: Zamiaceae, order Cycadales
  • Common names: none attested in English; Species+ gives no common name
  • Natural range: Benin (Atakora, Borgou, Collines, Donga, Zou), Ghana, Nigeria (Kwara, Plateau), Togo
  • IUCN status: Vulnerable (VU), criteria A2cd+4cd, assessment dated March 2020 and published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I (whole genus); Annex A in the European Union
  • Documented hardiness: none; a tropical species described as very cold-sensitive by a Californian nursery (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: in the wild, rocky granite or sandstone slopes in wooded savanna; filtered light or part shade and well-drained soil recommended in cultivation
  • Habit and adult size: trunk 20 to 25 cm thick, rarely more than about 30 cm tall in West Africa according to the literature cited by Newton (1978), but up to 2.6 m long in subsp. allochrous; leaves 94 to 165 cm (Melville, 1957)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: Kew, Bristol and Treborth (BGCI); Montgomery Botanical Center, Fairchild, San Diego Zoo and three other American gardens; Cairns Botanic Gardens (BGCI)
  • Toxicity: no data specific to the species; treat it as poisonous like all cycads (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Encephalartos barteri

Look for green, narrow, elongated leaves with fine, flexible leaflets, and for its range: it is the only Encephalartos in West Africa. Ronald Melville's revision (1957), prepared for the Flora of Tropical East Africa, describes it as follows:

  • Trunk: 20 to 25 cm in diameter, covered with leaf bases and grey-felted scale leaves; the printed height, “to 30 m”, is an obvious misprint
  • Leaves: linear-oblong, 94 to 165 cm long and 15 to 26 cm wide, rounded at the tip; rachis deeply grooved between the leaflets; swollen base of the petiole covered in shaggy grey felt
  • Leaflets: middle leaflets linear-lanceolate, 10 to 16 cm long and 10 to 15 mm wide, flexible, ending in a sharp point, with 0 to 6 spiny teeth and 20 to 24 parallel veins; the lower leaflets pass into simple spines
  • Male cones: almost cylindrical, 8 to 23 cm long and 3 to 5 cm wide, on a stalk 8 to 20 cm long
  • Female cones: oblong-ellipsoid, 12 to 35 cm long and 8 to 15 cm wide, dark olive green, on a stalk 5 to 12 cm long
  • Seeds: ovoid to almost round, more or less three-angled, 21 to 30 mm long and 18 to 23 mm wide, scarlet

Newton (1978) adds that, in the literature he cites (Prain, 1917; Keay, 1954; Hall and Jeník, 1967), the trunk of West African plants rarely exceeds about 30 cm, and that strong contractile roots pull it down so that much of it is often below ground. The new leaves of the typical subspecies emerge light green and darken as they mature.

Subspecies allochrous, described by Newton (1978) from the Jos Plateau, differs in its much longer trunks, up to 2.6 m, which become decumbent with age and branch at the base; in its young leaves, which emerge brown, turn olive green and finally dark green (hence the name); in its larger middle leaflets, up to 26 cm long and 2.2 cm wide; and in its habitat above 1,220 m, whereas known localities of the typical subspecies lie below 400 m. Its cones cannot be told from those of subsp. barteri.

Hybrids

No natural hybrid is known: no other cycad grows within the range of Encephalartos barteri. No garden hybrid has been reported.

Possible confusion

In the wild, the commonest confusion is an unexpected one: according to a conservation project carried out in Benin in 2022, local people often mistake Encephalartos barteri for the oil palm (Elaeis guineensis), which it resembles in habit, although the two plants are unrelated. A cycad is recognised by its cones and by its stiff leaflets set along a thick rachis.

Among cycads, the confusion is mainly historical. In 1957 Melville still included plants from north-western Uganda and neighbouring Sudan, where they grow near Encephalartos septentrionalis, and wondered whether intermediates existed. Newton (1978) noted that Heenan (1977), after an intensive search in north-eastern Uganda, doubted that Encephalartos barteri grows there at all. The World List of Cycads now restricts Encephalartos barteri to West Africa and places Encephalartos septentrionalis in the Central African Republic, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, South Sudan and Uganda. The latter has much broader leaflets (17 to 28 mm, with 26 to 43 veins according to Melville).

Taxonomy

Encephalartos barteri was published in 1868 by the botanist F. A. W. Miquel in the Archives néerlandaises des sciences exactes et naturelles (3: 243), using a name proposed by William Carruthers, hence the author citation “Carruth. ex Miq.”. The type is the collection Barter 1692, made in 1858 at Jeba, in Nigeria; an isotype is kept at Kew, according to Melville (1957). The name Encephalartos heteropterus Miq. ex J.Schust. was published only in synonymy, in 1932, in Schuster's monograph for the Pflanzenreich: it is not valid.

Two subspecies are accepted. In 1978 Leonard E. Newton, then at the University of Science and Technology in Kumasi, Ghana, described subsp. allochrous in the Botanical Journal of the Linnean Society (77(2): 125–129), from the collection Newton 1209, made on 6 April 1972 in a steep-sided valley near the village of Gilli, about 10 km north-north-west of Tokkos, in the Plateau region of Nigeria, at 1,370 m (holotype at Kew). He considered the Jos Plateau plants conspecific with Encephalartos barteri, since their cones are identical and they key out to it, but recognised them as a subspecies because of their vegetative features and altitudinal isolation. The same publication automatically created the name of the type subspecies, subsp. barteri. Melville had already noted in 1957 plants differing from the type in the scales of the female cones, one collected west of Abene in Ghana and others grown at Vom, in Nigeria, from Bokkos on the plateau.

According to The World List of Cycads, subsp. barteri occurs in Benin, Ghana, Nigeria (Kwara State) and Togo, and subsp. allochrous is confined to Plateau State in Nigeria. The checklist gives 1851 for Miquel's article in its references but 1868 on the species page and in IPNI; 1868 is used here.

Etymology and history

The species honours Charles Barter (1821–1859), who collected the type in northern Nigeria (Haynes, 2022, cited by The World List of Cycads). The collection dates from 1858, a year before his death. The subspecies epithet allochrous comes from the Greek allochroos, “changing colour”, for the young leaves, which Newton saw turn from brown to olive green and then dark green, both on plants in the wild and on plants brought into cultivation.

Encephalartos barteri has also served fundamental research: in 1959 G. K. Berrie published in Nature a study of mitosis in its microspores. Its populations in Benin and Ghana have been the subject of conservation work since the late 2000s.

Common names

No English common name is attested by a reference source, and Species+ gives none. In Benin, the 2022 conservation project notes only that local people often take it for an oil palm.

In the wild

Encephalartos barteri grows in the wooded savanna belt that stretches from Ghana to Nigeria. In Ghana it occurs sporadically on rocky slopes; the country's largest population is around Akosombo, and two other sites, Labolabo and Abotriansa, were studied by Alex Asase's research project (2013–2016). In Benin, seven populations have been reported, spread across the country's three climatic zones, from the Guinean zone in the south to the Sudanian zone in the north; four of them (Gangamou, Doh, Igbomakoro and Wannou) lie outside any protected area.

According to the 2010 IUCN assessment, as quoted by the nursery Jungle Music, the species occupies sandstone and granite outcrops in wooded savanna or at the forest edge, and grassland dotted with shrubs. Newton (1978) describes all the populations he knew as growing on rocky slopes in woodland savanna of scattered trees with a grassy ground layer, on granite or sandstone. On the Jos Plateau he found subsp. allochrous on granite at 1,220 to 1,370 m, in plentiful populations with many seedlings and young plants; the steep slopes, useless for farming, were then undisturbed. In 2008 Ekué and colleagues published a population genetics study of the typical subspecies in Benin, with notes on leaflet shape.

Conservation status and regulation

Encephalartos barteri is listed as Vulnerable (VU) on the IUCN Red List under criteria A2cd+4cd: a past and continuing decline of at least 30% of the population, inferred from habitat loss and exploitation. The assessment, by J. D. Bösenberg, is dated March 2020 and was published in 2022, according to The World List of Cycads. Each of the two subspecies was also assessed separately in 2022, but their categories could not be checked: The World List of Cycads shows them as “not evaluated”, and the Red List itself could not be consulted directly.

The documented threats differ between countries. In Ghana, Alex Asase's project, funded by the Rufford Foundation, identifies bush fires as the main threat, followed by firewood gathering and livestock. In Benin, Gabriel Michel Monteiro's project (2022) reports that the continuing collection of seeds and young plants is depleting the populations, and that no practical conservation action had yet been taken; it aims to involve villagers in monitoring the populations.

Like the whole genus, Encephalartos barteri has been listed in CITES Appendix I since 4 February 1977, without annotation, so plants, seeds and pollen are all covered. Commercial international trade in wild-collected plants is prohibited; only artificially propagated plants can be exported, from registered nurseries and with the required permits. In the European Union the species is in Annex A of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1383. For artificially propagated plants imported from South Africa, the EU issued a positive opinion in 2011 up to a caudex diameter of 7 cm and, since 7 February 2013, a negative opinion above that size. These details come from Species+, the official CITES and EU database, consulted in October 2026; check the current rules before any purchase or import, and ask for proof that the plant was propagated in cultivation.

Growing Encephalartos barteri

Encephalartos barteri is a tropical plant, rare in cultivation and suited only to hot climates or a warm glasshouse. The only published growing advice comes from a Californian nursery and is not confirmed by any other source.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

The San Diego nursery Jungle Music finds it difficult to grow in its area because of its cold sensitivity, and recommends it only for exceptionally warm, frost-free areas. It advises filtered light or shade in cooler or humid climates and a well-drained loam, and notes a fair tolerance of water, though not of waterlogged soil. It describes the female cones as green and the male cones as green with a bluish cast. No growing report is documented on growers' forums.

Container cultivation

In temperate climates the plant can only be grown under glass: a deep pot of free-draining compost in a warm glasshouse or heated conservatory, kept drier in winter. This advice follows from the climate of its natural range, not from published trials. Newton (1978) reports that small plants of subsp. allochrous collected for study grew in cultivation, where all of them showed the brown young leaves typical of the subspecies.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

United Kingdom: the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, the University of Bristol Botanic Garden and Treborth Botanic Garden declare the species to BGCI PlantSearch; in Britain such a tropical plant is grown under glass.

United States: Montgomery Botanical Center, in Coral Gables, Florida, lists it in its online catalogue (accession 20050399, from 2005 according to the number), and Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden lists it in its Garden Explorer. San Diego Zoo and three other American gardens declare it to BGCI, which shows that a garden has listed the species, not that the plant is alive today.

Australia: Cairns Botanic Gardens, in tropical Queensland, declares it to BGCI.

Propagation

Encephalartos barteri is propagated from fresh, fertile seed. Since the collection of seeds and young plants in the wild is one of the threats identified in Benin, buy only seeds or plants from cultivation, with their CITES documents. Branching is not a conspicuous feature of the species, though Newton notes that Hall and Jeník (1967) found a plant branched below ground; no data have been published on germination or offset production in cultivation.

Pests and diseases

No pest specific to Encephalartos barteri is documented. A parasitic fungus, however, has been described on the species: a new species of Pseudocercospora, published in 2019 in the Asian Journal of Mycology from material collected in Benin. In cultivation the usual risks are root rot in cold, wet soil, and scale insects.

Cold hardiness

Encephalartos barteri is not hardy: no cold tolerance data have been published, and its range, between the Guinean and Sudanian climatic zones, is tropical. No USDA zone is published for it by a botanical source.

Mediterranean and European experience

No growing report is documented in France, Italy or elsewhere in Europe, either in the ground or in a pot, and the Jardin zoologique tropical has published no observation on this species. Even in the mildest Mediterranean gardens it can only be grown under cover, in a glasshouse or a conservatory heated in winter.

United Kingdom, United States and Australia

The only indication comes from the nursery Jungle Music, which describes it as “very cold sensitive” in San Diego, without giving a temperature or a particular episode. Outdoor cultivation is conceivable only in tropical or near-tropical climates, such as southern Florida or tropical Queensland, where Montgomery, Fairchild and Cairns hold it; none of these gardens publishes cold data for it.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity data are published for Encephalartos barteri itself, but the risk is documented in the genus: the toxicity of Encephalartos horridus was demonstrated as early as 1944 by Van der Walt, and Encephalartos, like other cycads, contain toxic glycosides (cycasin, macrozamin), from which methylazoxymethanol is derived; the seeds are the most dangerous part. The ASPCA reports vomiting, diarrhoea, liver damage and deaths in dogs, cats and horses that have eaten cycads.

Treat the plant as poisonous: wear gloves to clean seeds, pick up any that fall, and keep plants and seeds away from children and pets. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet without delay.

Traditional uses

No traditional use of Encephalartos barteri is documented. The Benin conservation project planned an ethnobotanical survey, whose results did not appear in its first report.

Frequently asked questions

Are there cycads in West Africa?

Yes, just one: Encephalartos barteri. It grows in Benin, Ghana, Nigeria and Togo. It is also the only native gymnosperm of Benin, according to the researchers who study it there.

What are its two subspecies?

Subsp. barteri, found from Ghana to Nigeria at low altitude, with a short trunk often partly underground, and subsp. allochrous, described in 1978 and confined to the Jos Plateau in Nigeria, above 1,220 m, with trunks up to 2.6 m and young leaves that emerge brown.

Can Encephalartos barteri be grown outdoors?

Only in tropical or near-tropical climates. It is a tropical species, described as very cold-sensitive, and no outdoor trial in a temperate climate has been published. Elsewhere, grow it in a pot in a warm glasshouse or heated conservatory.

Why is it threatened?

Because of fires, habitat degradation and collecting. In Ghana bush fires are the main threat; in Benin the collection of seeds and young plants is depleting populations that often lie outside protected areas. The IUCN lists it as Vulnerable.

How can I tell it from a palm?

By its cones and leaflets. An Encephalartos bears true male or female cones in the centre of the crown, and its stiff, parallel-veined leaflets ending in a spine do not look like the soft leaflets of the oil palm.

Is it poisonous?

Probably, like all cycads. No study concerns this species, but Encephalartos seeds contain toxins. Keep seeds and plants out of reach of children and pets.
