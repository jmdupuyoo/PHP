Cycas beddomei is a small cycad endemic to the dry hills of the southern Eastern Ghats in Andhra Pradesh, India, where it grows on open, rocky slopes in the Seshachalam and Velikonda hills. Described in 1883 by William Turner Thiselton-Dyer and named after the forester-botanist Richard Henry Beddome, this member of the genus Cycas is the only species in the genus listed in CITES Appendix I, since 1987. It was once treated as a variety of Cycas circinalis (Cycas circinalis var. beddomei), and the IUCN classes it as Endangered (2022).

Key facts about Cycas beddomei

  • Accepted name: Cycas beddomei Dyer (1883), accepted by The World List of Cycads (version 2026.08.15); the World Checklist of Vascular Plants (WCVP), on which POWO is based, also accepts it (consulted through GBIF on 31 October 2026)
  • Former names and synonyms: Cycas circinalis var. beddomei (Dyer) J.Schust. (1932); garden names used before its description: Cycas pluma W.Bull (1877, catalogue name), Cycas boddami and Cycas squamosa (see Etymology and history)
  • Family: Cycadaceae, order Cycadales; section Cycas
  • Common names: Beddome's cycad (English); peritha or per ita, madanakamakshi and kondaeetha in Telugu (see Common names)
  • Natural range: India, Andhra Pradesh: Seshachalam hills (Tirupati–Kadapa hills) and Velikonda hills, in the Chittoor, Kadapa and Nellore districts, between 500 and 1,165 m
  • IUCN status: Endangered (EN B1ab(ii,iii,v)+2ab(ii,iii,v)), assessment by Rao and colleagues published in 2022
  • CITES and other protection: CITES Appendix I since 22 October 1987 (Appendix II before), the only Cycas so listed; Annex A in the European Union; India bans the commercial export of wild-taken specimens
  • Documented hardiness: no data (see Cold hardiness)
  • Light and soil: dry, open hill slopes in woodland or grassland, mostly on upper slopes and black soils
  • Habit and adult size: trunk up to 2 m tall and 12 to 23 cm across, often suckering at the base; leaves about 90 cm long (70 to 120 cm according to other measurements)
  • In the UK, US and Australia: one accession of 2024 at Montgomery Botanical Center (Florida); listed by the Huntington (California) in BGCI PlantSearch; no record in Britain or Australia
  • Toxicity: no analysis specific to the species; treat it as poisonous, like all Cycas (see Toxicity and precautions)

How to recognise Cycas beddomei

It is recognised above all by its very narrow leaflets, only 2 to 4 mm wide, with rolled-under (revolute) margins, which give the crown a fine texture quite unlike that of the other Indian Cycas. Anders Lindström and Ken Hill (2007), in their revision of the genus in India, describe it as follows:

  • Trunk: up to 2 m tall, 12 to 23 cm in diameter, often suckering at the base; bark thick and corky, with persistent cataphylls and leaf bases.
  • Leaves: grey-green, dull, about 90 cm long, flat in section (opposite leaflets inserted at 180° on the rachis), with brown felt shed as the leaf expands; petiole about 15 cm long (20 to 30 % of the leaf), hairless and spiny; basal leaflets not gradually reduced to spines.
  • Median leaflets: simple, strongly two-toned, 100 to 175 mm long and 2 to 4 mm wide, set at 50 to 60° to the rachis, 4 to 6 mm apart; margins revolute; tip acute or aristate, spiny; midrib raised above, flat below.
  • Cataphylls: narrowly triangular, soft, 50 to 70 mm long.
  • Male cones: narrowly ovoid, orange, about 30 cm long and 7.5 cm across; microsporophylls with a prominent apical spine about 30 mm long.
  • Megasporophylls: 15 to 20 cm long, persistently brown-felted, bearing 2 hairless ovules; blade lanceolate, 65 to 75 mm long and 20 to 25 mm wide, regularly toothed with 22 to 32 pungent lateral spines 5 to 9 mm long; apical spine distinct, 11 to 15 mm long.
  • Seeds: flattened-ovoid, 34 to 38 mm long and 30 to 34 mm wide; sarcotesta yellow, with a fibrous layer; no spongy layer beneath the shell.

The field study by Ravi Prasad Rao and colleagues (2015) gives somewhat different figures: trunks up to 1.5 m, leaves 70 to 120 cm long and up to 30 in the crown, 2 to 4 ovules (occasionally 6 to 8) per megasporophyll and seeds up to 5 cm, green turning yellow. Such differences are common between a herbarium-based revision and measurements taken on wild populations.

Hybrids

No natural or garden hybrid involving Cycas beddomei has been documented. The species is geographically isolated from its closest relatives.

Possible confusion

In India, confusion is most likely with other Cycas of the region:

  • Cycas pectinata: in the key of Lindström and Hill (2007), its megasporophyll apex is about as broad as long, with lateral teeth more than 10 mm long, whereas those of Cycas beddomei are short (under 6 mm in the key, 5 to 9 mm in the description); its leaflets are also much broader.
  • Cycas sphaerica and the plants once called Cycas circinalis var. orixensis, from further north in the Eastern Ghats: Raizada and Sahni (1960) included them in Cycas beddomei, but Lindström and Hill regard this material as quite distinct.
  • Cycas circinalis, a species of the Western Ghats in which Schuster placed it as a variety in 1932: it has broad, flat leaflets, without revolute margins.
  • Cycas revoluta, the sago palm of Japan, which Beddome himself first took it for: its ovules and seeds are felted, whereas those of Cycas beddomei are hairless. David de Laubenfels and Frits Adema (1998) also noted a resemblance to Cycas calcicola of northern Australia, which has flatter leaves and shorter leaflets.

Taxonomy

Cycas beddomei is an accepted species, recognised by The World List of Cycads and by every modern revision; the World Checklist of Vascular Plants (WCVP), on which POWO is based, also accepts it (consulted through GBIF on 31 October 2026). Lindström and Hill (2007) note that it is one of the few species of the genus whose name has been understood fairly clearly from the start, with little misapplication and no misunderstanding of the type. In 1932 Julius Schuster reduced it to a variety of Cycas circinalis (Cycas circinalis var. beddomei), a name now treated as a synonym; Lindström and Hill consider that he understood the limits of Cycas circinalis poorly, since he also included material from Java in his variety.

The species belongs to section Cycas, defined by hairless ovules and a megasporophyll blade that is not comb-like, and within it to subsection Cycas, whose seeds lack a spongy layer. With the other southern Indian Cycas, it shares subglobular seeds with a distinctive fibrous layer in the sarcotesta and a tapering microsporophyll tip.

Etymology and history

The epithet honours Colonel Richard Henry Beddome (1830–1911), forestry botanist in India, a director of the Lal Bagh, the government gardens at Bangalore, and author of the Forester's Manual of Botany for Southern India; according to The World List of Cycads, he was the first to discover the species (Haynes 2022).

The plant reached European horticulture before it was described. According to Lindström and Hill (2007), Beddome recorded it in 1869, with some hesitation, as Cycas revoluta. In 1881 Thiselton-Dyer, then Assistant Director at Kew, noted in the Gardeners' Chronicle that a small Indian cycad distinct from Cycas circinalis had been introduced several times under various names: by Beddome himself, who sent plants to Belgian nurserymen (who called it Cycas boddami); by Adolf Haage of Erfurt, who gave Travancore as its origin (Cycas squamosa); and through Colonel Puckle, another director of the Lal Bagh, whose plants the English nurseryman William Bull offered in 1877 as Cycas pluma, “a distinct Indian cycad, from the Presidency of Madras”. Thiselton-Dyer appealed for wild material, and H. H. Yarde, Deputy Conservator of Forests for the Cuddapah division, sent him plants and specimens collected in August 1882. Dyer described the species in 1883 in the Transactions of the Linnean Society of London (Botany 2(5): 85–86, plate 17).

Yarde apparently sent only young plants, which led Thiselton-Dyer to record stems only a few inches high, and later, in 1888, to state that this was their normal size. The myth of an almost stemless species was repeated in the literature for decades (Lindström and Hill 2007).

The type, Yarde's unnumbered collection from the Cuddapah Hills, is kept at Kew. De Laubenfels and Adema (1998) had treated it as a holotype, but this designation was ineffective because Yarde's material came from two separate gatherings; in 2018 Khuraijam and colleagues designated the Kew specimen with barcode K001325145 as the lectotype (The World List of Cycads).

Common names

The English name Beddome's cycad is used by Species+, the CITES database, and by Rao and colleagues (2015, “Beddome's Cycas”). In Telugu, the language of Andhra Pradesh, Rao and colleagues give peritha, madanakamakshi and kondaeetha; Lindström and Hill (2007) record per ita and madhana-kamakshi, which they translate as “lust-eyed one”, presumably a reference to supposed aphrodisiac properties, and konda itha, in an unidentified language.

In the wild

Cycas beddomei grows on dry, open hill slopes, in woodland or grass-dominated forest, between 500 and 1,165 m; it is mostly confined to the upper slopes and to black soils (Rao and colleagues 2015). Common companions include the dwarf palm Phoenix humilis, Terminalia pallida, Syzygium alternifolium, Pimpinella tirupatiensis, Curculigo orchioides and grasses of the genera Cymbopogon and Dechaschistia. Leaf growth is most vigorous during the rains of July and August; Rao and colleagues give the reproductive period as April to August in their description, and cone setting and fruit formation from September to November in their assessment.

Long thought to be confined to the Seshachalam hills, the species was found in 2015 in the Velikonda hills too, about 40 km away, on the border of the Nellore and Kadapa districts. Rao and colleagues counted 3,962 mature plants in 26 transects and extrapolated a total of about 390,000 mature individuals, 97 % of them in the Seshachalam hills, with about three female plants for every male. They put the extent of occurrence at 1,713 km² and the area of occupancy at only 16.52 km², the plants being patchily scattered: in many survey grids, around 80 % of the area held none.

Lindström and Hill (2007) describe a plant well adapted to recovering quickly after fire, but frequent grass fires burn its seeds and seedlings and so block reproduction.

Conservation status and regulation

Cycas beddomei is classed as Endangered (EN B1ab(ii,iii,v)+2ab(ii,iii,v)) on the IUCN Red List, in the assessment by Rao, Naik, Salamma, Babu and Rasingam published in 2022 (category checked through GBIF). The criteria reflect its small range and the continuing decline of its habitat and numbers. Its status has varied: Vulnerable in the Red Data Book of Indian Plants (Nayar and Sastry 1987), Critically Endangered for Jadhav and colleagues (2001) and in the IUCN action plan of 2003, then Endangered after the field surveys of Rao and colleagues (2010, 2015).

The threats are well documented. Forest fires, increasingly frequent according to Rao and colleagues, destroy seeds and seedlings in the Seshachalam hills; in the Velikonda hills, pests are the main problem: the scale insect Saissetia coffeae and caterpillars of the cycad blue butterfly, Chilades pandava, which deform the plants and can kill them. Male cones are harvested for Ayurvedic medicines, often before pollen is shed, which Lindström and Hill (2007) regard as an even more effective block to reproduction; plants are also uprooted for ornament and occasionally felled for their pith. Road works near Tirupati destroyed several large populations, which survive only as scattered individuals in inaccessible rock crevices. Rao and colleagues estimate that the population fell by 5 to 10 % over four years.

Cycas beddomei is the only Cycas in CITES Appendix I, the convention's strictest level, since 22 October 1987; it had been in Appendix II since 4 February 1977, like the rest of the family (Species+). Under the convention, international trade in an Appendix I species needs both an export and an import permit, and the exemption for seeds that applies to the other Cycas (annotation #4) does not apply to it. Lindström and Hill (2007) observed that it is no more endangered than many other species of the genus, and probably less so than some; Rao and colleagues (2015), on the contrary, recommended keeping it in Appendix I because of its restricted range and slow growth. India adds stricter domestic measures: it has banned the export for commercial purposes of wild-taken specimens (Species+), and, according to Lindström and Hill, a forestry act prohibits cultivation of the species in the country. The United Kingdom, the United States and Australia apply CITES through their own legislation; in the European Union the species is in Annex A of Regulation (EC) No 338/97, whose annexes in force are set by Commission Regulation (EU) 2026/1383, applicable since 29 June 2026 (Species+, consulted on 19 October 2026).

This general information does not replace the official texts: before any purchase, import or exchange, check the rules in force with the CITES authority of your country.

Growing Cycas beddomei

In short: this is a collector's and botanic garden plant, rarely seen in cultivation and tightly regulated; no institution has published growing recommendations for it. Its natural habitat suggests a plant for full sun, very free-draining soil and a climate with a long dry season, but these are inferences, not results of trials.

Outdoor and in-ground cultivation

No result of open-ground cultivation outside India has been published, in the Mediterranean, the United States, Australia or Britain. Within India, cultivation is restricted by law according to Lindström and Hill (2007). The species' habit of suckering at the base and its ability to recover after fire, both noted by them, have not been documented in gardens.

Container cultivation

No experience of container growing has been published either. The only practical guidance comes from its habitat: open, sunny slopes, black soils and a monsoon climate with rains in summer. In a cool, wet winter climate, a pot under glass, kept on the dry side in winter, is the prudent choice, by analogy with other tropical Cycas; this is advice by deduction, not drawn from a source on the species.

In cultivation in the UK, US and Australia

In short: the species is almost absent from public collections in these countries. A garden listed in the BGCI PlantSearch database (consulted in October 2026) has declared the species at some point, which does not prove that a living plant is there today.

  • United States: the online catalogue of Montgomery Botanical Center (Coral Gables, Florida) lists one accession of Cycas beddomei, whose number shows that it entered the collection in 2024. The Huntington (San Marino, California) lists the species in BGCI PlantSearch.
  • United Kingdom: no British garden lists the species. Kew holds the type specimen, but the published sources do not show that it grows the plant today.
  • Australia: no Australian garden lists the species.

No report from a grower in the United Kingdom, the United States or Australia has been published on English-language forums.

Propagation

Like all Cycas, Cycas beddomei is dioecious: a male and a female plant are needed to obtain fertile seed. In the wild, female plants outnumber males by about three to one (Rao and colleagues 2015). Lindström and Hill (2007) describe plants that often sucker at the base, which suggests that offsets could be used, but no account of vegetative propagation has been published. No germination data specific to the species is documented. Seeds are covered by CITES Appendix I, unlike those of other Cycas.

Pests and diseases

In the wild, Rao and colleagues (2015) record two pests: the scale insect Saissetia coffeae, persistently present, and the cycad blue butterfly, Chilades pandava, whose caterpillars attack young leaves; together they deform plants, slow their growth and can kill them, especially in the Velikonda hills. No disease specific to the species is documented in cultivation.

Cold hardiness

There is no measured figure for the cold tolerance of Cycas beddomei. Its range lies in the tropical, monsoonal south of India, and nothing allows us to say that it tolerates frost. No USDA zone for the species is given by any reliable published source.

No cultivation result, in the ground or in a pot, has been published from the Mediterranean, Europe, the United States or Australia, and no dated frost episode concerns it. The Jardin zoologique tropical in the south of France, whose observations are used elsewhere on this site, has published none for this species. Without data, grow it frost-free.

Toxicity and precautions

No toxicity analysis specific to Cycas beddomei is documented, but the risk is well documented in the genus: Cycas tissues, and the seeds above all, contain cycasin, which is converted during digestion into methylazoxymethanol, toxic to the liver and nervous system, along with a neurotoxic amino acid, BMAA. The ASPCA lists Cycas revoluta among plants poisonous to dogs, cats and horses. Treat the plant as poisonous: keep seeds and leaves out of reach of children and pets, and wear gloves to handle the seeds. If any part is swallowed, contact a poison centre, a doctor or a vet straight away.

Traditional uses

Local people harvest the seeds to make flour, the Yanadi tribes and rural communities prepare a health tonic from male-cone extracts, and the pith is often taken for use as an abortifacient (Rao and colleagues 2015). Lindström and Hill (2007) add that male cones are used in Ayurvedic medicines, which makes them a tradeable commodity. None of these uses is validated by modern pharmacology, and given the toxins of the genus they must not be copied under any circumstances.

Frequently asked questions

Why is Cycas beddomei the only Cycas in CITES Appendix I?

Because it was moved there on 22 October 1987, when it was thought to be extremely rare. Field surveys have since shown that it is more numerous than believed, but still Endangered; Lindström and Hill (2007) considered it no more endangered than many other species, whereas Rao and colleagues (2015) recommended keeping it in Appendix I.

Where does it grow?

Only in Andhra Pradesh, in southern India: in the Seshachalam hills around Tirupati and Kadapa and, since a 2015 discovery, in the Velikonda hills, between 500 and 1,165 m.

How many plants are left in the wild?

Several hundred thousand, according to the extrapolation by Rao and colleagues (2015): about 390,000 mature plants, 97 % of them in the Seshachalam hills, from 3,962 plants actually counted. Numbers are falling because of fire, pests and harvesting.

Can I buy a Cycas beddomei?

Only with great difficulty and never casually. International trade needs both export and import permits under CITES Appendix I, seeds included, India bans the commercial export of wild-taken specimens, and the species is very rarely offered in cultivation; check with your national CITES authority before any purchase.

Is it the same plant as Cycas revoluta?

No. Beddome first recorded it as Cycas revoluta, but it is a distinct Indian species with hairless ovules and seeds, very narrow leaflets and a range limited to a few hills of Andhra Pradesh.

Can it be grown outdoors in the UK or the cooler parts of the US?

There is no evidence that it can. No cold tolerance has been measured, the species is tropical, and no outdoor result has been published outside India; keep it frost-free under glass.

Is it poisonous?

Yes, it should be treated as such. There is no analysis of this species, but every Cycas studied contains toxins dangerous to people and animals, especially in the seeds.
